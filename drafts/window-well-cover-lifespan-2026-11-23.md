# How Long Do Window Well Covers Last in Nebraska?

Everyone wants a number. Five years? Fifteen? Twenty-five? The honest answer is that a window well cover doesn't have an expiration date, and anybody who gives you a flat figure is guessing. What a cover does have is a set of things that wear it down, and in Nebraska those are sun, hail, heavy snow and a lot of freezing and thawing.

This guide covers how each common cover material ages, what Lincoln weather does to them, the signs a cover is done, and what you can do to get more years out of the one you've got. ## Why There's No Single Answer

Two covers made of the same material can age very differently. One sits on a shaded north wall with a clear drain below. The other sits on a south-facing wall, takes full afternoon sun, catches a drift every January and has a cracked drain full of standing water. Same product, very different life.

The things that move the needle:

- **The material and how thick it is.** Thicker sheet usually handles hail and snow better.
- **Sun exposure.** South and west walls get the most UV.
- **Span.** A wide cover has more unsupported area to flex under load.
- **Fit and fasteners.** A cover that fits snugly and is anchored well moves less.
- **Maintenance.** A cover that gets rinsed and checked each season outlasts one that nobody looks at.

Treat any lifespan figure you see online as a rough idea only.

## How Each Material Ages

### Polycarbonate

Polycarbonate is tough, which is why it's a common choice for clear covers. It resists impact well. Its weakness is sun. Over the years, UV light can make it yellow, haze over and eventually turn brittle, unless the sheet has a protective layer on the surface. Once the surface layer wears away, the aging speeds up. Broadly, a good polycarbonate cover can last for many years, but the fine print is in the UV protection and the exposure.

### Acrylic

Acrylic stays clear and resists yellowing well, but it's more rigid and tends to crack instead of flexing. A hailstone or a hard blow can crack it, and cold makes it more brittle. A cover that is cracked is a repair or replacement question, and our post on [cracked window well cover replacement](/blog/cracked-window-well-cover-replacement/) covers that.

### Steel grates and metal covers

A steel grate doesn't yellow or shatter, so sun and hail matter less. What matters is the finish. Galvanized or painted steel holds up as long as the coating does. Once the coating wears through at the welds, hinges and bottom edge, rust begins. Rust spreads from those spots. Steel can last a long time with some touch-up paint, but a grate left to rust will eventually weaken. See [metal vs plastic window well covers](/blog/metal-vs-plastic-window-well-covers/) for the trade-offs.

### Wood and DIY covers

Wood rots, warps and splits, especially where it touches soil or sits under wet snow. Plywood sheets and homemade frames can be fine for a season or two, and they'll show wear much faster than a manufactured cover. They're also the most likely to fail the escape rule, because DIY covers often get screwed down or weighted. If a cover sits over an egress window, it has to release from inside without keys, tools or special knowledge. That's section R310.4 of the 2018 IRC, which you can [read for Nebraska](https://up.codes/viewer/nebraska/irc-2018/chapter/3/building-planning#R310.4). Confirm with Lincoln Building and Safety for your address.

## What Nebraska Weather Does to Covers

### Sun

Open sky, long summer days and reflected light off a house wall all add up. UV is the main reason clear plastic ages out. A cover under a deck or a tree lasts differently than one in open sun.

### Hail

Spring storms in Lincoln can bring hail. Thicker polycarbonate usually shrugs off small hail. Thinner plastic and acrylic can crack or pit. Check covers after a big storm, even if they look fine from across the yard.

### Snow and ice load

A wet, heavy snow on a wide span puts real weight on a cover, and drifts can stack up against the house. A cover that flexes and springs back each winter will eventually stay bowed. For more on this, read our post on [window well cover snow load in Nebraska](/blog/window-well-cover-snow-load-nebraska/). A cover should never be the thing you stand on or pile snow on.

### Freeze-thaw

Water gets into small gaps, freezes, expands, and widens the gap. This hits fasteners, frames, seams and the foundation around them. It's one reason screws work loose and why you should recheck mounting each spring.

## Signs It's Time to Replace

You don't need a cover to fall apart before replacing it. Watch for:

- **Yellowing that has turned to haze.** Cloudy plastic lets less light in and is often brittle underneath.
- **Cracks that spread**, especially from corners or screw holes.
- **A sag that doesn't spring back** after the snow melts.
- **Flaking, chalky surface** you can scratch with a fingernail.
- **Rust that has thinned bars or eaten through welds** on a steel grate.
- **Soft, black or crumbling wood.**
- **Bent frame or hinges** that no longer sit flat.
- **Latches or hinges that won't release easily from inside the well.** This one matters most for safety.
- **A cover that blows off** even after you've re-anchored it.

If you spot a few of these, price a new cover. Covers and ladders typically run $150 to $600, which is a modest outlay next to what a failed cover can lead to: water in the well, debris, a missing safety barrier over an open hole.

## Care That Gets You More Years

You can't stop aging, but you can slow it down.

1. **Rinse it each season.** Use water and mild soap. Skip harsh solvents and scouring pads on plastic, since they scratch and dull the surface.
2. **Clear the surface.** Leaves, dirt and snow trap moisture and add load. Brush off snow gently instead of chipping ice.
3. **Check the fasteners each spring.** Tighten or reset any that have loosened.
4. **Look at the hinges and latches.** A bit of lubricant on hinges helps. Make sure the release still works from inside.
5. **Clean the well, too.** A cover over a well full of wet leaves doesn't last the way one over a clean, drained well does. Water that stays in the well can also rust the steel and shorten the life of everything above it.
6. **Touch up steel.** Wire-brush early rust, treat it and repaint.
7. **Keep the grade sloping away from the house** so water doesn't pour into the well.

## Check the Well Under the Cover

Sometimes the cover fails early because the well beneath it is the problem. A bowed or rusted well can shift, which stresses the cover frame. A well that doesn't drain fills with water, adds weight and speeds up decay. If your cover keeps failing, have someone look at the whole setup. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long should a window well cover last?

There's no fixed answer. Material, sun, hail, snow load and care all change it, so any figure is a rough estimate. Manufactured polycarbonate and steel covers generally outlast wood or homemade ones, but a shaded cover and a south-wall cover of the same type can age very differently.

### Why is my clear window well cover turning yellow?

Usually UV exposure. Sunlight breaks down plastic over time, and once the surface layer wears off, yellowing and haze speed up. Brittle, yellow plastic should be replaced.

### Can I extend the life of my cover?

Yes. Rinse it, clear snow and debris gently, re-check fasteners every spring, lubricate hinges, touch up rust on steel and keep the well draining. None of this stops aging, but it slows it.

### Do I need to replace the cover if only the hinges are bad?

Not necessarily. If the sheet or grate is sound, a hinge or latch replacement can be a reasonable repair. Just make sure the cover still opens easily from inside the well.

### Should a window well cover come off in winter?

Most covers are built to stay on year-round, and winter is when they do their most useful work, keeping snow and ice out of the well. Just keep snow brushed off the top, and make sure a cover over an egress window still releases from inside without keys or tools.
