# Terraced, Stone and Block Window Wells: What Changes From a Steel Well

A terraced window well steps back from the window in two or more tiers, usually built from retaining wall block, natural stone or timber. It looks like part of the landscape instead of a metal bucket in the dirt. It also has to do the same job as any other egress window well, and a few details work differently once you build it from stone instead of buying it off the shelf.

This page covers what the code asks of a stone or tiered well, how built-in steps compare with a ladder, and where these wells tend to go wrong in Lincoln's clay soil and freeze-thaw cycles. For a plain steel-versus-plastic-versus-fiberglass comparison, see [the window well material comparison](/blog/window-well-material-comparison/). ## What a terraced well still has to meet

A window well is not exempt from egress rules because it is pretty. If the window is the required emergency escape for a bedroom or basement, the opening and the well both have to work.

The numbers in the IRC for the window itself are the same for every well: 5.7 square feet of net clear opening (5.0 at a grade-floor window), at least 24 inches high and 20 inches wide, with the sill no higher than 44 inches from the floor. Our page on [IRC egress window code requirements](/blog/irc-egress-window-code-requirements/) walks through them.

For the well, the IRC asks for a horizontal area of at least 9 square feet, with at least 36 inches of projection from the wall and 36 inches of width. That is a clear space you can actually stand in with the window open. A tiered well makes this measurement trickier, because a step that sticks into the space does not count as open floor. The code does allow a ladder or steps to encroach up to 6 inches into the required dimensions, but no more. If you are laying out tiers, measure the clear area at the floor of the well and at the sill, not at the top lip.

## Steps built into the tiers instead of a ladder

This is the best argument for a terraced design. The IRC requires a permanently affixed ladder or steps when the well is deeper than 44 inches, and the steps have to be usable with the window fully open. A terrace can be the steps.

The IRC text, reproduced on [UpCodes as R310.2.3.1](https://up.codes/s/ladder-and-steps), says the ladder or rungs need an inside width of at least 12 inches, must project at least 3 inches from the wall, and may be spaced no more than 18 inches apart vertically for the full height of the well. The same section says these ladders and steps do not have to meet the regular stair rules in R311.7. That page is the Texas adoption of the 2018 IRC, so treat it as a copy of the model text. Lincoln's adopted edition and any local amendment are for the permit office to confirm, and [the Lancaster County and Lincoln permit page](/blog/lincoln-lancaster-county-egress-permit/) explains how to ask.

What this means for tiers:

- **Each tread should be at least 12 inches wide and give you a usable step.** A decorative ledge that is 6 inches deep and fine for a flowerpot is not a step.
- **No rise between treads over 18 inches.** A 48-inch-deep well in two tiers has about 24 inches of rise per tier, which is more than the 18 inches the rung spacing allows. Three tiers get you closer. This is why the depth of the well decides how many terraces you need.
- **The steps must not block the window.** The tiers have to leave the window able to swing fully open, and the path from the sill to the top has to stay clear.

Because a terrace is not a ladder, it is worth asking the permit office whether a tiered wall with treads is accepted as "steps" in their reading of the code before any block is stacked. A Family Handyman build of a timber-walled well with interior stairs notes that [the city there required each step to be at least 12 inches wide, set 3 inches from the wall, with no more than 18 inches of rise](https://www.familyhandyman.com/project/how-to-install-a-terraced-window-well-in-your-basement/). That is one city's requirement, and it matches the model code language above. It is not a Lincoln statement.

A shallow well, 44 inches or less, needs no ladder or steps under the model code. Even then, a step or two can make the well safer to climb out of, and for pets and kids it is a real advantage. See [window well ladder requirements](/blog/window-well-ladder-requirements/) for how the 44-inch threshold works.

## Stone, block and timber compared with steel or composite

| Feature | Stacked block or stone | Steel (corrugated) | Composite or plastic |
|---|---|---|---|
| Egress footprint | Whatever you build, so you must check it | Set by the manufacturer | Set by the manufacturer |

## Drainage is where stone wells fail

Water behind and under a window well has to go somewhere. In Lincoln that matters twice: spring storms dump a lot of water at once, and Lancaster County's clay soils drain slowly.

The IRC says the well must be designed for proper drainage, and the usual way is a connection to the foundation drainage system. The [model text](https://up.codes/viewer/nebraska/irc-2018/chapter/3/building-planning#R310.2.3.2) allows an approved alternative, and skips the requirement on well-drained sand and gravel soils, which most Lincoln clay is not. Ask the permit office what Lincoln expects.

For a stacked wall, the common approach is:

- A compacted gravel base under the well floor, not a solid slab.
- Clean angular stone (a 3/4-inch clean rock is typical) behind the wall, wrapped in filter fabric so soil does not clog it.
- A drain path from the bottom of the well to the footing drain or a sump system.
- Backfill sloped away from the well. Our guide to [window well grading](/blog/window-well-grading-drainage-outside/) shows the slope.

Block joints let some water through, but they are not a drain. If the gravel at the bottom is the only exit, a wet spring fills the well like a bathtub.

We tie wells into working footing tile as part of well work. Rebuilding failed tile along the foundation or adding a waterproofing system is a job for a waterproofing contractor.

## Frost heave and wall height

Soil in Lincoln freezes and thaws every winter. A stone or block wall sits on the ground like a retaining wall, and it needs a base that will not shift when the soil heaves.

- **Base.** A compacted gravel pad, set below grade, is the usual start for dry-stacked block.
- **Height.** Retaining wall block is rated for a certain height unrestrained. Taller walls often need geogrid reinforcement, and tall or stepped walls can require an engineer's stamp for the permit. Whether yours does depends on the height and the local rules.
- **Pressure from the house side.** A wall that holds back the soil along the foundation takes real load. This is the kind of structural design we do not do.

Heaving cracks and tilts a wall over a few winters, and a wall that tilts toward the window is harder to fix than a steel well you can lift out.

## Covers for odd shapes

Standard covers fit standard wells. A stepped, curved or oversized stone well rarely matches anything off the shelf, so plan the cover before you plan the walls.

- Measure the opening at the top lip, not at the base. The measuring steps are in [how to measure a window well cover](/blog/how-to-measure-window-well-cover/).
- The cover on an egress well has to be openable from the inside without keys, tools or special knowledge, and with no more force than a normal window requires. A custom cover held down with screws, or with a release someone in the well cannot see or reach, fails that test.

Expect to commission a custom cover for any terrace shape that is not a rectangle. We do not give a price for custom covers, because the figure depends entirely on the shape.

## When a stone or terraced well makes sense, and when it does not

A built well is a reasonable fit if the window is deep (say 60 inches or more), the well faces a patio, or a landscape contractor is already building a wall next to it. A manufactured well is usually better if the window is shallow, you want a fast install and a stock cover, or you do not want to rely on a retaining wall holding up against clay soil for years.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can a terraced window well serve as the steps the code requires?

The IRC requires a permanently affixed ladder or steps in a well deeper than 44 inches. The model text sets 12 inches minimum inside width, at least 3 inches of projection from the wall, and no more than 18 inches between steps vertically. A tiered wall can meet that if every tread is deep enough and the rise is small enough, but confirm with the Lincoln permit office before building.

### Do I need a permit for a stone or block window well?

If the well serves an egress window, it is part of the egress work and normally falls under the permit for that window. A retaining wall of any real height can also have its own rules, including engineering. Ask the permit office before you start, because building first and asking later is the expensive order.

### Is a stone window well better than steel or composite?

Not automatically. Stone and block look better and can hold steps, but they depend on a good base, drainage and a wall that survives freeze-thaw in clay soil. A manufactured well is faster to install and easier to replace. Our [window well material comparison](/blog/window-well-material-comparison/) covers the manufactured options.

### What drainage does a stone window well need?

The IRC refers to connecting the well to the foundation drainage system, and the usual build is a gravel base, clean stone behind the wall and a path to the footing drain. Block joints let some water through but do not replace a drain. Rebuilding footing drains and waterproofing are done by a separate contractor.

### Can I put a standard cover on a terraced well?

Only if the top opening is a standard rectangle. Stepped, curved or oversized wells usually need a custom cover, and an egress cover must open from inside without keys, tools or special knowledge. Measure the top lip and plan the cover before you build the walls.
