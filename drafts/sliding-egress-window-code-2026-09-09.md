# Sliding Egress Window Code: When a Slider Actually Meets the Rule

Your basement slider looks wide enough through the rough opening, so why did the inspector still flag it? A sliding egress window only opens on one side. The other sash stays put, which means the actual escape path is close to half the window's total width, not the whole pane of glass you're looking at. That single fact trips up more basement sliders in Lincoln than any other part of the code.

If you're planning to install or replace a slider in a basement bedroom, it pays to work through the math before you order glass. Get the sizing wrong and you'll pay for a window, a wider rough opening, and a bigger well all over again.

## Why a Sliding Egress Window Loses Half Its Opening

A casement window swings its whole sash out on a hinge, so nearly the full window opening counts as clear space once it's open. A sliding egress window works differently. One sash is fixed in the frame and never moves. The other sash slides sideways behind or in front of it, and only that moving sash's clear width counts toward the code opening.

That's the core problem. A 60 in wide slider doesn't give you anywhere near 60 in of escape width. It gives you roughly the width of one sash, minus the frame and the track, and that number is what has to clear the code minimums, not the number on the spec sheet.

If you haven't settled on a window style yet, our comparison of [casement, slider, and inswing egress windows](/blog/casement-slider-inswing-egress-windows/) walks through the tradeoffs between all three. This article assumes you already want a slider and focuses on the narrower question: how wide does a slider have to be before it actually passes.

Nebraska follows the International Residential Code for emergency escape openings: 5.7 sq ft of net clear opening, a minimum 24 in clear height, a minimum 20 in clear width, and a sill no higher than 44 in above the finished floor. None of those numbers change because you picked a slider instead of a casement. The code doesn't grade sliders on a curve. It measures the opening you actually get when the sash is all the way open, and for a slider that's roughly half the unit.

## The 20 Inch and 24 Inch Rules Still Apply to a Slider

Homeowners sometimes assume that because a slider only has one sash doing the work, the width and height minimums are somehow softer for a slider. They aren't. The operable sash alone has to hit at least 20 in of clear width and 24 in of clear height, in addition to the two numbers multiplying out to 5.7 sq ft or more.

## Sizing a Sliding Egress Window for a Lincoln Basement

Here's the part most people skip: figuring out how wide the operable sash needs to be before you buy anything. Since only one sash opens, the net clear opening is simply that sash's clear width times its clear height.

The table below shows what that looks like at a 24 in clear height, which is the code minimum and a common height for a basement slider once you account for joist depth and sill placement.

| Operable sash clear width | Clear height | Net clear opening | Meets 5.7 sq ft minimum |
|---|---|---|---|
| 20 in | 24 in | 3.33 sq ft | No |
| 24 in | 24 in | 4.00 sq ft | No |
| 28 in | 24 in | 4.67 sq ft | No |
| 32 in | 24 in | 5.33 sq ft | No |
| 35 in | 24 in | 5.83 sq ft | Yes |
| 40 in | 24 in | 6.67 sq ft | Yes |

At the code minimum height, the operable sash needs to be close to 35 in of clear width before the opening reaches 5.7 sq ft. On a typical two lite slider with one fixed and one operable sash, that clear width usually means a total nominal window width somewhere in the 70 to 76 in range once the frame, track, and the fixed sash's own width get added in. Exact numbers vary by manufacturer, so treat that as a planning figure, not a code chart. The window's own cut sheet will list the certified clear opening.

That's the gap that surprises people. A casement that's 32 to 36 in wide can often pass on its own, because the whole sash opens. A slider usually has to be close to double that overall width to deliver the same usable opening.

## Where an Egress Window Slider Actually Makes Sense

None of this means a slider is the wrong choice. It means you have to size it correctly. There are a handful of situations where a slider is genuinely the better call.

A wide, shallow opening in a block foundation is the clearest one. Concrete block comes in fixed course heights, so a basement wall often has more room to go wide than to go tall without cutting an extra course. A slider uses that width instead of fighting for height. A casement sized to pass on height alone might need a taller cut than the wall easily gives up.

A shallow window well is another. A casement swings its sash outward into the well, so the well has to project far enough to let it open all the way without hitting the far wall. A slider's sash moves sideways instead of out, so it can work with a well that couldn't give an outswing casement room to open. The well itself still has to meet the 9 sq ft minimum with at least 36 in of horizontal projection and width, so a shallow well doesn't mean a small well.

Ease of operation matters too. A crank mechanism on a casement can be hard for someone with limited grip strength or shoulder mobility to work quickly in an emergency. A slider just needs a push, which some homeowners prefer for an older parent's room or their own aging in place plans.

Cost is the last piece. Sliders are often priced lower than a casement of the same brand and line, though once a slider is sized up to the width it needs to pass, that gap narrows. It's still frequently the cheaper of the two once you compare units that both meet code.

A split-level in Hickman with a block foundation is a good example of the well scenario. The wall gave plenty of width but only about 30 in of usable height before hitting the floor joist above, and the existing well couldn't project far enough for an outswing casement without running into a fence line. A wide slider paired with a new, deeper well came in around $5,500 to $9,500 for the full project, since both the window and the well had to be replaced.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can a sliding window ever meet the 5.7 square foot egress rule?

Yes. A slider can meet the rule, but the operable sash has to be wide enough on its own, since the fixed sash doesn't count. At the code minimum height of 24 in, that usually means an operable clear width close to 35 in, which typically means a total window width well beyond what a casement would need.

### Is a slider cheaper than a casement for egress use?

Often, yes, when comparing similar brands and lines. But once a slider is sized up to the width it needs to clear 5.7 sq ft, the price gap with a code-sized casement narrows. Well depth and rough opening work usually matter more to the final cost than the window style itself.

### Do both sashes on a slider need to open for it to count as egress?

No. Only the sash that moves counts toward the net clear opening. The fixed sash can be glass and can look identical to the operable side, but code only credits the space you get when the moving sash is fully open.

### What is the smallest slider that can legally serve as a basement bedroom's window?

At the code minimum 24 in clear height, the operable sash needs roughly 35 in of clear width, which usually means a total slider width somewhere around 70 to 76 in once the fixed sash and frame are included. Going taller reduces how wide the operable sash needs to be, but the sill can't sit higher than 44 in above the finished floor, which limits how much height you can trade for width.
