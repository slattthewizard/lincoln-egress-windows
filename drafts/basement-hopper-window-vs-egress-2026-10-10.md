# Basement Hopper Window vs Egress: Why Most Hoppers Don't Qualify

If your basement has small windows up near the ceiling that tilt inward from the top, you have hopper windows. They are the most common basement window in older Lincoln homes, and almost none of them count as egress. That matters the moment someone wants to sleep down there.

This page covers what a basement hopper window is, why it usually falls short of the code numbers, when it is perfectly fine to leave one alone, and what it takes to turn a hopper opening into a real egress window. ## What a basement hopper window is

A hopper window is hinged along the bottom edge. The top of the sash tilts inward, into the room, and the glass usually stays attached to the frame at the hinge. Think of a window that opens like a small drop-down flap. Most are wide and short, set in the upper part of the foundation wall, often with a window well outside.

Builders used them in basements for good reasons. They bring in air without letting rain run straight inside, they fit a short opening between the top of the foundation and the floor framing above, and they are cheap. For a utility room, a laundry area or an unfinished storage space, they do their job.

The problem is that a hopper was designed for ventilation and a little daylight. Emergency escape is a different job with different numbers.

## What egress requires

The IRC sets the numbers for emergency escape and rescue openings in section R310. You can read them in [the Nebraska 2018 residential code on UpCodes](https://up.codes/viewer/nebraska/irc-2018/chapter/3/building-planning#R310) (Lincoln may enforce a different edition, so treat that as a reference for the R310 wording and ask the city which one applies to your permit). The core requirements:

- Net clear opening of at least 5.7 square feet (5.0 square feet if the opening is at grade floor).
- Net clear height of at least 24 inches.
- Net clear width of at least 20 inches.
- Sill no higher than 44 inches above the floor.
- Operable from inside without keys, tools or special knowledge.
- A window well with at least 9 square feet of area and a 36 inch projection and width when the opening is below grade, plus a ladder or steps when the well is deeper than 44 inches.

"Net clear opening" is the part you can actually climb through with the sash open. It is not the frame size and not the rough opening. We go through the full set of numbers in [the IRC egress window code requirements](/blog/irc-egress-window-code-requirements/).

## Why a hopper almost never meets egress

Two things sink it: opening size and sill height. A third, the swinging sash, makes the first one worse.

### Opening size

Hoppers are typically wide and short. One retailer guide, [Koalaty Windows](https://koalatywindows.com/basement-hopper-windows/), says a typical 32 by 20 inch hopper does not meet the egress numbers. Do the math with those dimensions. Even before you subtract anything, 32 inches by 20 inches is about 4.4 square feet of glass and frame. The code wants 5.7 net clear.

The 24 inch minimum height is where hoppers usually fail first. Many basement hoppers are short in the frame, and after you subtract the frame and the sash, the clear height can land well under the code minimum. A window can be 36 inches wide and still fail because it is only 18 inches tall.

### The sash is in the way

On a hopper, the sash tilts into the room and stays there. When it is open, it hangs across the upper part of the opening. The same guide notes that the clear opening is a fraction of the full size once the tilted sash is in the way. For someone climbing out in smoke or in the dark, that sash is a real obstruction, and the code counts what is left after it.

Some manufacturers sell hopper-style units described as egress rated. If you see one, ask for the certified net clear opening for the exact size and the exact operating style, not the frame dimensions, and check it against the numbers above. A sash that opens only a few degrees, or one you have to lift out, is not the same as a casement that swings fully clear.

### Sill height

Hoppers sit high on purpose. A basement hopper is usually set near the top of the wall, well above the floor. The code caps the sill at 44 inches. Some hoppers sit at or above that, which means an adult would be climbing onto furniture to reach the opening, and a child or an older person might not manage it at all.

Put those three together and a basement hopper window is a ventilation window with an opening that is too short, partly blocked and too high. Swapping it for a newer hopper of the same size does not change any of that.

## When a hopper window is fine

Most hoppers in Lincoln basements do not need to change. The model code asks every basement for at least one escape opening, but [R310.6](https://up.codes/viewer/nebraska/irc-2018/chapter/3/building-planning#R310.6) says an existing basement does not need a new one just because it is being altered or repaired. The exception is a new sleeping room: create one, and that room needs its own egress opening. So in an existing house, a hopper is usually fine in spaces like these:

- Unfinished basements used for storage, a furnace, a water heater or laundry
- Workshops and mechanical rooms
- Recreation rooms, home gyms and play areas
- A family room or office without a bed

New houses are different, since a new basement needs its escape opening from the start, and local amendments can change the details, so confirm with the city when you plan a finish. If the room will have a closet, a door and a bed, [what counts as a legal bedroom in Nebraska](/blog/what-counts-as-a-legal-bedroom-nebraska/) covers the line in plain terms.

There is also a safety argument for adding egress to a basement family room even when the code does not require it. A finished basement with one stair and no second way out is a long way to travel in a fire. That is your call, not a requirement.

If the hopper itself is worn out (rust, fogged glass, rot, a sash that will not latch), replacing it with another hopper is a normal [basement window replacement](/basement-window-replacement-lincoln/). A non-egress basement window swap generally runs $400 to $1,200. Fix the well first if water is the cause, since a flooding well ruins the new window like it ruined the old one.

## Can you convert a hopper opening to egress?

Usually not in place. This is the part that surprises people. The hopper opening is too short, too narrow or too high, and the framing around it is part of the foundation wall.

To get from a hopper to an egress window, the opening has to change in size and often in position:

1. The wall is cut (concrete, block or poured) to enlarge the opening downward and sometimes sideways. The sill drops so it lands at or under 44 inches from the floor.
2. A new header or lintel is set above the opening to carry the wall.
3. A larger window goes in, usually a casement or slider that gives the full net clear opening when open.
4. A new, larger window well replaces the old one, with the right dimensions and a ladder or steps if it is deeper than 44 inches.

That is a cut-in. The process is the same one described in [how an egress window cut-in works](/blog/egress-window-cut-in-process/), and the foundation type changes how the cutting goes.

## How to check your own hopper

You can answer the question in ten minutes with a tape measure.

1. Open the sash as far as it goes.
2. Measure the clear height of the opening from the lowest part of the sash to the sill, or to the bottom of the frame if the sash is in the way. You need at least 24 inches.
3. Measure the clear width, frame edge to frame edge. You need at least 20 inches.
4. Multiply height by width and divide by 144. You need 5.7 (5.0 at grade).
5. Measure from the finished floor to the sill. You need 44 inches or less.
6. If the window is below grade, measure the well. It needs at least 9 square feet of floor area and 36 inches of projection and width.

If any one number fails, the hopper is not egress. If you are unsure, ask for a measurement and we will tell you what the opening would need.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is a basement hopper window an egress window?

Almost never. A hopper is usually too short and wide, its sash tilts into the opening and blocks part of it, and the sill often sits above 44 inches. Egress needs 5.7 square feet of net clear opening, 24 inches of clear height and 20 inches of clear width. Check your own numbers against those.

### Can I use a non egress basement window in a bedroom?

No. A sleeping room needs an emergency escape and rescue opening that meets the IRC numbers. A non-egress window is fine in a storage room, laundry or similar space where no one sleeps.

### Can I replace a hopper with an egress window in the same opening?

Only if the existing opening is already large enough and low enough, which hopper openings rarely are. In most cases the wall is cut to enlarge the opening and the well is rebuilt, which makes it a cut-in rather than a swap.

### Do egress hopper windows exist?

Some manufacturers market hopper-style units as egress compliant. Ask for the certified net clear opening for the exact size and check it against 5.7 square feet, 24 inches high and 20 inches wide, since the tilted sash reduces the clear opening.

### Do I need a permit to enlarge a basement window opening?

Cutting a larger opening in a foundation wall is structural work and generally needs a building permit. A Lincoln building permit typically runs $75 to $200, and we pull the permit as part of the project.
