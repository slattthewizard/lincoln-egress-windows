# Upstairs Bedroom Windows: Egress Code and the 24-Inch Fall Protection Rule

Most of what you read about egress windows is about basements. But the code that requires an escape opening doesn't care what floor the bedroom is on. Every sleeping room needs one. Upstairs, though, a second rule shows up that basements rarely hit: a low window high above the ground can be a fall hazard, and the code asks for protection.

These two rules pull in opposite directions. One says the window has to open wide enough to get out. The other says it shouldn't open wide enough for a child to fall out. ## Egress Isn't Only a Basement Rule

Under the 2018 IRC, which Nebraska and Lincoln use, section R310.1 requires an emergency escape and rescue opening in every basement and in each sleeping room. A bedroom on the second floor needs one just like a bedroom downstairs.

The numbers are the same as everywhere else: a net clear opening of at least 5.7 square feet (5.0 for a grade-floor opening), at least 24 inches of clear height, at least 20 inches of clear width, and a sill no higher than 44 inches above the floor. Confirm the details with Lincoln Building and Safety for your address.

For most upstairs bedrooms, the window is the escape route, and it's also the way a firefighter's ladder reaches the room. If you're not sure how the opening is measured, read [how to measure an egress window](/blog/how-to-measure-egress-window/).

## The Fall Protection Rule: R312.2.1

Section R312.2.1 deals with windows that could be a fall risk. It applies when both of these are true:

- The window is operable.
- The lowest edge of the opening is less than 24 inches above the finished floor inside the room, **and** the opening is more than 72 inches above the grade or surface below on the outside.

When both conditions hold, the window needs one of two kinds of protection:

1. **The opening is limited so a 4 inch sphere can't pass through.** The sash can't open more than that, even when it's opened as far as it goes.
2. **A window opening control device (WOCD) that meets ASTM F2090.** This is a hardware device that restricts how far the sash opens, but can be released so the window opens fully when someone needs to get out.

Those two numbers matter. A window sill of 24 inches or more from the floor isn't covered. A window that's low in the room but not far above the ground isn't covered either. The rule targets low windows on upper stories, where a toddler can climb to the sill and fall a long way. A basement window rarely meets both conditions, because it sits low to the ground outside.

## Where the Two Rules Collide

A bedroom window can meet the fall rule and still fail egress, and the other way around. Here's the conflict.

Say your upstairs bedroom has a low window. To satisfy R312.2.1, the opening could be limited to a 4 inch gap. But the same window has to work as an escape opening with a net clear opening of 5.7 square feet. A 4 inch gap is nowhere close.

That's why the code allows a window opening control device meeting ASTM F2090. The device limits the opening in everyday use, but it has to release so the sash opens fully for escape. Limiting the opening permanently doesn't work in a sleeping room, because it would block egress. The fall protection can't stand in the way of getting out.

## What a Window Opening Control Device Has To Do

A WOCD that satisfies the rule has to do two jobs.

- **Stop the window opening wide in normal use.** An adult can set it, or it holds the sash at a limited opening by default.
- **Release for escape.** Someone inside has to be able to open the window fully, without keys, tools or special knowledge, and without more force than operating the window. R310.4 says the same thing about bars, grilles and covers on an escape opening.

Those same words show up for security bars on basement windows. If the device needs a key, a tool, or a trick that a child or a person in smoke wouldn't know, it isn't acceptable on a sleeping room window.

We don't certify devices, and we won't point you to a product. When a device is on a window, look for a statement that it meets ASTM F2090 and that it releases without a tool. The building department decides what's accepted.

## Why It Comes Up With Replacement Windows

These rules usually come up when you replace a window. An old window installed before the rules is generally left alone until then. A new window in the same opening can trigger a question.

The 2018 IRC has a provision, R310.2.5, that exempts a replacement window in an existing opening from the size and sill rules when the replacement is the manufacturer's largest standard size that fits the opening and doesn't reduce the opening. The point is that you shouldn't have to rebuild a wall for a simple swap. Lincoln may apply this differently, so confirm with Lincoln Building and Safety before you order.

The fall protection rule is separate. If the replacement window is operable, the sill is under 24 inches, and the opening is more than 72 inches above grade, ask the building department how they treat that. Whether a replacement counts as new work for R312.2.1 can depend on how the city reads it.

This matters in older Lincoln houses, where an upstairs bedroom may have a low, double-hung window and a long drop to the yard.

## Practical Checks for an Upstairs Bedroom

Walk through the room with a tape measure.

1. **Find the sill height.** Measure from the finished floor to the lowest edge of the opening. Under 24 inches?
2. **Estimate the drop outside.** More than 72 inches from the window to the ground or surface below?
3. **If both are yes,** look at the window. Is the opening limited to a 4 inch sphere, or does it have a control device?
4. **Check the device.** Can an adult release it quickly, with no key or tool, to open the window fully?
5. **Measure the clear opening** with the device released. Does it reach 5.7 square feet, 24 inches of clear height and 20 inches of clear width?
6. **Check the sill height for egress.** No more than 44 inches.

If you fail step 5, the window fits the fall rule but not egress, and the bedroom has a problem. If you fail step 3 or 4, it's the other way around.

## Basement Bedrooms and the Fall Rule

A basement window is the opposite case. It's low to the ground, so the outside drop is nowhere near 72 inches, and R312.2.1 doesn't apply to it. A well, though, is its own kind of drop. A deep well is a fall hazard, which is why wells over 44 inches deep need a ladder, and why covers have to be strong enough and still release from inside. See [window well ladder requirements](/blog/window-well-ladder-requirements/) for how those pieces work.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do upstairs bedrooms need egress windows?

Yes. Under the 2018 IRC, every sleeping room needs an emergency escape and rescue opening, not just basement bedrooms. The net clear opening, height, width and sill height rules are the same.

### What is the 24 inch window fall protection rule?

R312.2.1 applies to an operable window with a sill less than 24 inches above the floor and an opening more than 72 inches above the ground outside. It needs an opening limited to a 4 inch sphere or a window opening control device meeting ASTM F2090.

### Can a window opening control device block my escape?

It shouldn't. A device that meets ASTM F2090 has to release so the window opens fully when someone needs out. If it needs a key or a tool, it isn't a safe choice on a sleeping room window.

### Does a new replacement window have to meet the fall rule?

It depends on how your building department treats replacements. R310.2.5 exempts some replacement windows from the size and sill rules, but fall protection is a separate section. Confirm with Lincoln Building and Safety for your address before you order.

### Does the fall rule apply to basement windows?

Almost never. Basement windows sit close to the ground outside, so the 72 inch condition isn't met. Their main safety concerns are a well that's too deep without a ladder, and covers that don't release from inside.
