# Egress Windows in Split-Level and Bi-Level Basements

If you own a split-level or a bi-level, you may have noticed that your "basement" doesn't feel much like the one in your cousin's ranch. The windows are bigger. You can see the lawn from the family room. Part of the wall may be above the ground outside. That changes the egress picture, usually in your favor, though not always in the way people assume.

Plenty of Lincoln neighborhoods built up in the 1960s through the 1980s include these house types, and the lower level of many of them was finished as a family room, a rec room or a bedroom. ## Why These Houses Are Different

In a standard full basement, the whole level sits below grade. Windows are small, up high on the wall, and open into a window well dug into the soil.

In a split-level or bi-level, the lower level is only partly below grade. Half of the wall height, or sometimes more, may be above the outside ground. The result:

- Windows are often larger because they sit in wall that's above grade.
- Some windows have a shallow well or no well at all.
- The ground outside may slope, so one side of the level is deeper than the other.
- The sill height inside may already be in the range that works for escape.

All of that can make egress easier. It can also create a false sense that the windows are fine. Size, sill height and opening type still have to meet the numbers.

## The Numbers Don't Change

Under the 2018 IRC, which Nebraska uses, an emergency escape opening has to meet the same dimensions no matter what house style you have. You can read the text in the [Nebraska 2018 IRC viewer](https://up.codes/viewer/nebraska/irc-2018/chapter/3/building-planning). The basics:

- Net clear opening of at least 5.7 square feet.
- At least 24 inches of net clear height.
- At least 20 inches of net clear width.
- A sill no more than 44 inches above the finished floor.

"Net clear opening" is the space left when the window is fully open, not the size of the glass or the frame. A big window can have a small net opening if the sash only slides halfway or the frame blocks part of the space.

## The Grade-Floor Exception

There's one number that matters especially for these homes. The code allows a smaller opening, 5.0 square feet, for what it calls grade-floor openings. This is an opening whose sill height is not more than 44 inches above or below the adjacent ground, so a person can step straight out without a well.

In a split-level, a lower-level window may qualify. If the bottom of the window is close to the outside grade, you may only need 5.0 square feet of net clear opening instead of 5.7. The 24 inch height and 20 inch width minimums still apply, and you can't get 5.0 square feet by cheating on one of them.

Whether a particular window qualifies depends on the measurement at that spot, and on how the city reads it. Measure from the outside ground at the window, not from some average lawn height, and confirm with Lincoln Building and Safety for your address.

## Does Your Existing Window Already Comply?

On these homes that's the first thing to check, and the honest answer is: maybe. Some lower-level windows in split-levels already work. Others look right and fail.

A quick way to check, with a tape measure:

1. **Open the window all the way.** Measure the clear height and the clear width of the opening you can actually pass through, not the frame.
2. **Multiply** height by width in inches and divide by 144. That's square feet. Compare to 5.7 (or 5.0 if it's a grade-floor opening).
3. **Measure the sill** from the finished floor up. It should be no more than 44 inches.
4. **Look outside.** Is there a well? Is the ground level with or near the sill? Is anything in front of the window?

Common reasons a big-looking window fails:

- It's a slider or a double-hung, and only half the window area opens.
- The sill is too high because the wall is mostly above grade and the window sits near the ceiling.
- A well exists but is too small or too shallow to climb through.
- Something outside blocks it: a shrub, an air conditioner, a deck, a fence.

If the window needs to be larger, that's a conversation about enlarging the opening, which is covered on our [egress window installation](/egress-window-installation-lincoln/) page. If the opening is fine and only the window itself is the problem, a swap into the same opening may do it, which is what our [egress window replacement](/egress-window-replacement-lincoln/) page covers.

## Replacing a Window in an Existing Opening

The 2018 IRC has a provision, R310.2.5, that exempts some replacement windows in an existing opening from the full size and sill rules. The replacement has to be the manufacturer's largest standard size that fits the opening, and it can't reduce the opening that's already there. Lincoln may apply this differently from the model code, so check with the city before you count on it.

For a split-level this can be a real help, because the existing openings are often decent in size. A new window that opens fully, like a casement, can turn a marginal opening into a useful one. Typical replacement figures run $1,200 to $3,000 for an egress window in an existing opening, often a half-day job. See the [cost page](/egress-window-cost/).

## Wells on Partly Exposed Walls

When the lower level is only partly below grade, wells show up in odd places. A wall may be above grade at one end and below at the other, because the lot slopes. You can end up with a shallow well, or a well that's a trench with a sloped back.

The model code's rules for a well, in R310.2.3, still apply when you have one:

- At least 9 square feet of horizontal area.
- At least 36 inches of projection and 36 inches of width.
- The window must be able to open fully.
- A permanently attached ladder or steps if the well is deeper than 44 inches.
- Drainage to the foundation drain or an approved alternative, except in well-drained soil types.

A shallow well often doesn't need a ladder. But it still needs the width and projection, and it still needs water to go somewhere. Lincoln's clay soil holds moisture and freezes, and a shallow well beside a sloped yard can collect runoff. We build wells with a gravel base and drain tile tie-in. Typical window well installation and drainage runs $600 to $2,500, depending on the layout.

If there's no well at all, because the window is at grade, you don't need one, but you do need clear space outside. A person has to be able to step out and move away. A bed of rock, a retaining wall or a fence right at the window can defeat the purpose.

## Fall Protection on Higher Windows

Here's a wrinkle that comes with these houses. Section R312.2.1 deals with falls. If an operable window has a sill less than 24 inches above the floor and the opening is more than 72 inches above the grade outside, the code wants fall protection. That can mean limiting the opening to a 4 inch sphere, or using a window opening control device that meets ASTM F2090 and still releases for escape.

In a split-level where the lower level sits high on one side, you can have a low sill on the inside and a long drop on the outside. That's the situation this rule targets. A window that's great for escape can be a hazard for a toddler. Ask the city how R312.2.1 applies to your window and what an approved device looks like.

## Creating a New Bedroom Changes the Rules

You aren't required to add a new escape opening just because you finish part of a lower level. Section R310.6 says altering an existing basement doesn't trigger a new opening unless you create a new sleeping room. If you do create a bedroom, that room needs its own compliant opening. For more on what makes a room count as a bedroom, read [what counts as a legal bedroom in Nebraska](/blog/what-counts-as-a-legal-bedroom-nebraska/).

If the room you want is on the exposed side of the house, you may be in luck, and the work may be simple. If it's on the buried side, a cut-in with a new well is more likely. Cut-ins typically take one to three days, with the Lincoln building permit typically $75 to $200. We pull it for our own work.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does a split-level basement need an egress window?

Any basement and every sleeping room needs an emergency escape opening under R310.1, with the exception that altering an existing basement doesn't require a new one unless you create a new sleeping room. A lower-level bedroom needs a compliant opening, and confirmation from Lincoln Building and Safety for your address.

### Can a window near ground level use the 5.0 square foot size?

Possibly. The 5.0 square foot minimum applies to grade-floor openings, where the sill is within 44 inches of the adjacent ground. The 24 inch height and 20 inch width still apply. Measure at the window and check with the city.

### My lower-level windows are large. Are they already egress windows?

Not necessarily. Look at the net clear opening when fully open, the sill height from the floor, and whether the window is a type that opens fully. Many large sliders and double-hung windows only open half their area.

### Do I need a window well on a split-level?

Only if the window sits below grade. If it's at or above the outside ground, you may not need a well, but you need a clear path outside. If a well is present, it has to meet the size, ladder and drainage rules.

### Can you replace my window without enlarging the opening?

Often yes, if the existing opening is workable. The 2018 IRC exempts some replacements in an existing opening if they're the largest standard size that fits and don't shrink the opening. Lincoln may apply this differently, so we confirm before quoting.
