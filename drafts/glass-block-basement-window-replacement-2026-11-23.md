# Replacing Glass Block Basement Windows: Venting Windows, Egress and What Changes

Plenty of older Lincoln basements have glass block windows. They were a sensible choice when they went in: they let light in, they're hard to break through, and they don't need a sash or latch. Then years go by. The mortar cracks, the basement smells stale, and someone wants to turn the room into a bedroom.

At that point the question is what to put in the opening instead. The answer depends on one thing: whether the room is going to be a bedroom. If you want help figuring that out for your house, call [(402) 261-0485](tel:+14022610485) or [request a free written estimate](/#contact).

## What Glass Block Does Well and Where It Falls Short

A glass block window is a wall of small glass units set in mortar and sealed around the edges. It's strong, it passes plenty of daylight, and it's hard to see through, which is why it often turns up in bathrooms and basements.

The tradeoffs are real:

- **No ventilation.** A standard glass block panel doesn't open. A basement with no operable windows gets stale, holds humidity and leaves nowhere for fumes or smoke to go.
- **No escape route.** You can't climb out through a solid panel of block. In an emergency it's a wall.
- **Mortar and sealant wear out.** Freeze-thaw cycles and settling crack the mortar joints and open the perimeter caulk. Water gets in through those cracks, and a panel that looks solid can leak at the edges.
- **Condensation.** The block itself is a cold surface in a warm room, and moisture collects on the inside face in winter. Some of what looks like a leak is condensation.
- **Hard to repair.** One cracked block means cutting out mortar, and tuckpointing or replacing the panel isn't a quick patch.

None of this makes glass block bad. For a storage area or a utility space, a well-sealed panel can last a long time. It just can't do the jobs that people increasingly want from a basement window.

## The Bedroom Rule: Why Glass Block Can't Serve a Sleeping Room

The 2018 IRC, which Nebraska and Lincoln use, requires an emergency escape and rescue opening in every basement and in each sleeping room (R310.1). A glass block panel can't count as that opening, because it doesn't open at all.

So if the room is, or will be, a bedroom, the glass block has to go. The window needs a net clear opening of at least 5.7 square feet (5.0 at a grade-floor opening), at least 24 inches of clear height, at least 20 inches of clear width, and a sill no higher than 44 inches above the floor. You can see the full list in our [guide to the IRC egress code requirements](/blog/irc-egress-window-code-requirements/), and [what makes a legal bedroom in Nebraska](/blog/what-counts-as-a-legal-bedroom-nebraska/) covers the rest of the picture. The [Nebraska 2018 IRC on UpCodes](https://up.codes/viewer/nebraska/irc-2018/chapter/3/building-planning) has the text. Confirm the details with Lincoln Building and Safety for your address, because the city has the final say.

If the room is not a bedroom, you have more freedom. Which brings us to the options.

## Option 1: Keep the Block and Repair It

If the panel is sound and you just want it tighter, a repair might be all it needs. That means raking out cracked mortar, repointing, and resealing the perimeter. It's the least expensive path and it keeps the security and the look you already have.

It doesn't add ventilation or an escape route. Choose this one for a room that will stay storage or a workshop, and where the cracks are limited and the block itself isn't loose.

## Option 2: A Vented Glass Block Panel

A vented panel keeps the glass block look and adds a small operable section, usually a hopper or slider built into the panel. The vent lets air through and often comes with a screen.

It solves the stale air and humidity problem. It doesn't solve the escape problem, because the vent is far too small to climb through. A vented panel makes sense for a basement bathroom, laundry or workshop where you want air and security but nobody needs to get out through it.

## Option 3: A New Hopper or Slider in the Same Opening

You can take out the glass block and set a regular basement window in the same opening. A hopper tilts in at the top, and a slider moves sideways. Both open for air, and both come in sizes that fit the common opening dimensions of older Lincoln foundations.

This is a straightforward swap. It's the same kind of project as any basement window replacement, and a non-egress window swap typically costs $400 to $1,200 per window. Our [basement window replacement](/basement-window-replacement-lincoln/) page covers what's involved, including cutting out the block and the mortar, setting a new frame, and sealing and flashing it so it holds up in freeze-thaw weather.

What a new hopper won't do is make the room a legal bedroom. Most basement openings sized for glass block are too small to meet the net clear opening for egress, even with the best window in them. If you're curious about the difference, [basement hopper window vs egress](/blog/basement-hopper-window-vs-egress/) breaks it down. Pick this option when you want air, easy cleaning and a window you can actually open, and the room is not a sleeping room.

## Option 4: Enlarge the Opening to Egress

If the room needs to be a bedroom, the opening has to grow. That means cutting the foundation wall to a larger size, installing a properly sized header over the new opening, setting a window that meets the egress numbers, and building a window well outside if the opening sits below grade.

In a below-grade opening the well has its own rules. It needs at least 9 square feet of horizontal area, at least 36 inches of projection and width, and room for the window to fully open (R310.2.3). A well deeper than 44 inches needs a permanently attached ladder or steps (R310.2.3.1). The well also needs a way to drain, either to the foundation drain or by an approved alternative (R310.2.3.2). Any cover or grille has to release from inside without a key, tool, or special knowledge (R310.4).

A cut-in with the window typically costs $3,500 to $7,000, and a full Lincoln project with the well and finishing commonly runs $5,500 to $9,500. A Lincoln building permit typically runs $75 to $200, and we pull the permit for our work. The timeline for a cut-in is usually one to three days. Foundation type matters too. Glass block openings in poured walls and block walls both can be cut, though the method and the work around the header differ. We do cut-ins in both. If the wall shows signs of structural trouble, such as stair-step cracks or bowing, that needs an engineer or a foundation specialist before anyone cuts it. ## Which Option Fits Which Room

| Room use | Best fit |
|---|---|
| Storage, utility, workshop | Repair the block, or a vented panel |
| Bathroom or laundry | Vented panel, or a new hopper or slider |
| Rec room, office, gym | New hopper or slider, or a vented panel |
| Bedroom, now or later | Enlarge the opening to egress |

If you're not sure whether the room will become a bedroom, think about resale. Buyers and inspectors look at basement bedrooms closely, and a room with no escape opening shouldn't be listed as a bedroom.

## Condensation and Moisture on the Way Out

If your glass block weeps in winter, a replacement helps, but only so far. A new window still faces a cold basement wall and warm indoor air. Running a dehumidifier, sealing the perimeter well and keeping the soil sloped away from the house all help. Clay soil around many Lincoln homes holds water against the foundation, and spring storms test that quickly.

Moisture or rot in the framing around an old opening is something we repair as part of a basement window replacement. Water coming through the wall itself, away from the window, is a waterproofing or foundation job for someone else.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I replace glass block with a regular window?

Yes. A hopper or slider can go into the same opening once the block and mortar are cut out and the frame is sealed and flashed. It gives you ventilation but won't count as egress unless the opening is enlarged.

### Does glass block count as an egress window?

No. Glass block doesn't open, so it can't serve as an emergency escape opening. A bedroom needs a window that meets the egress size, sill and operation requirements.

### Is a vented glass block window good enough for a bedroom?

No. The vent is too small to climb through, so it can't satisfy the escape requirement for a sleeping room.

### Can any glass block opening be enlarged for egress?

Often, but not always. It depends on the foundation, where the opening sits and what's around it. Cutting a foundation wall needs a proper header, and walls with structural problems need an engineer first. We can look at the wall and give you a written estimate.

### How much does it cost to replace glass block?

A basement window swap typically runs $400 to $1,200 per window. Enlarging to egress typically costs $3,500 to $7,000 for the cut-in, and a full project with the well commonly runs $5,500 to $9,500.
