# Energy-Efficient Egress Windows for Nebraska Basements

A basement egress window has two jobs. It has to open wide enough to get a person out, and it has to sit in a concrete wall that's cold half the year. The first job is set by code. The second is up to you, and it comes down to the label on the glass and how well the opening is sealed.

## Egress Rules Still Come First

An efficient window that doesn't meet egress rules is a problem in a bedroom. The 2018 IRC calls for a net clear opening of at least 5.7 square feet (5.0 at grade-floor openings), at least 24 inches of clear height, at least 20 inches of clear width, and a sill no higher than 44 inches above the floor.

Those numbers are the net opening, with the sash fully open. A window can be energy efficient and still fail the size test. Start with an egress window that opens enough, then compare efficiency among those. Confirm requirements for your address with Lincoln Building and Safety.

## Reading the NFRC Label

Every rated window carries a label from the National Fenestration Rating Council. It gives the performance of the whole window, not just the glass. Here's what the numbers mean.

**U-factor.** This measures how much heat passes through the window. Lower is better. In a cold climate, U-factor is usually the number to watch first.

**Solar Heat Gain Coefficient (SHGC).** This is how much of the sun's heat comes through as warmth. A below-grade basement window in a well gets little direct sun, so SHGC matters less here than on a south-facing upstairs window. Still, look at it on the label.

**Air leakage.** This tells you how much air passes through the window assembly. A lower number means a tighter window. Not every label shows it, so ask for it.

**Visible Transmittance.** This is how much daylight gets through. In a basement, you want light, so don't pick a coating so dark that the room goes dim. A window well already cuts daylight.

Compare the same type of window against the same type. A casement and a slider won't have identical numbers, and the label for each is the starting point for the comparison.

## ENERGY STAR and the Northern Zone

ENERGY STAR sets performance criteria for windows by climate region, and Nebraska falls in a northern region. Look for the ENERGY STAR mark and check which zone the product is certified for. Windows built for a warmer zone won't perform the same here. The program's criteria change over time, so we're not quoting numbers. Go to [energystar.gov](https://www.energystar.gov/) for the current requirements and the map.

The label doesn't replace a look at the full NFRC numbers. Use it to narrow the field, then read the label.

## Glass: Low-E Coatings and Gas Fills

Most efficient windows use the same few ideas:

- **Double-pane or triple-pane glass.** Two or more panes with sealed air space between them slow heat transfer. Triple-pane adds more, with extra weight and cost.
- **Low-E coatings.** A thin metallic coating on one or more glass surfaces reflects heat. It keeps warmth inside in winter. Different coatings favor different seasons, so the right one depends on the climate.
- **Gas fills.** Argon or krypton in the space between panes slows heat movement better than plain air.
- **Warm-edge spacers.** The spacer at the edge of the glass can be a weak point for heat and for condensation. A better spacer helps the edge of the glass stay warmer.

Ask the supplier which coating and fill the window has, and make sure the numbers on the NFRC label match. Some fancy-sounding options add little.

## Frame Material Matters

The frame is part of the window. Vinyl, fiberglass, wood and aluminum conduct heat differently. Aluminum conducts a lot and tends to be cold unless it has a thermal break. Vinyl and fiberglass usually insulate better. Wood insulates well but needs upkeep, especially in a damp basement.

In basements, moisture is a factor as well. Wood next to a cold, damp foundation wall can rot if the flashing and sealing aren't right. ## Installation Matters as Much as the Glass

You can buy a window with excellent numbers and lose most of the benefit if the opening around it leaks. The NFRC label rates the window as built in a lab. Your house has gaps.

Here's where the performance is won or lost:

- **The gap between the frame and the opening.** This space, around the window buck, has to be insulated and sealed. Stuffed with whatever was handy, it leaks air.
- **Air sealing.** A good seal around the frame stops drafts and keeps moist air from the house from reaching cold surfaces.
- **Insulation around the buck.** The buck is the frame set into the concrete opening. Insulation there cuts heat loss at the edge, where concrete conducts cold into the room.
- **Flashing and water management.** A window that leaks water loses efficiency and risks rot. Water, not air, is what ruins basement openings.
- **The well.** Gravel and drainage at the base, and a cover that keeps snow from packing against the glass, affect how the window sits through winter.

A tight, well-insulated opening with a decent window usually beats an excellent window in a sloppy opening. ## Condensation on Cold Basement Glass

Basement glass fogs because the glass surface is colder than the room's air, and warm moist air condenses on cold surfaces. Basements are often damp, and the concrete next to the window is cold, so condensation is common.

A higher-performing window helps because the inner glass surface stays warmer. It doesn't solve every case. Other steps matter:

- Control humidity in the basement. Dehumidifiers, a working bathroom fan, and a dryer that's vented outside all help.
- Keep air moving across the glass. Heavy curtains and furniture pushed up against the window trap damp air.
- Fix leaks. Water entering the well or around the frame adds moisture.
- Check the seal. Fog or moisture between the panes means the sealed glass unit has failed, and the glass or the window needs replacing.

A well with poor drainage can keep the area around the window damp. If moisture is a constant issue, see our article on [egress window condensation](/blog/egress-window-condensation/).

## A Practical Way to Compare Windows

When you're comparing options, put the numbers side by side:

1. Check that each meets egress size for the opening and the room.
2. Compare the NFRC U-factor, with lower better.
3. Look at air leakage if listed.
4. Check the ENERGY STAR mark for the northern zone.
5. Ask about glass coatings, gas fill and spacers.
6. Look at frame material and how it handles moisture.
7. Ask how the opening is sealed and insulated.
8. Get the plan in writing.

## Don't Chase One Number

It's easy to fixate on a single figure. A great U-factor in a window that won't open wide enough is a poor choice for a bedroom. A tight window installed with gaps loses the benefit. The best result is a window that meets egress rules, has a good label, and is sealed and insulated well.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What U-factor should I look for?

Lower is better, but we don't pin it to a single number because the right target depends on the window type and the current ENERGY STAR criteria for the northern zone. Check energystar.gov for the current requirements and compare the NFRC labels side by side.

### Do energy-efficient windows still meet egress requirements?

They can, but check the net clear opening. The window has to provide at least 5.7 square feet (5.0 at grade-floor openings), 24 inches of clear height and 20 inches of clear width, with the sill at or below 44 inches. Efficiency doesn't change those rules.

### Why does my basement window fog up?

Warm, moist air meets cold glass and condenses. Reduce humidity, keep air moving, fix any leaks, and consider a better-rated window. Moisture between the panes means the glass unit has failed.

### Is triple-pane worth it for a basement?

It depends on the window and the opening. Triple-pane adds insulation, but the gap around the frame and the humidity in the basement often matter more. Ask for the NFRC numbers and compare.

### Does the installation really affect efficiency?

Yes. Air leaks and missing insulation around the frame can undo the benefit of good glass. Ask how the opening is sealed and insulated.
