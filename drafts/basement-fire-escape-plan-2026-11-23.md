# Basement Fire Escape Plan: How to Practice Using Your Egress Window

Most families have a rough idea of how they'd get out of a burning house. The plan usually goes: down the hall, out the front door. That plan has a hole in it if someone sleeps in the basement. The stairs may be the very thing the fire blocks, and the person downstairs may be the last to hear the alarm.

An egress window is the second way out. But a window nobody has ever climbed through is a theory, not a plan. ## Start With Two Ways Out of Every Room

The basic rule of home fire escape planning is simple: every sleeping room needs two ways out. One is the normal door. The other is a window or a second exit. National fire safety groups such as the [U.S. Fire Administration](https://www.usfa.fema.gov/) and [NFPA](https://www.nfpa.org/) publish free escape-planning guides, and they all start from that same idea.

In a basement bedroom, the door leads to a hallway or a family room, and from there to the stairs. If a fire starts at the top of those stairs, or in the furnace room between the bedroom and the stairs, that route is gone. The window has to work without the door.

So sketch your basement on paper. Mark every bedroom, mark the stairs, and mark every window. Then ask the plain question for each sleeping spot: if the door route is blocked, what is the second route? If the answer is "I don't know" or "the little window up near the ceiling," that's the first thing to fix.

## Check That the Window Is Actually a Way Out

A basement window can be a window without being an escape route. Before you practice anything, look at it with an escape in mind.

- **Size.** Someone has to fit through it. Our [egress window installation](/egress-window-installation-lincoln/) page covers the sizes in detail. The short version: 5.7 square feet of net clear opening (5.0 at a grade-floor opening), at least 24 inches of clear height, at least 20 inches of clear width.
- **Sill height.** The bottom of the opening should be no more than 44 inches above the floor. Anything higher and a child or an older adult is climbing, not stepping.
- **How it opens.** Open it all the way. Does the sash swing or slide fully out of the way? Does the lock or crank take two hands and some effort? Anyone who sleeps in the room should be able to open it half asleep.
- **Bars and grilles.** Anything over the window or well has to release from inside without a key, a tool, or special knowledge.

If the window fails any of these, the plan isn't ready. Sometimes the fix is a bigger opening, sometimes it's a different cover. The place to start is a measuring tape and an honest look.

## Walk the Whole Route, Not Just the Window

Getting to the window is step one. The route continues outside. Stand in the well and look up.

Is there a ladder or steps if the well is deep? A well deeper than 44 inches needs a permanently attached ladder or steps under the model code, and for good reason. Nobody climbs a bare vertical wall of corrugated steel in socks. Is there room to stand in the well and move? A well is supposed to be at least 36 inches in projection and width, so a person can actually stand in it and turn around.

Then look at the top. Is there a cover? Can it be lifted from below, with one hand, without a key? Is there anything on top of it, like a planter, a snow shovel or a flower pot? Our [window well covers](/window-well-covers-lincoln/) page explains what a cover has to do to still work as part of the escape route.

Finally, look past the well. Where does a person standing at the top of it go next? Is there a clear way across the yard, or a fence, a hedge, a stack of firewood in the way?

## Practice It, Because Reading About It Isn't Practice

Here's the part most plans skip. Do a drill. Not a lecture, an actual drill.

1. **Pick a time when everyone is home**, ideally in the evening. Tell the household this is a practice.
2. **Start in bed.** Whoever sleeps in the basement bedroom lies down in it, in the dark if you can.
3. **Sound the alarm.** Use the test button on a smoke alarm so everyone hears what it really sounds like.
4. **Go out the window.** Open it, get through it, climb the well, and go to the meeting place. Time doesn't matter yet. Getting it right does.
5. **Talk about what was hard.** The lock was stiff. The ladder wobbles. The cover stuck. Fix those things this week, not someday.

Do it again a few months later. Do it again when the household changes, like a new baby, a visiting grandparent, or a teenager who moved downstairs.

## Kids and Older Adults Need a Different Drill

Everyone should practice, but the people who struggle most are the ones you should watch most closely.

**Children.** A small child may not be able to open the window alone, may not be able to climb the ladder, and may be scared to go out into the dark. Let them practice with an adult below them in the well and another above. Teach them one job: get to the window and yell. Don't hand an eight-year-old a plan that depends on them pushing up a heavy cover alone. If they can't do it yet, the plan needs an adult in the basement bedroom nearby, or a different bedroom.

**Older adults.** Climbing out of a well takes balance, grip and leg strength. Try it in daylight, with no smoke and no hurry, and see honestly how it goes. If the sill is high or the well is deep, a sturdy step or a better ladder might make the difference. If it's still too hard, that's a sign the sleeping room should be on a main floor. A good escape plan fits the people who actually live in the house.

**Anyone with a mobility aid.** A person using a walker or wheelchair isn't going out a window well. If someone in the household relies on one, their bedroom should be on the main level, with the plan built around the doors.

## Keep the Well and Cover Clear in a Lincoln Winter

An escape route that works in July can be useless in January. Lincoln gets enough snow, drifting and freeze-thaw cycles that wells fill up in ways people don't expect.

- **Snow.** A drift can pack a well to the top and sit on a cover. After a heavy storm, clear the well and the path from it, the same way you'd clear the front steps.
- **Ice.** Water that sits in a well and freezes can seal a cover shut or make a ladder slick. Good drainage at the bottom of the well matters here. We install gravel and tie into drain tile as part of well work, and the model code expects well drainage to the foundation drain or an approved alternative. See our [window well installation](/window-well-installation-lincoln/) page.
- **Clay soil.** Lincoln's clay holds water. A well with no drain can fill after spring rain and stay full.
- **Stuff.** Leaves, toys, a grill cover, a bag of salt. The well is not storage.

Make a habit of looking at the well each time you shovel. It takes almost no time.

## Pick a Meeting Place and Say It Out Loud

Once everyone is out, they need to know where to go. Choose a spot at a safe distance in front of the house, like a mailbox, a neighbor's tree or the end of the driveway, somewhere you can see from the street. A basement window usually puts you on the side or back of the house, so the meeting place needs to be reachable from there without going back past the house.

Then the rule that matters most: once you're out, you stay out. Nobody goes back in for a pet, a phone or a wallet. Call 911 from outside, from a neighbor's phone or your own cell once you're at the meeting place, and let the fire department handle the rest.

## Alarms Are Part of the Plan

You can't escape from a fire you haven't heard. Smoke alarms belong in each sleeping room, outside each sleeping area, and on each level of the home including the basement. Our other article on [smoke and CO alarm requirements for a basement bedroom](/blog/smoke-co-alarms-basement-bedroom/) covers where they go. ## When the Plan Shows the Window Isn't Good Enough

Sometimes the drill tells you something uncomfortable. The window is too small. The sill is too high. The only window faces a deck or a fence. There's no well, or the well is too shallow to stand in. That doesn't mean the room can't be used, but it does mean nobody should sleep in it until you have a real second way out.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How often should we practice a basement fire escape plan?

A couple of times a year is a good habit, and again whenever something changes in the household. A new bedroom downstairs, a new baby, an older relative moving in, or a new window or cover are all good reasons to run the drill again. The first time is the most useful, because that's when you find the stuck lock or the wobbly ladder.

### Should kids sleep in a basement bedroom?

That's a family decision, and there's no single answer. If a child sleeps downstairs, the window and well need to be something they can actually use, and the plan needs an adult who knows to go to them first. Some families keep very young children on a floor with a parent nearby and save the basement room for older kids and teens.

### Do we need a ladder in the window well?

A well deeper than 44 inches needs a permanently affixed ladder or steps under the model code. Even if yours is shallower, a step or ladder can help anyone climb out. Confirm the requirement for your address with Lincoln Building and Safety.

### Can I put a cover on the well and still use it as an escape route?

Yes, if the cover releases from inside without a key, a tool or special knowledge, and without more force than it takes to open the window. Test it from below. If you can't lift it with one hand, it's the wrong cover or it needs adjusting.
