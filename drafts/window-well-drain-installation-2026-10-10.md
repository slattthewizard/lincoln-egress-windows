# Window Well Drain Installation: Three Ways to Add a Drain to an Existing Well

Say your window well is 40 inches deep, lined with a few inches of gravel, and after every hard rain it fills like a bucket. You dig through the gravel and find soil, no pipe, nothing. That is the usual starting point for a window well drain installation: not a new build, but a well that was set without a drain and now needs one.

The fix is not one-size-fits-all. There are three realistic ways to give a well somewhere to send water, and each one depends on what is already under your yard. This article covers what each option involves, when it fits, and where retrofits go wrong. ## Does a Window Well Need a Drain at All?

The model building code says yes, with an exception. The IRC window well provision says wells must be designed for proper drainage by connecting to the building's foundation drainage system, or by an approved alternative method. The exception: a window well drainage system is not required where the foundation sits on well-drained soil or sand-gravel mixtures in Group I of the Unified Soil Classification System. You can read that wording in the IRC section as reproduced in [St. Paul's egress window handout](https://www.stpaul.gov/sites/default/files/2023-06/DSI.Bldg_EgressWindow%202020.pdf) and in the [IRC text on UpCodes](https://up.codes/viewer/nebraska/irc-2018/chapter/3/building-planning#R310.2.3.2).

Two cautions. Section numbers shift between code editions, so treat those as the model text, not a statement about which edition applies to your house. And well-drained sand and gravel is not what most of Lancaster County sits on. Clay and silty soils hold water, which is why a gravel-only well so often fills and stays full. If a drain is not required where you live, you may still want one, and it matters more if the room behind the window is a bedroom.

## First, Find Out What You Already Have

Do not pick an option until you know what is underground. Three quick checks:

1. **Dig out the gravel at the bottom of the well.** A pipe opening or a short stub means a drain was installed at some point. Bare soil or a concrete pad means it was not.
2. **Run a garden hose into the well for a few minutes.** If the level drops steadily, the well is draining somewhere. If it climbs, nothing is taking the water.
3. **Look inside the basement.** A sump pit, a floor drain, or an interior drain line along the wall tells you the house already has a place to send water.

Also find out whether your house has footing drain tile at all. Many older homes do not, or have tile that has been silted shut for years. A well drain tied into dead tile is a drain to nowhere. A plumber or a drainage contractor can scope the tile with a camera and tell you.

## Option 1: Tie the Well Into the Footing Drain Tile

This is the arrangement the code points to first. A perforated pipe at the bottom of the well runs through the gravel, connects to the perforated pipe that runs around the base of the foundation (the footing drain), and the water leaves with the rest of the foundation drainage. Where that tile ends depends on the house: a sump pit, or a pipe that runs downhill to daylight.

**When it works:**
- The house has working footing tile, and you can confirm it with a camera or by finding its outlet.
- The tile has somewhere to go that can take extra water during a storm.
- The well bottom sits at or above the tile, so water can flow into the pipe by gravity.

**What it involves:** digging down beside the well to reach the tile, then setting a pipe with a steady fall from the well bottom to the tile. Tying a well into working tile is part of our window well work, and it is easiest when the well is being replaced anyway. If the tile itself turns out to be crushed or missing along the house, rebuilding it is foundation drainage work for a waterproofing contractor. See [window well installation](/window-well-installation-lincoln/) for how we handle the well itself.

**What goes wrong:**
- **The tile is clogged or crushed.** Tree roots and silt are the usual causes. The new connection works for a week and then backs up.
- **The well sits lower than the tile.** Gravity only moves water downhill. If the well bottom is below the tile, the water has to be pumped.
- **No slope in the connecting pipe.** A flat pipe holds a little water and silt on every storm and clogs over time.
- **Extra load on a sump.** If the tile ends at a sump pump, the well now sends more water to a pump that was sized without it. A power outage during a storm then fills both.

## Option 2: Pipe to an Interior Sump or Standpipe

If the footing tile is missing or unreliable but the house has a sump pit, or can get one, the well can be drained to the inside. A pipe runs from the bottom of the well through the foundation wall, either into the sump pit directly or into a vertical standpipe that feeds the drain system. The sump pump then moves the water out through a discharge line.

**When it works:**
- There is already a sump pit with a pump in good shape, or you are installing one for other reasons.
- The foundation is poured concrete, where a clean opening can be cored through the wall. Block walls need more care because the pipe passes through hollow cores.
- The water table in your yard is low enough that the sump is not running constantly.

**What it involves:** a core hole through the foundation wall, a sealed pipe sleeve, and a connection to the sump. That is foundation and plumbing work, and it belongs to a waterproofing contractor or plumber, not to a window well installer. A hole in the wall also has to be sealed properly or you have traded one leak path for another.

**What goes wrong:**
- **The pipe is sealed poorly at the wall.** Water comes in around the pipe rather than through it.
- **The pump fails.** A window well that depends on a sump pump is only as reliable as the pump and the power. A battery backup is worth asking about.
- **Radon and humidity.** An open sump pit connects to the soil. If you add one, ask for a sealed lid. We cover high groundwater and sump setups in [window well high water table and sump](/blog/window-well-high-water-table-sump/).

## Option 3: A Gravel Dry Well

A dry well is a pit dug a few feet from the window well and filled with washed stone. A pipe carries water from the bottom of the window well to the pit, where it spreads out and soaks into the ground. No tie to the house, no pump.

**When it works:**
- The soil around the pit drains reasonably. In sandy or loamy soil a dry well can handle a normal storm.
- The pit sits lower than the well bottom and far enough from the foundation that the water you are sending away does not come right back.
- Nothing buried is in the way: utility lines, a sewer service, or a sprinkler main.

**When it does not:** Lincoln has a lot of clay. A dry well in clay fills during a heavy rain and stays full for days, because the water has nowhere to soak. Then it backs up into the window well, and you are back where you started. If you hit clay when you dig, a dry well is not a drain, it is just a bigger puddle.

**What goes wrong:**
- **Undersized pit.** A pit the size of a bucket is not enough for a roof's worth of water.
- **Fabric missing.** Without a layer of filter fabric around the stone, silt moves in and the pit loses volume year by year.
- **Frost.** A pit that is full of water in November can freeze and heave. Nebraska's freeze-thaw cycle is hard on shallow pipe.

## Fix the Water Before It Reaches the Well

A drain is not the first thing to try. Soil that slopes toward the house sends water straight into the well (see [window well grading and drainage outside](/blog/window-well-grading-drainage-outside/)), and a downspout that ends near a well can overload it alone. Extending the downspout is cheap. A cover keeps out leaves and some rain, though it does not replace drainage; covers and ladders as add-ons run $150-$600.

Whatever you add, the well still has to work as an escape route. A ladder or steps are required when the well is deeper than 44 inches, and covers on a bedroom window must open without keys or tools. See [window well ladder requirements](/blog/window-well-ladder-requirements/).

## Which Option Fits Your House

Working footing tile and a well bottom above it: tie in. No tile but a sump already in the basement: pipe to the sump. Loamy soil and open yard: a dry well can work. Heavy clay, no tile, no sump: the water has few good places to go, and the answer may be grading, a cover and a sump together.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does a window well need a drain?

The model IRC says window wells must be designed for proper drainage by connecting to the foundation drainage system or by an approved alternative. It makes an exception for foundations on well-drained Group I soils, which most clay-heavy Lincoln yards are not. Even where a drain is not required, a well with no way to shed water tends to fill after hard rain.

### Can I add a drain to an existing window well?

Yes. You can tie the well into working footing drain tile, run a pipe through the foundation wall to an interior sump, or build a gravel dry well nearby. Which one fits depends on whether your house has working tile, whether it has a sump, and how well your soil drains.

### Is a gravel dry well enough for a window well in Lincoln?

Only sometimes. A dry well relies on the surrounding soil absorbing water, and clay soil absorbs slowly. In clay it can fill and stay full after a storm, backing water into the window well. Dig a test hole and watch how fast water leaves before you commit.

### Can you install the drain pipe along my foundation?

We tie window wells into working footing tile as part of well work, and we pull the permit. Rebuilding failed tile along the foundation, or coring through the wall to an interior sump, is work for a waterproofing contractor or plumber.

### Why does my window well still fill up after I added a drain?

The usual causes are a clogged or collapsed pipe, a connection that sits higher than the well bottom, tile that was already blocked, or a pump that cannot keep up. Water entering from bad grading or a nearby downspout can also overwhelm a working drain. Start by running a hose into the well and watching where the water goes.
