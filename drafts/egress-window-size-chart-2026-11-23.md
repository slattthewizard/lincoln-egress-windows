# Egress Window Size Chart: Minimum Code Sizes for Casement, Slider and Hung Windows

Everyone wants a simple chart: window style down the side, a size across the top, done. The trouble is that the code doesn't set a size for the window. It sets a size for the opening you're left with once the window is open. That's why a casement and a slider with the same frame can land on opposite sides of the line.

This article gives you the rule, the math, and a chart of what the numbers mean for each style. ## The Rule in One Paragraph

The 2018 IRC, which Nebraska and Lincoln use, requires an emergency escape opening in every basement and each sleeping room (R310.1). The opening needs a net clear opening of at least 5.7 square feet, or 5.0 square feet at a grade-floor opening. The clear height has to be at least 24 inches and the clear width at least 20 inches. The sill can be no more than 44 inches above the finished floor. Confirm with Lincoln Building and Safety for your address.

We cover those numbers in plain language in [egress window size requirements in Nebraska](/blog/egress-window-size-requirements-nebraska/), so we won't repeat that article here. This one is about turning the rule into real window dimensions.

## Net Clear Opening: Where It's Measured

Net clear opening means the open space you could pass through with the sash fully open. It's measured on the open sash and frame, not on the frame's outside size and not on the glass.

When a sash is open, some of the space is still taken up by the sash itself, the frame, the track, the crank hardware or the stops. All of that is subtracted. What remains is the net clear opening. Two different measurements apply:

- **Clear height:** the vertical distance of the open space.
- **Clear width:** the horizontal distance of the open space.

Multiply them and divide by 144 to get square feet.

**Area in square feet = clear width (inches) x clear height (inches) / 144**

Flip it around and the target gets easy to remember. 5.7 square feet is 820.8 square inches. Any combination of clear width and clear height that multiplies to at least 820.8 meets the area rule, as long as the height is at least 24 and the width at least 20.

## Why the Same Rule Gives Different Sizes

Here's the part that surprises people. The window style decides how much of the frame is left open.

- **Casement:** the sash swings out on hinges. Nearly the whole frame is open, minus hardware and the sash edge. The clear opening is close to the frame size.
- **Slider:** one sash slides past another. At best, about half of the frame width is open.
- **Single or double hung:** one sash lifts past another. At best, about half of the frame height is open.

So to deliver the same clear opening, a slider or hung window needs a frame roughly double the size of a casement in the direction it opens. That's why a slider that looks plenty large in the wall can come up short, and why a casement that looks small can pass.

Frame thickness, sash rails and hardware change the real numbers, so treat "half" as a rule of thumb, not a promise.

Read the first row like this: at the minimum 24 inches of clear height, the clear width has to be at least 34.2 inches. At the minimum 20 inches of clear width, the clear height has to be at least 41 inches. A window that's exactly 24 inches high and 20 inches wide gives only 480 square inches, well short of 820.8, which is exactly why neither minimum alone tells you the window passes.

For a grade-floor opening, the target is 5.0 square feet, or 720 square inches. At 24 inches of clear height you'd need 30 inches of clear width.

## Worked Examples With Your Own Numbers

Say you've got a slider with a 40 inch wide frame and the sash slides over a fixed half. You open it and measure from the edge of the moving sash to the opposite frame, and you get about 18 inches of clear width. That fails the 20 inch minimum before you even calculate area.

Now say you have a casement and you measure 32 inches of clear width and 26 inches of clear height with the sash cranked fully open. That's 832 square inches, divided by 144, is 5.78 square feet. It passes the area rule. Heights and widths both clear the minimums.

One more. You measure 22 inches wide and 30 inches high on a hung window. That's 660 square inches, or 4.58 square feet. It fails, even though both numbers clear their own minimums. Passing the minimum height and width isn't enough on its own.

## Check the Manufacturer's Stated Net Clear Opening

Window makers publish a net clear opening for their egress-rated models. When you're buying, look for that number, not the frame size in the product name. Some product names reflect the frame or the rough opening, and the two can be very different from the clear opening on the open sash.

A few habits that save trouble:

1. **Ask for the stated net clear width, height and area,** not only the overall size.
2. **Check that the stated numbers are for the window fully open.** Some casements have a limiter or hardware that stops the sash short of fully open.
3. **Do the math yourself** with the formula above. If the stated numbers and the math don't agree, ask.
4. **Think about the finish.** Trim, blinds and screens can reduce the opening, so keep the opening clear.
5. **Confirm the sill height** from the finished floor, not from bare concrete. Flooring changes it.

Bars, grilles, covers and screens have to release from inside without keys, tools, special knowledge, or more force than operating the opening (R310.4). A window that passes the math but has a fixed security grille fails the rule.

## The Well Has Its Own Size Chart

Below grade, the window is only part of the opening. The well needs at least 9 square feet of horizontal area and at least 36 inches of projection and width, and the window has to be able to fully open into it. A casement that swings out needs room to swing. Wells deeper than 44 inches need a permanent ladder or steps. See [window well size requirements in Nebraska](/blog/window-well-size-requirements-nebraska/).

In Lincoln, freeze-thaw cycles and clay soil put pressure on foundation walls and wells, so a window that opens well on installation day needs a well that stays square and drains. That's part of picking a size, not an afterthought.

## When a Smaller Frame Is the Better Choice

Because a casement opens more, a smaller casement can meet the code where a much larger slider would be needed. That can mean a smaller cut in the foundation wall and a smaller well. A slider might look better with the room, or fit a wide, short opening where a casement can't swing. There's no single right answer. The right answer depends on the wall, the well, and what you want to see from the room.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the minimum size of an egress window?

There's no single window size. The code requires a net clear opening of at least 5.7 square feet (5.0 at grade), at least 24 inches high and at least 20 inches wide, with the sill no more than 44 inches above the floor. The frame size needed depends on the style.

### Why does a casement need a smaller frame than a slider?

A casement swings out and opens nearly the whole frame. A slider only opens about half. Hung windows open about half the height. To get the same clear opening, a slider or hung window needs a frame roughly double in the direction it opens.

### Is net clear opening measured on the frame or the sash?

On the open window. Open the sash all the way and measure the clear space you could pass through. Frame size, glass size and rough opening size don't count.

### How do I figure out the square footage of an egress window?

Measure the clear width and clear height on the fully open window, multiply them, and divide by 144. The result has to be at least 5.7 square feet, with 24 inches of height and 20 inches of width as separate minimums.

### Can I trust the size on the window's label?

Treat the label as a starting point. Look for the manufacturer's stated net clear opening and check it against the math. Your building department has the final say on whether a particular window is accepted at your address.
