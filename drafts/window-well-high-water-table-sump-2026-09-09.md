# Window Wells on a High Water Table: Where Does the Drain Actually Go?

Pour a bucket of water into a window well on a normal lot and it disappears into the gravel within a few minutes. Try that on a lot with a high water table, and the water just sits there, because the ground under the well is already full.

That's the reality on parts of plenty of Lincoln-area lots, especially out toward Waverly and Ashland, where the water table sits close to the surface for weeks at a time and heavy clay slows everything else down. A window well high water table problem isn't really about the well. It's about where the drain is supposed to send the water, and whether that destination can actually take it.

A window well drain has to discharge somewhere. On a dry, well-drained lot, that somewhere is usually just the surrounding soil, and gravity does the rest. On a wet lot, gravity alone isn't enough, and the well needs a real destination: the perimeter drain tile that feeds your sump pit, a dedicated dry well, or in tougher cases, a small pump.

## What a High Water Table Does to Window Well Drainage

Most window wells lean on a simple system: rain falls into the well, filters through a bed of gravel, and either drains through a pipe or soaks into the soil underneath. Our guide on [how window well drainage works](/blog/how-window-well-drainage-works/) covers that basic mechanism if you want the full picture first.

A high water table changes the second half of that system. When the ground around and under the well is already saturated, gravity has nowhere left to send new water, because the soil isn't absorbing anything. It's already full. Heavy clay makes the same problem worse even where the water table itself isn't unusually high, since clay barely lets water through even when it isn't saturated.

You'll usually see the same signs whether the cause is a genuinely high water table or just slow clay. A well that stays wet for days after a storm instead of hours. Water sitting at the bottom during dry stretches with no rain to explain it. A well that seems to fill from underneath rather than just from rain landing in it.

That last sign, water rising up through the gravel instead of pooling on top of it, is the clearest tell that the water table itself is the problem and not a clogged drain.

## Window Well Drainage Options for a High Water Table Lot

A window well drain on a wet lot needs somewhere reliable to send its water, and there are really three honest options. None of them is a trick. Each one just fits a different site.

**Tying into the perimeter drain tile that feeds your sump pit.** This is the option we reach for first when it's available. If your home already has a perimeter drain system running to a sump pit, extending the well's own drain line into that system gives the water an active outlet instead of asking already-saturated soil to absorb it.

**A dedicated dry well.** Where a sump tie-in isn't practical, an oversized gravel-filled pit set away from the foundation can absorb water on its own schedule instead of the window well's schedule. It runs on the same principle as a normal window well drain, just bigger and placed where the soil has more spare capacity.

**A small pump.** When neither of the above will keep up, mainly on lots where the water table itself is the real issue rather than just slow soil, a small pump in or near the well can move water out faster than gravity ever will. This is usually the last option we recommend, not the first, since it adds a mechanical part that needs power and occasional upkeep.

| Option | How It Works | Best For | Watch For |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sump or perimeter tile tie-in | Well drain extends into the existing perimeter drain system feeding the sump pit | Homes that already have a working perimeter drain and sump | Sump pump needs the extra capacity, and the discharge line has to run well away from the foundation |
| Dedicated dry well | A larger gravel-filled pit set apart from the foundation absorbs water on its own | Lots with no accessible sump system, or where sump capacity is already maxed out | Needs enough distance and soil capacity of its own, or it just becomes a second full well |
| Small pump | A pump in or near the well lifts water out faster than gravity alone | A genuinely high water table where gravity options can't keep pace | Needs power, ideally a battery backup, and a discharge point that won't recirculate |

## Tying a Window Well Drain Into Your Sump System

We see the first option play out often around Waverly, where plenty of homes sit on lower ground with heavy clay close to the surface. One 1970s ranch there had a window well that stayed half full through most of April and May, year after year. It already had a drain pipe. The pipe just emptied into bare soil a few feet down instead of connecting to anything. Running that line the rest of the way into the home's existing perimeter drain tile, plus resetting the gravel bed, is the kind of job that lands in the same $600 to $1,500 range as a full window well replacement. A straightforward case like that one usually comes in closer to $1,000.

Where the tie-in lands matters as much as whether it happens at all. If the sump pump's own discharge line dumps water at the base of the same foundation wall, or even just a few feet away on a lot that's already flat and wet, you can end up feeding the same water right back toward the house. A discharge line on a high water table lot needs to travel far enough away, and downhill enough, that the water actually leaves instead of circling back.

## A Dry Well or a Small Pump When a Sump Tie-In Isn't Enough

Not every lot has a sump system to tie into, and not every sump system has room for one more source of water.

A split-level in Hickman was a different story: no sump anywhere near the well, and an older foundation with no interior drain tile at all. Rather than force a connection that didn't exist, we set a dedicated dry well several feet from the foundation and let it take the well's water instead. A standalone dry well like that, gravel pit and connecting line included, falls in the same $600 to $1,500 range as a full window well replacement, and this one landed close to the middle.

A pump comes into the picture when the water table itself, not just the absence of a drain system, is working against you. If a well keeps filling from underneath no matter how well the gravel and drain line are built, more gravel won't fix that. A small pump set to kick on before the water reaches window sill height is the honest answer at that point, even though it's the option with the most moving parts to maintain.

## Why This Matters More Once You Cut a New Egress Opening

A new egress opening does something a repair doesn't: it creates a new low point in a foundation wall that didn't have one before. Before the cut, that section of wall was just concrete or block holding back soil. After the cut, there's a window, a well, and a drain, all sitting lower than the surrounding grade.

On a normal lot, that's not a big deal as long as the drainage is designed correctly from the start. On a lot with a high water table, it's the difference between an opening that stays dry for the life of the house and one that becomes the wettest spot on the property. The well doesn't create the water table. It just gives water that's already in the ground a new, closer path toward your basement if the drainage underneath isn't built to handle it on day one.

## What a Contractor Should Do at Install Time, and Where Our Scope Ends

On a wet lot, the install details matter more than they do anywhere else. A few things worth asking any contractor before they start digging.

**Gravel depth.** A wet lot needs more gravel depth under and around the well than a dry lot does, so the well has somewhere to hold water briefly instead of backing straight up against the glass. Relying on the same shallow bed you'd use on dry ground is a shortcut that shows up again the next wet spring.

**The drain tie-in itself.** The well's drain should physically connect into the perimeter tile or dry well, not just aim a pipe toward nearby soil and hope for the best. That connection needs to happen before backfilling and landscaping close it up, not get bolted on afterward as an afterthought.

**Confirming the discharge point.** Before the job is called done, someone should walk the yard and confirm the sump or pump discharge actually carries water away and downhill, not back toward the foundation. On a flat, wet lot, "downhill" might only be a few feet of difference, and getting it wrong undoes the rest of the work.

Here's where our scope stops and a waterproofing contractor's starts. The window well itself, the window, and the well's own drain line down to where it ties in are our work. What happens on the other side of that tie-in, the interior sump pit, the perimeter drain tile running the rest of the way around your footing, and the sump pump itself, belongs to a waterproofing contractor. If your existing sump pump is old, undersized, or already maxed out by other water in the basement, replacing it is their job, and we'll tell you plainly when that's what we're looking at.

Any work that means cutting into finished walls or a slab to trace or replace drain tile, or wiring in a pump or sump system, is a job for a licensed waterproofing or electrical contractor, whether the water table is the cause or not. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why does a window well flood on a lot with a high water table?

Because the soil around and under the well is already saturated, gravity drainage has nowhere left to send new water. Heavy clay makes the same problem worse even where the water table itself isn't especially high, since clay absorbs water slowly under any conditions. The result is a well that stays wet for days instead of draining within hours.

### Can a window well drain always tie into an existing sump system?

Not always. It depends on whether a perimeter drain system already runs near the well and whether the sump pump has capacity left for another source of water. Where that connection isn't practical, a dedicated dry well is usually the next honest option.

### Is a pump the best fix for a window well on a wet lot?

Not usually first. A pump becomes the right answer when the water table itself is working against gravity, not just when the site lacks a drain tie-in or dry well option. It's the option with the most parts to maintain, so we recommend it last, not as a starting point.
