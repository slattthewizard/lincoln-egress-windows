## Why a Window Well Cover Snow Load Problem Doesn't Show Up Until February

Most covers go on in the fall, sit through the first light snows without issue, and get forgotten. That's normal. A dusting of snow on a flat cover is nothing.

The trouble starts once storms stack up. Southeast Nebraska rarely gets one clean snow that melts off before the next one arrives. Instead, a few inches from one system sit on top of a cover, get topped off by the next, and settle into a denser layer than either storm produced on its own.

By the time a cover is carrying two or three storms' worth of settled, wind-packed snow, plus whatever has drifted into the well itself, it's under a very different window well cover snow load than the one it handled comfortably in November. A cover that was fine for light coverage was never tested against that kind of buildup, and a lot of covers on Lincoln-area homes are older than anyone realizes.

## How Drifting Snow Fills a Window Well Before You Ever See It on the Cover

Wind is the part people underestimate. A window well sits below grade, right next to the foundation, and that little pocket is exactly where blowing snow wants to land. Snow that never even sticks to the open yard drifts straight into the well and piles up against the wall and the underside of the cover.

A cover that sits flush with the ground, without a lip or a raised edge, makes this worse. Drift blows right up to the seam and packs in tight, instead of sliding past the way it would off a cover with some pitch or clearance. Once that drift fills a well halfway, it isn't resting only on the ground below. Some of that weight is pressing against the cover from the side and from underneath as it compacts.

A well on the north or west side of a house, where prevailing winter wind hits directly, will fill faster than one tucked against a sheltered wall. If you've noticed one particular well always has the deepest drift after a storm, that's not bad luck. It's wind direction, and it happens the same way every year.

## How a Freeze-Thaw Cycle Turns Snow Load Into Ice on Your Cover

Fresh snow is manageable. Ice is the problem. Southeast Nebraska's winters don't stay cold in a straight line. A week of freezing temperatures gets interrupted by a warm afternoon, snow on top of the cover starts to melt, and that meltwater runs to the low points and the edges before the temperature drops again overnight.

Nebraska winters swing between thaw and hard freeze all season long, and the National Weather Service tracks it in real time at [weather.gov](https://www.weather.gov). Water that refreezes at the lip of a well, or in the gap between the cover and the frame, doesn't behave like loose snow anymore. It bonds the cover to the well rim, fills in any flex the material had, and adds a hard, brittle layer on top of whatever snow was already sitting there.

A cover that could flex a little under fresh snow can't flex at all once ice has locked it down. The next storm's weight, or even just foot traffic nearby, is what finally cracks it. That's why so many covers fail in late January and February instead of during the first big snow of the season. It isn't one storm. It's weeks of freeze-thaw cycling that used up whatever give the material had. ## Flat Sheets, Wide Wells, and Why a Support Bar or Dome Beats a Flat Sheet

A flat sheet of cover material spans the width of a well with nothing holding up the middle. On a narrow well, that's rarely a problem. On a wide well, especially one built under a bay window or an oversized egress opening, that same flat sheet is carrying its load across a much longer unsupported span.

Think of it like a shelf. A short one holds a stack of books without sagging. Stretch that same shelf twice as long and it bows in the middle under the identical weight, because the span is doing more work with no more material to resist it. A window well cover behaves the same way once snow and ice pile on.

So a domed or arched cover holds up better on a wide well than a flat one of the same material. The curve carries load out toward the edges and the frame instead of concentrating it in the center, the same principle that makes an arch stronger than a flat beam. A support bar across the middle of a flat cover does something similar by breaking one long span into two shorter ones.

## The Real Danger: A Snow-Locked Cover Can Trap Someone in a Basement Bedroom

Everything above is about property damage and repair cost. This part is about safety, and it matters more.

An egress window exists so someone can get out of a basement bedroom without help. That only works if the cover over the well opens from the inside, without a key, a tool, or any special knowledge, exactly when someone needs it most. A cover that's frozen shut, weighted down with a foot of packed snow, or iced into its frame defeats that purpose completely.

This isn't a rare edge case. Every egress well on every home collects snow the same way each winter, and a cover that looks fine from the yard can still be locked solid from the inside. If a fire or another emergency happens during or right after a storm, a resident in that basement bedroom needs the cover to give way immediately, not after someone outside has dug it out.

If you have a basement bedroom with an egress window, treat a snow-covered or iced-over well cover as a safety issue, not a maintenance item to get to eventually. A cover that can't be pushed open from inside isn't doing its job, no matter how new or expensive it is.

## How to Clear Snow and Ice From a Window Well Safely

Clearing a window well after a storm is usually a homeowner job, as long as you're careful about how you do it.

- Clear the top of the cover first with a broom or a soft-bristled tool rather than a metal shovel, which can gouge or crack the cover.
- Check for ice buildup at the seam between the cover and the well rim. A heat-safe de-icer made for walkways can loosen it without damaging the cover.
- Never chip at ice on the cover with a hard tool. That's exactly the kind of impact that turns a frozen but intact cover into a cracked one.
- After clearing the top, open the cover from inside if you can safely do so, to confirm it still releases the way it's supposed to. If it's stuck, that's the sign to call for service before the next storm.
- Keep an eye on drifting spots. If one well fills faster than others after every storm, plan to clear it first each time.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much snow does it take to crack a window well cover in Nebraska?

There's no single amount that applies to every cover. What matters more is repetition: several storms stacking up without fully melting off, combined with freeze-thaw cycles that turn that snow into ice at the seams. A cover that could handle one heavy storm often can't handle the same snow after it has frozen, thawed, and refrozen several times.

### Can I put a tarp or plywood over my window well cover to protect it during a storm?

That's not a good idea on an egress well. Adding another layer on top of the cover makes it heavier and can freeze into a solid mass that's even harder to clear, and it risks blocking the cover from releasing from inside in an emergency. A properly fitted, well-supported cover sized for your well handles snow load better than any add-on layer.

### Which type of window well cover holds up best against heavy snow?

It depends on your well's width, how much foot traffic the yard sees, and your budget. Our [window well cover types guide](/blog/window-well-cover-types/) breaks down the full comparison. For snow load specifically, a domed or supported cover generally outperforms a flat sheet of the same material once a well gets wider than a standard size.

### Can a frozen window well cover really trap someone in a basement bedroom?

Yes, and that's the most serious risk of letting snow and ice build up unchecked. A cover has to release from inside without a key or a tool to meet egress requirements, and ice or packed snow can defeat that even on a cover that looks fine from outside. Clear egress wells after every storm and confirm the cover still opens freely from inside.
