## Window Well Drainage Starts With the Yard, Not the Drain

A window well's drain only has to move the water that reaches it. If the ground around the well keeps sending more water than that drain was built to handle, the drain looks broken even though it isn't.

Nebraska's clay-heavy soil makes this worse than it would be somewhere sandier. Clay drains slowly, so water sits on the surface and runs downhill toward whatever low point it finds, often a window well. [Nebraska Extension](https://extension.unl.edu) publishes general guidance on managing drainage in soils like this, and the short version matches what contractors here already do: keep water moving away from the house before it gets anywhere near a well.

Six things send water toward a well often enough that we see them on nearly every service call: bad grading, close downspouts, raised flower beds, mulch and edging, AC condensate lines, and hardscape pitched the wrong way. Fix all six, and most wells stop flooding without anyone touching the well itself.

## Grading and Downspouts: Get the Two Basics Right First

Grading is the shape of the ground, and it's the single biggest factor in whether a window well floods. The rule of thumb: the ground should drop roughly six inches over the first six to ten feet moving away from the house, or about an inch for every foot, not stay flat and not tip back toward the foundation.

Over the years, soil settles, mulch beds get built up, and mowing wears grass down to bare dirt near the foundation. A yard that used to slope away can end up flat. Or even tipped back toward the house, aiming water straight at the nearest window well. Fixing this usually means adding and compacting soil against the foundation so the slope falls away again, then reseeding or laying sod over it.

A homeowner in Waverly with a 1970s ranch might spend a weekend regrading a flower bed that's been holding water against the foundation for years. Sometimes that's the whole fix. If the well still fills up after that, the well itself is usually the reason, and a straightforward replacement runs $600 to $1,500 depending on size.

Downspouts matter just as much. A gutter can move a few hundred gallons off a roof in one hard storm, and if the downspout empties within a few feet of a window well, that water goes straight into the well's gravel bed no matter how good the drain is. Extensions should carry water at least four to six feet clear of the well, ending on ground that already slopes away, with a splash block to keep the discharge from digging a trench.

An owner in Seward could add a downspout extension after noticing the well floods every hard rain, only to find it keeps filling anyway. That usually means the well itself has no working outlet for the water that still reaches it, and replacing it typically costs $600 to $1,500. ## Flower Beds, Mulch, and Edging That Trap Water Against the House

Flower beds built up against the foundation look nice. But soil piled above a window well's lip works against you: instead of shedding water away, it holds water right at the top edge of the well, where it seeps in.

Edging causes the same problem in a different shape. Plastic or metal edging installed to keep mulch in place can act like a small dam, holding water against the house until it finds the well as the lowest point nearby.

The fix is usually just regrading the bed itself: keep the soil level below the well's lip, and angle any edging so water can pass by instead of pooling against it. Pulling mulch back a few inches from the well opening helps too, since soaked mulch against the wall keeps the ground next to it wetter longer.

## The AC Condensate Line Nobody Thinks About

Central air conditioners produce a surprising amount of water on a humid Nebraska afternoon, and that water has to drain somewhere. A lot of installers run the condensate line to the nearest convenient spot near the foundation, which is sometimes just a few feet from a window well.

That's a slow, steady source of water all summer long, not just during storms. It's easy to miss because it doesn't show up as a sudden flood, just a well that always seems a little damp even on dry weeks.

The fix is simple once you spot it: reroute the condensate line's discharge point away from the well, or extend it the same way you'd extend a downspout. This is a quick job for an HVAC tech or a handy homeowner, not something that needs the well touched at all.

## Driveways and Patios Pitched the Wrong Way

Concrete and pavers move water faster than soil, so a driveway or patio pitched even slightly toward the house can send a lot of runoff at a window well in a hurry. This is easy to miss because the surface can look flat to the eye.

Near Beatrice, a patio that's settled just enough to tip back toward the house can send a sheet of water at a window well every time it rains hard. If that constant soaking has already rotted the sill, a basement window swap runs $400 to $1,200, on top of whatever the patio fix costs.

Fixing pitched hardscape is bigger than a weekend project. It usually means a concrete contractor regrading or replacing the slab, or adding a channel drain along the edge to catch runoff before it reaches the well. Either way, it's outside work, done by a landscaper or concrete crew, not by us.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How far should a downspout discharge from a window well?

Aim for at least four to six feet of clearance, and further if the ground between the downspout and the well is flat. The extension should end on ground that's sloped away from the house, not on flat soil where water can sit and soak in. A splash block at the end helps keep the discharge point from eroding.

### Can regrading alone stop window well flooding?

Often, yes, especially when the well's drain and gravel bed are in decent shape. Regrading fixes the most common cause, which is water running toward the well instead of away from the house. If flooding continues after the yard is graded correctly and downspouts are extended, the well itself likely needs a look.

### What if my window well floods even after I fix the yard?

That points to a well problem rather than a yard problem: an undersized well, a wall that's bowed or pulled away from the foundation, or a drain that no longer empties anywhere useful. These aren't landscaping fixes. A replacement well typically runs $600 to $1,500 depending on size and depth.

### Does landscaping mulch actually cause window well flooding?

It can, mainly through the edging that holds it in place rather than the mulch itself. Edging installed to keep mulch neat can act like a small dam, holding water against the foundation until it finds the well as the lowest nearby point. Pulling mulch back from the well and angling edging so water can pass by usually solves it.
