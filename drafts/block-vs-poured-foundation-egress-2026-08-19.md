# Cutting an Egress Window in a Block vs Poured Foundation

Your basement wall is either poured concrete or concrete block, and which one you have changes how an egress opening gets cut, what has to go above it, and how much of the job is structural engineering versus straightforward sawing.

Both are routine. Neither is a reason to abandon the project. But block walls are less forgiving of a bad cut, and it's worth knowing which you have and what the right approach looks like before you compare quotes, because this is one of the places where a cheap bid means a corner was cut.

The important part is not which you have. It's whether whoever cuts it treats the wall above the opening as a load that has to go somewhere.

## Why the Wall Type Matters

A basement wall is holding back soil, and it's carrying the house above it. Cutting a hole roughly three feet wide into it removes a section of both functions, and the load that section was carrying has to be redirected around the new opening.

**Poured concrete** behaves as one continuous element. It has tensile strength from its reinforcement and it can bridge modest spans on its own. When you cut a clean opening in a poured wall, the surrounding concrete tends to redistribute load reasonably well, and a properly sized header above the opening handles the rest. The cut edges stay where you put them.

**Concrete block** is a stack of individual masonry units held together by mortar. Its strength in compression is good; its ability to span an opening on its own is essentially zero. Remove a few courses and everything above them has nothing to sit on unless a lintel is installed to carry that load across the opening to the block on either side.

That's the whole difference in practice. In a block wall, the lintel is not a detail. It is the reason the wall above the window stays where it is.

## What a Proper Block Cut Involves

**Assessing the wall first.** Block condition, mortar joint condition, whether cores are grouted, whether there's existing reinforcement, and whether the wall shows any pre-existing movement. A wall that's already [bowing](/blog/bowing-window-well-wall/) or cracking is a different conversation before anyone talks about cutting it.

**Sizing and installing a lintel.** A steel angle or precast lintel spans the opening and bears on sound block at both ends, with adequate bearing length on each side. This is engineering, not eyeballing, and it should be part of the permitted plan.

**Cutting cleanly.** Block is cut course by course rather than as one removed slab, and clean cuts matter because ragged edges leave units without full bearing.

**Handling grouted cores and rebar.** Some block walls have grouted, reinforced cores. Hitting one is normal and manageable, but it changes the cut.

**Sealing and flashing.** Block is more porous than poured concrete and the cut edges expose cores. Waterproofing the opening properly is what keeps you from chasing a leak later.

## What a Poured Cut Involves

Simpler, but not trivial.

**Rebar.** Poured walls contain reinforcement, and the saw will find it. Cutting rebar is expected; what matters is that the opening is sized and located so that severing reinforcement doesn't compromise the wall, and that the header above is designed for the actual load.

**Wall thickness.** Thicker walls are slower cutting and heavier sections to remove, which affects time more than method.

**Removing the section.** A cut section of poured wall is heavy. Getting it out of the hole safely requires equipment and planning, and it's one reason the exterior excavation gets done first.

**Clean, square edges.** A well-cut poured opening gives the window a true frame to seat into, which makes the seal easier and better.

## Which One Does Your Lincoln Home Have?

**Older homes**, particularly in Lincoln's established neighborhoods, are frequently concrete block. Look at an unfinished section of basement wall: if you can see a grid of rectangular units with mortar joints between them, that's block. Painted block still shows the joint pattern under the paint.

**Newer construction** is usually poured. A poured wall reads as one continuous surface, often with faint horizontal form lines and occasional tie holes in a regular pattern.

If the basement is finished and you can't see the wall, check the utility room, behind the water heater, or inside a crawl space. Failing that, the exterior above grade often reveals the material at the top of the foundation.

## Signs the Cut Was Done Badly

Whether you're evaluating past work or watching your own job, these are the things that indicate the load above the opening wasn't handled:

- Stair-step cracking in mortar joints above or beside the opening in a block wall
- A horizontal crack running along a mortar joint above the window
- The window frame out of square, or the window binding and getting [stuck](/blog/stuck-egress-window/)
- Visible sag in the courses directly above the opening
- Drywall cracking upstairs above that wall section
- Daylight or draft at the perimeter of the frame
- No visible lintel above the opening in an exposed block wall

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can you cut an egress window into a concrete block foundation?

Yes, routinely. The requirement that makes it safe is a properly sized lintel above the opening, bearing on sound block at both ends, to carry the load that the removed courses were supporting. Block cannot span an opening on its own, so the lintel is structural, not optional.

### Is it harder to cut an egress window in poured concrete or block?

Poured concrete is harder to cut physically, because it's continuous and reinforced. Block is easier to cut but less forgiving structurally, since everything above the opening needs a lintel to carry it. Block also tends to require more care with waterproofing because cut cores are exposed.

### Will cutting an egress window weaken my foundation?

Not when it's done correctly. The opening is engineered with a header or lintel that redirects the load around it, which is standard practice and what the permit inspection verifies. Done without that, in a block wall especially, cracking and settlement above the opening are real possibilities.

### How do I know if my basement is block or poured?

Look at an unfinished stretch of wall, such as in the utility room. Block shows a grid of rectangular units with mortar joints, visible even under paint. Poured concrete reads as one continuous surface, often with faint horizontal form lines and small regularly spaced tie holes.

### Does a block foundation cost more for an egress window?

Usually somewhat, because of the lintel and the slower, more careful removal, but it stays within the same **$3,500 to $7,000** cut-in range. Excavation depth, exterior access, and whether the basement is already finished all move the price more than wall type does.
