# Egress Window Blinds That Keep a Basement Exit Clear

An egress window has one job that matters more than privacy or light: it has to be a way out. Anything you hang over it can get in the way of that job. The wrong blind turns a code-compliant opening into a window you have to fight your way through in the dark.

Choosing egress window blinds is mostly a question of what happens in the first ten seconds of an emergency. Can someone half asleep push the covering aside with one hand and climb out? If the answer involves unhooking, unscrewing, or cutting anything, the treatment is wrong for this window.

## What the window has to do first

A basement bedroom needs an escape opening that works for a person, not just on paper. The IRC sets the numbers: 5.7 square feet of net clear opening (5.0 at grade floor), at least 24 inches of clear height, at least 20 inches of clear width, and a sill no higher than 44 inches above the floor. The window must also open from the inside without keys, tools, or special knowledge. The full breakdown is in [IRC egress window code requirements](/blog/irc-egress-window-code-requirements/).

That last rule is the one blinds run into. A covering that has to be removed, unlatched, or untied before the sash can swing is a tool-and-knowledge requirement in practice, even if the window itself is fine. Net clear opening is also measured with the sash open, so anything that hangs into the opening, such as a thick valance or a bulky shade stack, shrinks the space a person has to climb through.

So the test for any treatment is simple: with the window open, is the opening still clear, and did opening it take nothing but the window's own handle or crank?

## Three ways a covering can block escape

**It sits in the opening.** Casement windows swing outward on a crank, and the sash and crank handle sit near the lower inside corner. A blind mounted inside the frame can press against the crank or stop the handle from turning fully. Egress casements often need the handle turned a long way, so a few extra inches of clearance matter.

**It has to come down.** Anything held up with screws, adhesive film that has to be peeled, a rigid insulating panel pressed into the frame, or a board cut to fit is a treatment someone has to remove first. In a smoke-filled room that is the same as a blocked window.

**It snags on the way through.** Long curtains, loose cords, and wide valances catch clothing and limbs. A person climbing out a window sill 40 inches up is already working hard. They should not also be untangling fabric.

## Window types and what fits each

How the sash opens decides what covering works. The three common egress styles are covered in [casement, slider, and inswing egress windows](/blog/casement-slider-inswing-egress-windows/), and each treats a covering differently.

**Casement.** The crank and the swinging sash are the issue. Outside-the-frame mounts work best, because the covering stays clear of the crank. If you want an inside mount, measure from the glass to the crank handle at full travel and make sure the covering's depth stays well short of it. A shade that stacks tight when raised leaves the opening most open.

**Slider.** One half slides sideways. A covering on a track or pulled to one side is easy to push past. Avoid anything that hangs in the sliding path.

**Inswing windows.** The sash swings into the room like a door. Hanging anything across the full opening, or mounting it inside the frame, can stop the sash from opening at all. Keep the area where the sash travels completely free.

## Treatments that tend to work

None of these are a promise for your window, because mounting space and sash travel vary. They are the types that most often leave the opening clear.

**Cordless cellular (honeycomb) shades.** They are slim, stack small when raised, and have no cord. The trapped air layer adds some insulation on cold glass, which helps in a Lincoln basement in January. Pick a cordless model you push or pull by hand.

**Top-down/bottom-up shades.** These let you drop the top edge down for light and privacy while the lower part of the window stays clear. They are handy for ground-level windows where a neighbor or passerby can see in. Make sure the model is cordless, and confirm the shade can be raised or pushed out of the way with one hand.

**Roller shades, cordless or spring-loaded.** A flat, simple roll that retracts fully. Outside-mounted above the frame, a roller leaves the opening fully clear when up.

**Short curtains on a tension or ring rod.** A curtain that stops at the sill and slides fully to one side keeps things simple. Light fabric is better than heavy lined drapes. Pick a curtain that slides or pulls aside with one hand and does not need to be unhooked.

**Frosted privacy film.** Static-cling frosted film stays out of the opening entirely and obscures the view without affecting the sash. It is a good fit for window wells where privacy, not insulation, is the goal.

## Treatments to avoid on an egress window

- **Fixed or semi-permanent covers.** Foam panels, shrink-wrap insulation kits, plywood, or anything screwed to the frame or sealed with tape.
- **Heavy drapes and valances.** Weight hangs into the opening and into the climbing path.
- **Blinds with dangling lift cords.** Beyond the snag risk, cords are a child safety hazard (more below).
- **Hard-mounted decorative grilles or bars.** Anything on the room side that cannot be opened from inside without a key is a code problem on an escape window.
- **Stacked layers.** A blind plus a curtain plus a valance quickly turns a one-hand exit into a two-hand struggle.

Curtains and blinds also do nothing for the outside of the window, so they are not a security measure.

## Cord safety: the part that is not about escape

Egress windows are in bedrooms, and bedrooms hold sleeping kids. The U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission says that [cordless window coverings are the only option that eliminates the strangulation hazard](https://www.cpsc.gov/Safety-Education/Safety-Education-Centers/Window-Covering). It also says shortening cords, adding cord stops, and anchoring continuous loops reduce the risk but do not remove it.

CPSC reported 46 child deaths from window covering cord strangulation between 2014 and 2019, an average of about eight children a year, and warns that [a child can strangle in under a minute even when cords are tied high or fitted with cleats](https://www.cpsc.gov/Newsroom/News-Releases/2021/Go-Cordless-with-Window-Coverings-to-Avoid-Risk-of-Strangulation-to-Infants-and-Children). It also says to replace blinds sold before November 2000, since their inner lift cords can form a loop.

For an egress window, that points to a clear answer: buy cordless, or products with inaccessible cords. Cordless also solves the escape problem, because there is nothing to snag. Keep cribs, beds, and furniture away from the window and its cords.

## Measuring so the covering clears the opening

Before you order anything, spend ten minutes with a tape measure.

1. **Open the window all the way.** Note where the crank handle, latch, and sash end up.
2. **Measure the depth of the frame.** Compare it with the mounting depth listed for the covering. Many shades need more depth than a casement offers.
3. **Check the sill-to-floor height.** A 44-inch maximum sill means the window may be higher than you expect. A covering that reaches the floor is too long.
4. **Test the stack.** Raise the covering and see how far into the opening it sits. If it narrows the clear space you measured, pick something slimmer or mount it higher on the wall.
5. **Open and close it with one hand in the dark.** That is the real test.

## Light, condensation, and privacy tradeoffs

Basement windows get less light, so heavy coverings make rooms dark. Top-down shades, sheer curtains, and frosted film hold privacy while letting light through. Condensation on cold glass is another reason some homeowners hang insulating shades. If you see moisture often, check [egress window condensation](/blog/egress-window-condensation/) first, since a covering that traps damp air against the glass can make mold more likely.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I put blinds on an egress window?
Yes, as long as the blind does not block the opening and the window still opens with no keys, tools, or special knowledge. Choose a cordless, slim covering that stacks small, and mount it clear of the crank and sash. Test it by opening the window with one hand.

### Are curtains or blinds better for an egress window?
Short, light curtains on a tension rod or a slim cordless shade both work. Curtains have no lift cords, and they pull aside fast. Blinds with slats or lift cords are the harder choice in an emergency, so go cordless if you choose blinds.

### Are cordless blinds required on a basement bedroom window?
We are not aware of a rule specific to egress windows, but the U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission recommends cordless window coverings for any home with young children. Cordless is also the easier choice for an escape window because nothing can snag.

### Can I use an insulating panel or film on an egress window in winter?
Static-cling film is fine because it does not affect the sash. Rigid foam panels, taped plastic kits, and anything that has to be peeled or pulled off before the window opens defeat the purpose of the window. Cellular shades are a better way to cut heat loss.

### Does a window covering change whether my window meets code?
The code measures the window's net clear opening and how it operates. A covering that hangs into the opening or makes the window harder to open can still defeat the purpose of the opening, so mount it clear of the sash and keep it easy to move aside.
