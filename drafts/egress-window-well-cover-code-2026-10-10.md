# Egress Window Well Cover Code: What a Legal Cover Has to Do

An egress window well cover has one job the code cares about more than keeping out leaves: it must not trap anyone who is trying to get out. If a fire starts upstairs and the only way out of a basement bedroom is through the window, a cover that will not move turns a legal exit into a dead end.

That is why the cover is part of the egress system, not an accessory. If you are shopping for one, or wondering whether the one on your well is legal, the rule is short. The hard part is knowing which products actually follow it.

## What the code says about covers

The IRC section on emergency escape and rescue openings (R310) covers bars, grilles, covers and screens placed over an escape opening, an area well or a window well. [The IRC text](https://up.codes/viewer/nebraska/irc-2018/chapter/3/building-planning#R310.4.3) says two things about them.

First, the opening has to keep its minimum net clear size even with the device in place. A cover cannot shrink the opening below what the code requires.

Second, the device must be releasable or removable from the inside without a key, a tool, or special knowledge, and it cannot take more force than the normal operation of the window itself.

Read that second sentence slowly, because each phrase rules something out:

- **No key.** A padlock, a keyed lock or any lock that needs a key from inside is out.
- **No tool.** Bolts, lag screws, wing nuts that need pliers, or anything that calls for a screwdriver are out.
- **No special knowledge.** A hidden latch, a two-step release, or a trick that only the person who installed it knows is out. A scared teenager at 2 a.m. should be able to work it out.
- **No more force than a normal window.** A cover you have to shoulder up with all your weight does not meet this, and neither does one that is loaded down with snow, ice or stored items.

The code text sets the standard, not the product label. A cover can be sold as "egress compliant" and still fail on your well if the installation changes how it works. We cannot tell you how a particular inspector will read a particular cover, only what the code asks for.

## Which cover types fit the rule

Most legal covers share one trait: you can see how they open, and the opening takes one motion.

**Hinged covers.** A hinged window well cover swings up from the inside. It is the most common answer for egress wells because the person inside pushes up and the lid rises, with no parts to remove. The things to check are the hinge side, the latch, and how much force the lid needs. A latch that can be thrown from inside with a hand is fine. A latch that only works from outside is not.

**Sloped polycarbonate or clear domes.** These shed water and let light in. Many are made to sit in place or lift off from inside. Whether one qualifies depends on its weight and how it is held down. A light dome that is simply set on the well curb and can be pushed up from below is a different thing from one that has been screwed to the foundation.

**Light metal grates with a release latch.** A grate can work if it is light enough to lift and has a latch operated from inside. The weight matters more than the material.

**Plastic or polycarbonate lids with spring or quick-release hardware.** These exist, and a spring-assisted lid makes the force requirement easier to meet for a smaller person. The wording on the packaging is not the test. Your hands on the lid are.

## Which covers do not meet the rule

These are the ones that fail the "from inside, no key, no tool" test, or are very likely to:

- **Covers bolted or screwed to the foundation or well wall.** If a drill or wrench is the only way off, it needs a tool. Some people fasten a cover down because of wind or because they worry about theft. The code does not allow that trade on an egress well.
- **Padlocked or key-locked covers.** The padlock is on the outside in most cases, so the person inside cannot do anything about it.
- **Heavy steel grates or diamond-plate lids with no assist.** A grate that takes two adults to lift is a weight problem, and the weight is what you are measuring. Heavy grates are common over non-egress wells, where nobody is expected to climb out. Do not move one to an egress well without checking how it comes off.
- **Covers with the latch on the outside only.** If the release is on the wrong side, it does not count.
- **Homemade plywood or lumber lids.** They rot, swell and warp in Lincoln's freeze-thaw cycle, and they are hard to lift once they do. They also often get screwed down.
- **A cover buried under snow or stored items.** The cover itself may be legal, but a pile of snow, a drift pushed off a shoveled driveway, or a planter parked on it means it will not move. This part is on the homeowner, not the product.

## The ladder and the cover work as a pair

Depth changes the picture. Wells deeper than 44 inches need a permanently affixed ladder or steps that can be used with the window fully open. A cover that sits low can block the ladder or force a climber to push up at an awkward angle.

That makes depth part of the buying decision. Say your well is 48 inches deep. A person standing on the ladder reaches the cover with arms bent, not extended. The release needs to be where that person's hands will be. A latch that only works with arms fully overhead is a poor fit for a short climber.

## What to check before you buy

Do this before you pay, and again after the cover is on.

1. **Measure the well.** Cover sizes are matched to width, projection and curb shape. A cover that does not sit square on the curb gaps, rocks or binds. [How to measure for a window well cover](/blog/how-to-measure-window-well-cover/) shows where to take each number.
2. **Find out how it opens.** Ask the seller where the release is, whether you can reach it from inside the well, and whether it needs a tool or key. If the answer is "you unscrew it," you know.
3. **Ask the weight.** Compare it with the weight you can lift from a ladder rung. If the seller will not state a weight, treat that as a warning.
4. **Check that the clear opening survives.** The cover cannot reduce the net clear opening below the code minimum. That includes any bars, mesh or crossbars inside the cover.
5. **Test it in winter conditions.** Frozen hinges, ice on the lid and a snow load are the real tests. A cover that opens in July may stick in January. Snow load is the one that catches people.
6. **Look at the retailer's wording.** A seller that calls its kits IRC-compliant, as [Window Well Supply does for its egress kits](https://windowwellsupply.com/product-category/egress-kits/), is telling you the product is meant for this use. It is still your job to confirm the cover works with your well, not just the catalog page.
7. **Keep kids and pets in mind.** A cover that opens easily from inside should also stay put under a child's weight when it is closed. The two goals pull against each other, and [window well cover safety for kids and pets](/blog/window-well-cover-safety-kids-pets/) walks through how to balance them.

If you are not sure what you have, do a simple home test. Stand in the room with the window, open the window, and ask whether a person your size could get the cover to move from the inside without any tool. That is not an inspection, but it shows you quickly whether the cover is a problem.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does an egress window well cover have to open from the inside?
Yes. The IRC says covers, bars, grilles and screens over an escape opening or window well must be releasable or removable from the inside. It also says that must work without a key, a tool or special knowledge, and with no more force than normal operation of the window. A cover that only opens from outside does not meet that.

### Can I padlock or bolt down my window well cover?
Not on an egress well. A padlock or key lock needs a key, and bolts or screws need a tool, which are the exact things the code rules out. If you worry about wind or theft, ask about a hinged cover with an inside release instead of fastening the lid down.

### Are hinged window well covers always legal?
No. A hinged cover is a common way to meet the rule, but it still has to open from the inside with one hand, without a tool, and without excessive force. Check the latch, the weight and how it behaves with a load of snow on it.

### Do I need a cover on an egress window well at all?
The code section covers what a cover must do if you install one. It does not require one for every well, though a cover is often added to keep out leaves, snow and people. If you add one, it has to follow the release rule.

### Does a cover change the ladder rule?
No. A well deeper than 44 inches still needs a permanently affixed ladder or steps usable with the window fully open. The cover must not block the ladder or keep the window from opening fully.
