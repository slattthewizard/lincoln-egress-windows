# Finishing the Inside of a New Basement Egress Window: Returns, Sills and Trim

When a new egress window goes into a basement wall, you end up with a thick opening. Foundation walls are deep, much deeper than a normal framed wall. The window sits in the middle or toward the outside, and the inside has a bare, deep reveal of concrete or block waiting for a finish.

How you finish that reveal affects how the room looks, how the window performs, and whether the escape opening stays usable. ## The Deep Reveal Problem

A framed wall in a house is about the thickness of a 2x4 plus drywall. A poured or block foundation wall is a lot thicker. After a cut-in, the window frame sits somewhere within that thickness, and the inside has a stretch of raw concrete or block on four sides.

That bare space is called the reveal or the return. You have to decide what to do with it. The main choices:

### Drywall Returns

The most common approach is to wrap drywall into the opening, on the sides and top, so the window looks like it's set in a framed wall. A finisher typically furs out the wall around the opening with framing or strapping, then runs drywall from the room surface into the reveal, with corner bead at the edges.

Pros: it matches the rest of the room, it's inexpensive, and it paints easily. Cons: drywall is vulnerable to moisture, and a basement window can see condensation, so use moisture-tolerant materials near the window, and leave a gap or use a sealant at the bottom edge where water might sit.

### Wood Jamb Extensions

Wood jamb extensions, or jamb extension boards, are strips that extend the window frame to the face of the finished wall. They give a clean look with a stained or painted finish. They do well in a dry room but may swell and rot if exposed to repeated moisture, so below-grade installations often call for a sealed or primed wood or a different material.

### PVC or Composite Jamb Extensions and Trim

PVC and composite boards don't absorb water. They're a popular choice below grade for that reason. They paint well, they don't rot, and they're easy to cut. If you're worried about moisture, which in a basement is reasonable, they're worth a look.

### The Sill (Stool)

The bottom of the reveal can take a stool, which is a shelf-like piece set at the bottom of the window. Options:

- **Wood,** sealed on all sides. It looks good but needs care.
- **PVC or composite,** which is the low-maintenance pick.
- **Tile,** which handles water well and works with a tiled basement bath or bar.
- **Solid-surface or stone-type material,** which is easy to wipe down and doesn't absorb moisture.

A sill shouldn't be a place where things pile up. A deep ledge becomes a shelf for plants, books and a phone charger, and every one of those things is something to push aside on the way out the window. Keep it smooth and slightly sloped to shed condensation, and keep it clear.

## Insulating the Gap Around the Frame

Between the window frame and the foundation edge there's a gap. If it isn't filled, you get drafts, moisture and cold spots. This is part of setting the window, and we seal and insulate this gap as part of our scope of work.

In a Lincoln winter, this matters. Cold concrete and warm room air meet at the window, and that's where condensation forms. A well-sealed, well-insulated frame helps keep that cold from reaching the interior surface. If your finisher is wrapping the reveal in drywall or jamb extensions, they should leave the sealed and insulated joint alone and not pack it with something that holds water.

If your window sits deep in the wall, ask the finisher about insulating the foundation behind the returns. How that's done is outside our scope, and it depends on the wall and your overall basement plan.

## Moisture-Tolerant Materials Below Grade

A basement window sits near the ground. It sees condensation, and sometimes a little water coming in around the frame if something fails outside. Lincoln clay soil holds water, and snow and freeze-thaw cycles put stress on a well and on the joint at the window.

For the interior around the window, the safest choices are:

- **Moisture-resistant drywall,** or cement board where it's close to water.
- **PVC or composite trim,** which won't swell.
- **Sealed or primed edges** on anything that's wood, including the back and cut ends.
- **Caulk and sealant** at joints, with materials rated for damp locations.
- **A drain at the well,** so water doesn't sit against the window. See our [window well installation](/window-well-installation-lincoln/) page.

If you see staining, soft trim or a musty smell around an older basement window, that can mean a leak or rot in the frame. We repair moisture and rot around basement window frames. The foundation and waterproofing questions behind a leak are a separate trade, and we don't do those.

## Don't Block the Opening

This is the part that matters most. An egress window is an escape route first and a decorated window second. Whatever you do on the inside has to leave the full opening usable.

Remember what the opening has to be: a net clear opening of at least 5.7 square feet (5.0 for a grade-floor opening), at least 24 inches high and 20 inches wide, with the sill no higher than 44 inches above the floor. Net clear means what's left when the window is fully open. Anything added inside the opening, like a deep stool, thick trim, a returned drywall edge or a window treatment, can take away from it.

Check these, after finishing:

- **Open the window all the way.** Does the sash clear the trim and the sill?
- **Is the sill too thick?** A stool that sticks out can catch a knee or a foot.
- **Are blinds and shades out of the way?** Treatments should lift or pull aside completely. See our guide to [egress window blinds and treatments](/blog/egress-window-blinds-treatments/).
- **Can you get out in the dark?** Try it. Practice getting through the window with the finish in place.
- **Do any grilles, covers or screens release from inside?** They have to open without a key, a tool or special knowledge.

A small bit of trim isn't going to wreck a window, but a lot of small things add up. If you're not sure, measure the clear opening again after the finishers are done. If it dropped below the minimums, fix it before the inspection.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do you finish the inside of the window with drywall and trim?

Our work covers cutting the opening, setting the window, and sealing and insulating around the frame. Drywall, painting and finish carpentry are usually done by the homeowner's finisher. We'll spell out the line in your written estimate.

### What's the best material for a basement window sill?

For a damp location, PVC, composite, tile or a solid-surface material are good choices because they don't absorb water. Wood works if it's sealed on every side and kept dry. Whatever you pick, keep the sill clear of things that block the opening.

### Can I install blinds on an egress window?

Yes, but they can't reduce or block the clear opening. Choose a treatment that lifts or pulls fully aside, and operate it with no tools. Our [blinds and treatments article](/blog/egress-window-blinds-treatments/) goes through the options.

### Should I use drywall or PVC around a below-grade window?

Either can work. Drywall matches the room and costs less, but it can suffer from moisture. PVC or composite jamb extensions resist water and are common below grade. Many people use moisture-resistant drywall and PVC trim together.

### Does trim affect whether the window passes inspection?

It can. The net clear opening is measured with the window fully open, so thick trim, a deep sill or a returned edge that sticks into the opening can reduce it. Confirm with Lincoln Building and Safety, and measure after finishing.
