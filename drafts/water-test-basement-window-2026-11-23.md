# How to Water Test a Basement Window After Replacement

A new basement window looks finished the day it goes in. The trim is clean, the glass is clear, and the sash opens. What you can't see is whether it keeps out water when it counts. The first heavy spring storm is a bad time to find out.

A garden-hose water test is the cheap way to find out early. It takes about an hour, one helper if you can get one, and a flashlight. If you want a pro to look at a window that fails the test, call [(402) 261-0485](tel:+14022610485) or [request a free written estimate](/#contact).

## Why Test a Window You Just Paid For

Water gets into a basement window opening three ways, and a new window only fixes one of them. The window unit itself can fail at a corner seal or a weep path. The frame can fail where it meets the foundation, through bad sealant or missing flashing. And the soil and well around the window can fail by holding water against the wall.

A test sorts these apart. If you run it right after the install, any problem is fresh, the installer is easy to reach, and nothing inside the basement has been damaged yet. If you're checking an older replacement, the same steps work.

If water is already coming in during rain and you don't know why, start with [what to do when a basement window leaks in the rain](/blog/basement-window-leaking-rain/). This article is about checking before that happens.

## What You'll Need

- A garden hose with a spray nozzle that has a gentle "shower" or "mist" setting
- A flashlight
- Dry paper towels or a few sheets of newspaper for the sill
- A helper inside the basement, or a phone you can set on the sill with video running
- Dry weather, and a day above freezing

Don't use a pressure washer. A pressure washer forces water into places rain never would and tells you nothing useful. You want to copy a hard rain, not a firehose.

## Before You Start: Dry Everything and Take a Look

Wipe the inside frame and sill dry. Put the paper towels along the sill and the bottom of the frame. Look at the interior trim, the drywall or paneling around the window, and the floor below. Note anything that's already stained or soft so you don't blame the test for it later.

Check that the window is fully closed and latched. Then look at the outside. Is the sealant smooth and continuous? Is soil piled up against the frame, or is there a gap between the frame and the foundation? Take a photo of what you see. It helps if you end up calling the installer.

## The Test: Work From the Bottom Up

Water runs down. That is the whole logic of the test. If you start at the top, water from the upper zones runs down over the lower zones, and you can't tell which one let it in. So start at the lowest point and move up, one zone at a time, and give each zone its own turn.

### Zone 1: The Well and the Soil Line

If the window has a well, start there. Run the hose into the well at a gentle flow and watch how fast it drains down through the gravel. It should soak away without filling. If the well fills up and stands, you have a well problem, not a window problem, and you can stop and read [how window well drainage works](/blog/how-window-well-drainage-works/).

Don't let the well fill to the window. A well that stands full of water can push water past almost any frame. That's a drainage problem to fix, and it would be unfair to blame the window for it.

### Zone 2: The Bottom of the Frame and the Sill

Now direct the hose at the foundation wall just below the window, then at the bottom edge of the frame. Hold it there for a good ten minutes. Your helper watches the interior sill with a flashlight, looking for beads of water, a dark line along the bottom of the frame, or the paper towels going damp.

The bottom of the frame is where most failures show first, because water pools there. If this zone stays dry for ten minutes, that's a good sign.

### Zone 3: The Sides of the Frame

Move the spray up each side of the frame, slowly, from bottom to top. Spend a few minutes on each side. The side joints are where sealant tends to shrink and pull away, and where flashing laps can be wrong.

Your helper looks at the interior side trim, and the corners of the frame where it meets the wall. A dark streak running down the trim means water is getting behind it.

### Zone 4: The Top of the Frame and the Window Unit

Last, spray across the top of the frame and then over the glass and sash. Let water run down the face of the window, as rain would, for several minutes. Watch the inside: the head of the frame, the top corners, and the sash track.

If the window is a slider, also spray along the tracks and around the sash edges. Water that shows up in a track and then drains to the outside through the weep holes is normal. Water that runs onto the interior sill is not.

## What You're Watching For Inside

Look for these, in this order:

1. **Beads of water on the sill or frame**, even small ones.
2. **Dark or wet lines** along a joint between the frame and the wall.
3. **Damp paper towels** where they touch the frame.
4. **Drips or streaks** running down the trim or wall.
5. **A musty smell** after the test, which can mean moisture got somewhere you can't see.

If you find mold or a musty smell, the EPA has basic guidance on moisture and mold at [epa.gov](https://www.epa.gov/mold). Take your time, and give the last zone a few minutes after the hose is off. Some leaks take a bit to work their way through a joint.

## Reading the Results: Which Problem Do You Have?

When something does get wet, where and when it shows up tells you a lot.

**Water appears through the glass area, the corners of the sash, or from inside the window unit.** That points to the window itself: a failed corner seal, a cracked frame, or a defective unit. This is usually a warranty or installer conversation, not a sealant fix.

**Water appears along the edge of the frame where it meets the foundation.** That points to the frame, flashing or sealant. The window may be fine. The joint between the window and the wall is what failed. This is repairable by resealing or redoing the flashing and trim, often without replacing the window.

**Water only appears when the well filled up, or when you ran water into the well.** That points to the well and the soil. The window is working. The water is being held against the wall, usually because the well has no way to drain or the gravel is packed with silt and debris. Clay soil around many Lincoln homes drains slowly, so this is worth taking seriously. The fix is a drainage fix at the well.

**Nothing leaks, but the sill looks damp afterward.** Check for condensation before you worry. A cold window and warm, moist basement air can fog and drip with no leak at all.

## When a Test Passes but You Still Worry

A hose test copies rain, but not every kind of rain. It doesn't copy snow melt pooling for days, or a heavy storm that fills a well overnight. Lincoln gets freeze and thaw cycles and spring storms, and those test a window harder than a hose does. A pass is a good sign. It's not a guarantee. Keep an eye on the window after the first few big rains and look at the well afterward.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long should I run the hose on a basement window?

Plan on about ten minutes for the bottom of the frame and a few minutes for each other zone. Then give it a few minutes after you shut the water off. Some leaks take a bit to show up inside.

### Can I use a pressure washer for the test?

No. A pressure washer pushes water past seals that would hold in real rain, so a fail tells you little. A garden hose on a gentle setting is closer to heavy rain.

### Should I test a new window right after it's installed?

Yes, once the sealant has had time to set. Check the installer's cure time for the products they used. Testing early means any problem is fresh and easy to take up with them.

### The sill is damp but I can't find a leak. What is it?

It might be condensation. Cold glass and moist basement air can leave water on the sill without any outside water getting in. Wipe it dry and see if it comes back on a dry day when you haven't used the hose.

### What if water only gets in when the window well is full?

Then the window is probably doing its job, and the problem is the well. A well that holds standing water against the wall needs better drainage, such as a clear gravel base and a tie-in to the foundation drain.
