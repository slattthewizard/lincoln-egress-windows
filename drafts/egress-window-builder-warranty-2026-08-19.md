## What These Warranties Actually Cover

Newer homes in Nebraska frequently come with two overlapping things people call "the warranty."

**The builder's own warranty**, typically covering workmanship and systems for a shorter period, often a year or two.

**A structural warranty**, frequently administered by a third-party warranty company, running longer, commonly around ten years, and covering major structural defects specifically. This is the one that matters for a foundation question.

Structural warranties are narrower than people assume. They generally cover designated load-bearing elements failing in ways that make the home unsafe or unlivable, not cosmetic cracking or ordinary settlement. And they almost always contain exclusions for damage arising from modifications, alterations, or work performed by others.

That exclusion language is the crux. It usually doesn't say "any modification voids everything." It says damage attributable to a modification isn't covered. Which is different, and much more reasonable.

## What That Means in Practice

If your foundation develops a structural problem five years from now on the opposite side of the house from the egress window, a modification near the west wall isn't a plausible cause of a failure on the east wall, and a well-documented, permitted opening is not a general disqualifier.

If a problem develops **at or near the opening** — cracking above the window, settlement in that wall section, a lintel issue in a [block wall](/blog/block-vs-poured-foundation-egress/) — then the modification is the obvious first suspect, and the warranty administrator will look at it that way. That's the realistic exposure.

Which is exactly why how the work is done matters more than whether it's done:

- A properly engineered header or lintel, sized for the actual load
- A permitted opening with an inspection on record
- A licensed, insured installer, not a weekend project
- Documentation you can produce years later

Done that way, a claim near the opening is a discussion about whether the modification was sound. Done badly or without a permit, it's a much shorter conversation.

## What to Do Before You Cut

**1. Find and read the warranty document.** Not the marketing summary. The actual document, with the exclusions section. Look for language about alterations, modifications, and work by others.

**2. Check for a notification requirement.** Some warranties require that you notify the administrator before modifying covered elements. Missing a notification requirement is an easy, avoidable problem. This is the step people skip.

**3. Contact the builder or administrator in writing.** Describe the planned work: a code-required egress window opening, cut by a licensed contractor, permitted and inspected. Ask specifically how it affects coverage. Keep the reply.

**4. Ask what documentation they want.** Some will want the engineering detail for the header or lintel. Providing it up front is far easier than reconstructing it later.

**5. Confirm the structural approach is engineered.** This is the substance behind all the paperwork. The load above the opening has to be carried, and the method depends on whether your wall is poured or block. Our guide to [block versus poured foundations](/blog/block-vs-poured-foundation-egress/) covers the difference and why block is less forgiving.

**6. Pull the permit.** In Lincoln that's **$75 to $200**, covered in [Lincoln and Lancaster County egress permits](/blog/lincoln-lancaster-county-egress-permit/). The permit inspection is independent verification that the structural work was done correctly, which is exactly what you'd want in hand if a claim ever comes up. Outside Lincoln, [permits in the surrounding communities](/blog/egress-permits-outside-lincoln/) covers who issues yours.

**7. Photograph everything.** The opening before the window goes in, the header or lintel in place, the well and drainage before backfill. Five minutes with a phone.

## Don't Let the Question Talk You Out of the Window

Worth stating plainly, because the warranty concern occasionally becomes a reason to do nothing, or worse, to finish a basement bedroom without the window.

A structural warranty covers a financial risk to your house. An egress window addresses whether someone sleeping in that basement can get out during a fire. Those aren't comparable stakes, and the second one doesn't wait for the first to be resolved.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does cutting an egress window void a builder's structural warranty?

Usually it doesn't void the whole warranty. Structural warranties typically exclude damage arising from modifications rather than terminating coverage entirely, so the realistic exposure is a future claim at or near the new opening being attributed to the modification. Permitted, engineered, professionally installed work is what protects you.

### Should I tell my builder before adding an egress window?

Yes, in writing, and check the warranty document for a notification requirement first. Describe the work, note that it will be permitted and inspected, ask how it affects coverage, and keep the reply. Missing a required notification is an avoidable problem.

### What documentation should I keep?

The permit and final inspection, the engineering detail for the header or lintel, the contractor's invoice and insurance certificate, the window's specifications and [warranty paperwork](/blog/egress-window-warranty-questions/), and photographs of the opening and structural work before it was covered up.

### Is it safer to wait until the structural warranty expires?

Rarely a good trade. You'd be leaving a basement bedroom without a legal escape route for years to protect coverage that generally survives a properly documented modification anyway. If a bedroom is in use down there, the safety issue is the live one.

### Does a permitted egress window help if I file a warranty claim later?

It helps considerably. A permit and inspection are independent verification that the opening was engineered and built correctly, which is exactly the question a warranty administrator would raise about a modification near the area of a claim.
