# How to Measure an Egress Window and Check It Against Code

You can tell a lot about a basement window with a tape measure, a pencil and about fifteen minutes. You don't need special tools. You do need to measure the right thing, and the right thing isn't the frame.

This walkthrough shows you how to measure an egress window the way an inspector would look at it: the clear opening on the open sash, the sill height, and the window well outside. ## What You're Checking Against

These are the 2018 IRC numbers, which Nebraska and Lincoln use. Confirm with Lincoln Building and Safety for your address, since the city can apply things its own way.

- **Net clear opening:** at least 5.7 square feet (5.0 for a grade-floor opening)
- **Clear height:** at least 24 inches
- **Clear width:** at least 20 inches
- **Sill height:** no more than 44 inches above the finished floor
- **Window well (if below grade):** at least 9 square feet of horizontal area, at least 36 inches of projection and width, and the window must be able to fully open

All of this is explained in [IRC egress window code requirements](/blog/irc-egress-window-code-requirements/). Here, we'll measure.

## What You Need

- A tape measure (a metal one, not a cloth one)
- Paper and a pencil, or your phone's notes
- A flashlight
- A step stool, if the sill is high
- A second person if the well is deep, so you're not alone

## Step 1: Open the Window All the Way

Before measuring anything, open the window as far as it goes. Casement: crank it until it stops. Slider: push the sash fully to one side. Hung window: lift the sash as high as it will go. If it sticks, try the lock and any stops first. If it won't open at all, that's your first finding: a window that won't open can't serve as an escape opening. A stuck window is also common in old basements, and it's fixable.

Don't measure a half-open window. The number you want is the biggest clear space the window gives you.

## Step 2: Measure the Clear Width

Clear width is the horizontal space you could pass through. Put the tape across the opening at its narrowest point. Measure from the inside edge of the open sash or frame on one side to the nearest obstruction on the other side.

- **Casement:** from the hinge-side frame to the edge of the open sash, or to the opposite frame, whichever leaves less room. The hinge hardware and the sash edge sit in the opening and use up some space.
- **Slider:** from the edge of the sash that slid over to the far frame. Not the full frame width. A slider only opens about half.
- **Hung window:** the width between the side stops or tracks.

Write it down. You need at least 20 inches.

## Step 3: Measure the Clear Height

Clear height is the vertical space. Measure from the top of the sill (the bottom of the open space) up to the lowest part of whatever is above: the head of the frame, or the bottom of the raised sash on a hung window.

- **Casement:** from the sill to the underside of the head, less anything that hangs into the space.
- **Slider:** from the sill or track up to the head of the frame.
- **Single or double hung:** from the sill to the bottom of the sash you've lifted. This is about half the frame height, so it's often where hung windows fail.

Write it down. You need at least 24 inches.

## Step 4: Do the Math

Multiply clear width by clear height, then divide by 144.

**Area in square feet = clear width x clear height / 144**

You want 5.7 or more (or 5.0 at a grade-floor opening). Some examples with made-up numbers:

- 30 inches wide by 28 inches high: 840 / 144 = 5.83 square feet. Passes.
- 24 inches wide by 30 inches high: 720 / 144 = 5.0 square feet. Passes only at a grade-floor opening, fails at 5.7.
- 36 inches wide by 22 inches high: 792 / 144 = 5.5 square feet. Fails on area and also fails the 24 inch height minimum.

A window has to pass three tests at once: the area, the height and the width. Passing one doesn't carry the others. For the rules behind these numbers, see [egress window size requirements in Nebraska](/blog/egress-window-size-requirements-nebraska/).

## Step 5: Measure the Sill Height

Sill height is measured from the finished floor to the top of the sill, where you'd put your foot or knee to climb out. The finished floor means what's actually under your feet: carpet, vinyl plank, tile, not the bare slab.

Stand a tape on the floor and run it up to the sill. You need 44 inches or less.

The 44 inch limit is a real problem in basements with a high window set near the ceiling. If your sill sits at 50 inches, the window fails on that alone, however large it is. A permanent step or bench can sometimes help, but ask your building department before you count on it.

## Step 6: Check the Window Well

If the window is below grade, there's a well, and the well is part of the opening. Go outside, and with the window open, measure:

1. **Projection:** how far the well sticks out from the wall. At least 36 inches.
2. **Width:** side to side. At least 36 inches.
3. **Area:** multiply projection by width. At least 9 square feet (for example, 36 by 36 is 1,296 square inches, which is 9 square feet).
4. **Depth:** from the sill down to the bottom of the well and from the top of the well down to the bottom. If the well is deeper than 44 inches, it needs a permanent ladder or steps.
5. **Swing:** with the window fully open, does the sash clear the well wall? A window that can't open all the way in its well fails.
6. **Ladder:** if there's one, rungs at least 12 inches wide, projecting at least 3 inches from the wall, no more than 18 inches apart vertically, center to center. A ladder can intrude up to 6 inches into the required well size.
7. **Drain:** look at the bottom. Standing water, mud and leaves mean the drain isn't working. The IRC calls for drainage to the foundation drain or an approved alternative.

More on well dimensions in [window well size requirements in Nebraska](/blog/window-well-size-requirements-nebraska/).

## Step 7: Check What's Covering the Opening

Look for bars, grilles, screens or a well cover. Each one has to release from inside without keys, tools, special knowledge, or more force than operating the window itself (R310.4). A security bar held with a padlock fails, no matter how big the window is.

## Write It All Down

When you're done, you should have:

- Clear width and clear height on the open window
- Area in square feet
- Sill height from the finished floor
- Well projection, width, depth and swing clearance
- A note on anything covering the opening

Take a photo of the open window with the tape across it. If the numbers are close to the line, a photo helps whoever checks them.

## Common Mistakes

**Measuring the frame.** The most common error. A frame might be 48 inches wide and the open sash might clear 22. Only the open space counts.

**Trusting a crank casement to open fully.** Some casements stop well short of 90 degrees, and the sash edge and hardware stay in the opening. Open it as far as it goes and measure what's left.

**Measuring to the old floor.** If you plan to add flooring, the sill height changes. Thick carpet and pad, or a new subfloor, lower the number, which can help a sill that's too high. Taking flooring off raises it. Measure from the finished floor you expect to have.

**Ignoring the stops and the lock.** Stops, locks and a sash that opens only partway change the opening. A window that's painted shut or won't stay open isn't an escape opening.

**A well that's too shallow in projection.** A well that sticks out less than 36 inches fails, and a casement can hit the wall before it opens. Older wells can be undersized to begin with, or pushed in over the years by freeze-thaw. See [what freeze-thaw does to window wells](/blog/nebraska-freeze-thaw-window-well-damage/).

**Forgetting the well drain.** A flooded well isn't an escape route.

If the window passes everything, you're in good shape for a bedroom or for a sale. Run it by your building department, since they decide what's accepted at your address.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Where do you measure an egress window?

On the open window. Open the sash fully, then measure the clear width and clear height of the space you could pass through. Multiply them and divide by 144 for square feet. Don't measure the frame or the glass.

### How high can the sill of an egress window be?

No more than 44 inches above the finished floor under the 2018 IRC. Measure from the floor surface as it will be when finished, not from bare slab if you plan to add flooring.

### What size does an egress window well need to be?

At least 9 square feet of horizontal area, with at least 36 inches of projection and width, and enough room for the window to open fully. A well deeper than 44 inches needs a permanently attached ladder or steps.

### My casement doesn't open all the way. Does that matter?

Yes. Measure the clear opening with the sash opened as far as it goes. If the hardware or sash leaves less than the required clear space, the window doesn't meet the rule even if the frame is large.

### Do I need an inspector to check my measurements?

Not to measure, but for a bedroom, a permit or a sale, the building department decides what's accepted. Confirm with Lincoln Building and Safety for your address.
