# What the Inspector Checks at an Egress Window Permit Final

You've got the permit, the window is in, and the well is dug. Now someone from the city has to look at it before the permit closes. For most homeowners that visit is a mystery. What do they measure? What fails? Do you have to be home?

This article covers what an inspector typically looks at on a new egress window. We say "typically" on purpose. Inspectors work from the adopted code and from their own judgment on site, and Lincoln Building and Safety has the final say on what your project needs. ## Who Is Responsible for the Inspection

If a contractor installs your window, the contractor is normally responsible for pulling the permit and getting the inspection signed off. We pull the permit for our own work and schedule the inspection. If you do the work yourself or hire someone who doesn't pull a permit, that falls to you, and an unpermitted window can cause trouble when you sell or file an insurance claim.

The permit process itself, including how to apply in Lincoln and Lancaster County, is covered in [how the Lincoln and Lancaster County egress permit works](/blog/lincoln-lancaster-county-egress-permit/). This article is about what happens at the end of it.

## What Gets Checked, Item by Item

The code behind all of this is the 2018 IRC, which Nebraska and Lincoln use. Here's what the inspector typically works through. Not every item applies to every project.

### Net Clear Opening

The inspector wants to see that the window, when open, gives at least 5.7 square feet of net clear opening (5.0 square feet at a grade-floor opening). They'll look at the clear height, at least 24 inches, and the clear width, at least 20 inches. These are measured with the sash fully open, not from the glass or the frame.

This is where a lot of windows come up short. A window that looks big can have a small net clear opening if it opens only partway. Our [IRC egress window code requirements](/blog/irc-egress-window-code-requirements/) guide has the numbers.

### Sill Height

The sill can't be more than 44 inches above the finished floor. The inspector measures from the floor inside the room to the bottom of the clear opening. If the floor isn't finished yet, the height may be checked against the planned finished floor, so tell them what's going down.

### Opening Operation

They'll open the window. It has to open fully, without keys, tools or special knowledge, and with normal effort. A stiff or painted-shut window can fail here even if the size is right.

### Well Dimensions

If the window is below grade, the inspector will look at the well. The code asks for at least 9 square feet of horizontal area, at least 36 inches of projection and 36 inches of width, and room for the window to fully open (R310.2.3). They may measure the well and check that the sash doesn't hit the wall when it swings.

### Ladder or Steps

A well deeper than 44 inches needs a permanently affixed ladder or steps (R310.2.3.1). Rungs are at least 12 inches wide, project at least 3 inches from the wall, and sit no more than 18 inches apart vertically. A ladder can intrude up to 6 inches into the required well dimensions. The inspector may shake it to see that it's fixed, not just hung there. Our [window well ladder requirements](/blog/window-well-ladder-requirements/) page goes into it.

### Drainage

The well has to drain to the foundation drain or an approved alternative, unless the soil is well-drained Group I soil (R310.2.3.2). The inspector may look at the gravel base and where the drain tile ties in. This part is often covered by gravel quickly, so ask about timing. Some inspections happen before the well is backfilled, and some happen after. Ask Building and Safety which stages they want to see.

### Cover or Grille Release

If there's a cover, bar, grille or screen over the well, it has to release from inside without keys, tools or special knowledge, and without more force than it takes to operate the window itself (R310.4). A locking cover with no inside release is a common failure. See [egress window well cover code](/blog/egress-window-well-cover-code/) for the details.

### Header Over the Cut

When a new opening is cut into a foundation wall, there has to be a header or other support above it. The inspector may want to see it before it's covered, which is one reason cut-ins often get inspected in stages. If the wall carries unusual loads, an engineer may need to sign off. We don't do structural engineering, and [when an egress window needs a structural engineer](/blog/egress-window-header-structural-engineer/) covers when that happens.

### Safety Glazing Where It Applies

Some locations need tempered or other safety glass, such as near a door, near a tub or shower, or low to the floor in certain cases. The inspector may look for the stamp in the glass corner. Your installer knows where it applies.

### Fall Protection

If the sill is less than 24 inches above the floor and the opening is more than 72 inches above the outside grade, R312.2.1 asks for fall protection, either a limited opening or a window opening control device meeting ASTM F2090 that still releases for escape. This comes up with upper-floor windows more than basement ones, but a walkout or daylight basement can trigger it.

### Smoke and CO Alarms, If a Bedroom Is Created

If the project makes a new sleeping room, the inspector may check alarms under R314 and R315. Smoke alarms go in each sleeping room, outside each sleeping area, and on each story including the basement. CO alarms go outside sleeping areas if there's a fuel-fired appliance or an attached garage. Work that needs a permit and adds a bedroom generally brings alarms up to new-construction rules, interconnected where practical, with some exceptions when interior wall or ceiling finishes aren't being removed.

Alarm wiring is an electrician's job. We don't do electrical work, so if you need new hardwired alarms, line that up before the final.

## Common Reasons a Final Doesn't Pass

None of this is a surprise if you know the list:

- Net clear opening comes up short because the window only opens partway
- Sill is above 44 inches
- Well is too small, or the window hits the wall
- Missing ladder in a deep well
- Cover or grille can't be opened from inside
- No alarms in the new bedroom
- Permit work doesn't match what was approved

A failed final is usually a correction and a re-inspection, not a disaster. Ask the inspector what needs to change.

## How to Get Ready

A few steps help the visit go smoothly:

1. Have the permit card or number handy.
2. Make sure the window and the well are accessible, and clear anything in the way.
3. Leave a tape measure and a step stool, if it's useful.
4. Test the window and any cover release yourself first.
5. Know who is letting the inspector in, and whether an adult has to be home.
6. Check Building and Safety's own guidance on [the city's Building and Safety page](https://www.lincoln.ne.gov/City/Departments/Building-and-Safety).

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Who schedules the egress window inspection?

Normally the contractor who pulled the permit. We pull the permit for our work and handle the scheduling. If you pulled the permit yourself, you schedule it with Lincoln Building and Safety.

### Do I have to be home for the inspection?

Often someone needs to let the inspector in, and the inspector will want to open the window and check the room. Ask Building and Safety what they require for your project.

### What if the egress window fails inspection?

You fix the items on the list and ask for a re-inspection. Common fixes are adjusting a ladder, changing a cover release or correcting a well dimension.

### Does the inspector check the well drain?

They may. The code asks for drainage to the foundation drain or an approved alternative, unless the soil qualifies as well-drained. Confirm with the city when they want to see it.

### Do I need new smoke alarms for an egress window?

If the project creates a new bedroom, alarms typically have to meet the current code for that room and its area. Ask Building and Safety, and use an electrician for any wiring.
