# Egress Window Kits: What's in the Box vs a Full Install

Search for an egress window kit and you will find pages that look like a complete package: a window, a steel or plastic well, a ladder, a cover, sometimes a drain fitting. The photos make it look like the hard part is done once the pallet shows up.

It is not. A kit is a set of parts. The expensive, slow and risky work of getting those parts into a foundation wall happens after delivery, and none of it is in the box. This page walks through what a kit usually includes, what it never includes, and who a kit actually suits.

## What a kit usually includes

Kits differ by seller, so read the exact listing before you buy. Most bundle some mix of these four things:

- **The window.** Usually a casement, slider or single hung unit sized to meet the egress opening rules. Some kits include only the glass unit and frame, with no trim or sill finish.
- **The window well.** A corrugated galvanized steel or molded plastic well that sits against the outside of the foundation wall. Sizes are listed by width, depth and how far the well projects from the wall.
- **A ladder or steps.** Sometimes built into the well, sometimes a bolt-on add-on.
- **A cover.** A grate, a clear dome or a hinged lid. Some listings sell this as a separate line item.

Other items you may see: fasteners, a drain flange, a sill plate or a bag of clips. Treat anything not named on the page as not included.

Do not assume the parts match your opening. A kit window is built to a fixed size, and your existing wall, if there is one, has its own. The measurements have to line up before you order, not after.

## What a kit never includes

This is the part the listing leaves out. Every item below is labor, a decision or an approval, and none of it ships on a pallet.

**Cutting the foundation.** A new egress window in a poured or block wall means cutting a clean opening through structural concrete or masonry. The wall type changes the method, the dust, the noise and the risk of cracking. Our [egress window cut-in process](/blog/egress-window-cut-in-process/) page covers how that step goes.

**The header.** Once you remove a section of wall, something has to carry the load above the new opening. How that is done depends on the wall, the framing above it and what the structure above is carrying. That is a structural question. We install egress windows and window wells, but we do not do structural design, so if your situation needs an engineer's drawing, that comes from an engineer.

**Excavation.** Outside the wall, someone has to dig down to the footing depth you need, in clay soil that holds water and swells in spring. A pallet of window well parts does not include a trench or a place to put the spoil.

**The permit.** Basement egress work generally needs a permit. The [Lincoln and Lancaster County egress permit](/blog/lincoln-lancaster-county-egress-permit/) page explains how that works. When we install, we pull the permit. When you buy a kit and hire a handyman, the permit becomes your job.

**Drain tie-in.** A window well with no way to shed water becomes a bucket. Gravel, a drain connection to the footing drain where one exists, and grading around the top of the well are all part of making the well work. None of that is a kit part.

**Backfill and compaction.** The soil you put back around the well has to be placed and tamped so the well does not shift, bow or let water run down the wall. This is slow, physical work.

**Waterproofing at the wall.** Sealing around the window frame and the cut edge of the foundation is separate from any waterproofing system for the house. We do not install waterproofing systems, and if your basement already takes on water, that needs its own fix first.

**Interior finish.** Framing returns, drywall or a finished sill, paint, trim and insulation around the frame. A kit window is not a finished room.

**Disposal.** The concrete or block you cut out, the soil you do not put back, and the packaging all need to go somewhere.

## Why a kit's parts still have to meet the code

A kit does not make an opening legal by being a kit. The IRC sets the numbers for the opening and the well, and the finished install has to meet them.

The figures below come from the IRC R310 text as published on [UpCodes](https://up.codes/viewer/nebraska/irc-2018/chapter/3/building-planning). Lincoln may adopt a different edition than the one on that page, so treat the numbers as the standard IRC figures and confirm with the building department.

- The net clear opening of the window is at least 5.7 square feet (5.0 square feet at grade floor), with at least 24 inches of clear height and 20 inches of clear width.
- The sill is no more than 44 inches above the floor.
- The window well has at least 9 square feet of horizontal area, with at least 36 inches of projection and width.
- A well deeper than 44 inches needs a permanently fixed ladder or steps that work with the window fully open.
- Bars, grilles, covers and screens cannot reduce the required opening, and they must be releasable or removable from inside without a key, tool or special knowledge.

Notice what that last point means for a kit with a locking cover. A cover that needs a key to open from the inside does not meet R310.4, even if it ships with the kit. Our [window well ladder requirements](/blog/window-well-ladder-requirements/) page goes through the ladder rule in more detail.

The practical point: the kit's published window size is the unit's nominal size, not the clear opening after the frame, sash and hardware are accounted for. Look for the net clear opening number on the listing, and check it against your own measurement of the finished opening.

## Reading a kit listing

A few habits help when you compare listings.

**Check the net clear opening, not the nominal size.** A window described as 36 by 48 inches may not give you the clear height and width the code needs once the sash is open. A reputable listing states the net clear opening.

**Check the well projection.** Many wells are sold by width and projection. If the projection is under 36 inches, it does not meet the minimum no matter how good the price is.

**Check the depth.** Your well depth depends on the sill height and how deep the wall sits below grade. If the finished well will be deeper than 44 inches, the ladder is not optional.

**Check the energy label.** A good window carries an NFRC label showing a U-factor and solar heat gain coefficient. The [NFRC consumer page](https://www.nfrc.org/consumers/) explains what those two numbers mean: a lower U-factor means less heat escapes, and a low solar heat gain coefficient means less solar heat gets in. For a below-grade window in a Nebraska basement, the U-factor is the number to compare.

**Read the shipping terms.** Kits are bulky. Check who handles delivery, what damage terms apply, and what the return policy says about a window that turns out not to fit.

**Ask what is excluded.** If the listing does not say, assume the answer is "everything on the list above."

## Who a kit actually suits

A kit makes sense in a few narrow situations.

**You already have a contractor who will install it.** If you hire a licensed contractor who is happy to work with owner-supplied parts, a kit can be a reasonable way to choose the products yourself. Ask first. Some contractors will not warranty work built around parts they did not source, and some prefer to order the window to the opening.

**You are comfortable with a large scope of work.** A kit suits someone who wants to manage the project, hire the pieces separately and accept the coordination. That means a concrete cutter, an excavator, a framer, a drywall finisher and an inspector, and it means you carry the schedule.

**You are replacing a failed well, not cutting a new opening.** If the opening already exists and the window is sound, a replacement well is a smaller job.

A kit is a poor fit if you were hoping to save money by doing the cutting yourself. The saving is real only if nothing goes wrong, and the foundation cut is where things go wrong. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does an egress window kit include everything I need?

No. A kit covers parts: usually the window, the well, a ladder and a cover. It does not cover the foundation cut, the header, excavation, the permit, drainage, backfill or interior finish. Read the listing for exactly what ships, and assume anything not named is excluded.

### Can I buy an egress window kit and install it myself?

You can buy one, but the install is the hard part. Cutting a foundation wall, supporting the opening, digging and draining the well and getting the permit are separate jobs. Many homeowners hire out the cut and the structural work and manage the rest.

### Will any egress window kit pass code?

Not automatically. The opening must give a net clear opening of at least 5.7 square feet (5.0 at grade floor), with at least 24 inches of height and 20 inches of width. The well needs 9 square feet of area and a 36 inch projection, and a ladder if it is deeper than 44 inches. Check each number on the listing.

### Are covers that lock allowed on an egress well?

Covers must be releasable or removable from the inside without a key, tool or special knowledge. A cover that needs a key to open from inside does not meet that requirement. Choose a cover that opens with normal effort from the inside.

### Is a kit cheaper than a full installation?

Sometimes the parts cost less than a contractor-supplied package, but the parts are only one piece of the project. Add the cut, the header work, the excavation, the permit and the finish before comparing. See our egress window cost page for the ranges on a full Lincoln project.
