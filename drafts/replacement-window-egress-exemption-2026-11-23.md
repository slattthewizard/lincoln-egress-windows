# Replacing a Bedroom Window: How the Egress Code Treats Replacement Windows

You're swapping an old window in a bedroom, and someone tells you the new one has to meet today's egress rules. Someone else says it doesn't. Both are partly right. The code does have an exemption for replacement windows, but it comes with conditions, and it's easy to lose it without realizing.

## The Short Version of the Rule

The 2018 IRC, which Nebraska and Lincoln use, requires an emergency escape and rescue opening in every basement and in each sleeping room (R310.1). The usual numbers are a net clear opening of at least 5.7 square feet (5.0 at a grade-floor opening), at least 24 inches of clear height, at least 20 inches of clear width, and a sill no more than 44 inches above the floor. The full list is in our guide to [IRC egress window code requirements](/blog/irc-egress-window-code-requirements/).

Then there's R310.2.5 in the 2018 edition. It says replacement windows installed in an existing opening are exempt from those size and sill rules when two things are true:

1. The replacement is the manufacturer's largest standard size that will fit in the existing frame or opening.
2. The replacement doesn't reduce the size of the existing opening.

You can read the text in the [Nebraska 2018 IRC on UpCodes](https://up.codes/viewer/nebraska/irc-2018/chapter/3/building-planning). Lincoln may apply it differently, so confirm with Lincoln Building and Safety for your address. The city has the final say.

## Why the Exemption Exists

An older house may have bedroom windows that were fine when they were built. Making every replacement meet current egress sizes would mean cutting into walls and foundations just to swap a window. The exemption keeps a simple replacement simple, as long as you aren't making the escape route worse.

Notice what it does not say. It doesn't say any replacement is fine. It doesn't say the window is legal as an escape opening. It says the replacement isn't held to the new numbers if it gives you the most opening the existing space allows.

## What "Manufacturer's Largest Standard Size" Means in Practice

Window makers sell standard sizes. A given opening can take more than one of them. The exemption asks for the largest standard size that fits, not the cheapest one or the one that looks nicest.

Say your old window is a small double-hung and the opening could take a larger one, or a different style that gives more clear opening. If you put in a smaller standard window and leave a gap filled with trim or framing, you've probably lost the exemption. The code's idea is that you didn't have to enlarge the hole, but you did have to use the room that was there.

This also changes with window style. A double-hung opens only about half its height, so the net clear opening is roughly half the frame. A casement swings fully open and can give a much larger clear opening in the same space. A slider opens about half its width. Two windows with the same frame size can have very different net clear openings. Our guide to [casement, slider and inswing egress windows](/blog/casement-slider-inswing-egress-windows/) goes through how each one behaves.

In practice, when someone picks a window for a bedroom replacement, the question to ask is: of the standard sizes that fit this opening, which gives the biggest clear opening? If the answer isn't the one in the cart, change the cart.

## What "Not Reduce the Existing Opening" Means

This is the second condition, and it's where plenty of jobs go wrong. The opening you have today sets a floor. The replacement can't make it smaller.

Ways a replacement can shrink the opening:

- A new frame that's thicker than the old one eats into the glass area.
- Filling part of the rough opening with extra framing, or a panel to make a smaller window fit
- A style change, such as swapping a casement for a double-hung, which cuts the net clear opening roughly in half
- Adding a divided or fixed lite that can't open

Each one gives up a piece of the escape route. The exemption doesn't cover a window that opens less than the old one did.

## Full-Frame vs Insert Replacement

This is the part most homeowners don't hear about until the quote comes in.

A **full-frame replacement** takes the old window out down to the rough opening. The old frame comes out, the opening gets inspected and flashed, and a new frame goes in. You can pick the size to match the opening. You have the most room to get the largest clear opening.

An **insert (pocket) replacement** leaves the old frame in place and fits a new window inside it. It's quicker and costs less, but the new window has to fit inside the old frame, so it's always a bit smaller. The glass area shrinks by the width of the old frame, and the net clear opening shrinks with it.

For a bedroom, that matters. An insert can pass the "largest standard size that fits" test for the existing frame, depending on how the code reads it, but you end up with less clear opening than a full-frame job in the same hole. If your old window was already close to the minimums, an insert can drop you under them, and then you're relying on the exemption instead of meeting the numbers.

How the city reads "existing opening," whether the frame counts or the rough opening does, is exactly the kind of thing to ask Lincoln Building and Safety before you order.

## Why Upgrading Is Often the Smarter Move

The exemption is a minimum, not a goal. A few reasons to go further when you can:

- **Safety.** A bigger, easier opening is a better way out in a fire. The numbers in R310 exist for a firefighter in gear and for a person climbing out in the dark.
- **A legal bedroom.** If the room is in a basement, the exemption only keeps you from making things worse. It doesn't turn a small window into a compliant one. If you want the room counted as a bedroom when you sell, it helps to meet the numbers. See [what counts as a legal bedroom in Nebraska](/blog/what-counts-as-a-legal-bedroom-nebraska/).
- **Inspections and sales.** A buyer's inspector may flag a bedroom window that doesn't meet the numbers, whether or not the exemption applied when it was installed.
- **You only open the wall once.** If an enlarged opening is coming eventually, doing it with the replacement saves repeating the work.

In a basement, a real upgrade usually means enlarging the opening and often adding a well. That's a cut-in, not a simple swap, and it typically costs $3,500 to $7,000. If the opening is already big enough, an [egress window replacement](/egress-window-replacement-lincoln/) in the existing opening typically costs $1,200 to $3,000 and is often a half-day job. ## Other Things That Don't Go Away

The replacement exemption deals with size and sill height. It doesn't wipe out everything else.

- **Fall protection.** R312.2.1 requires operable windows with a sill less than 24 inches above the floor and an opening more than 72 inches above grade outside to have fall protection. A window opening control device has to meet ASTM F2090 and still release for escape.
- **Covers and bars.** Anything over the window or well has to open from inside without keys or tools (R310.4).
- **Wells.** If the window is in a well, the well has its own rules, and a replacement doesn't change that.
- **Safety glazing.** Some locations need tempered glass. Ask your installer.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I need to enlarge the opening when I replace a bedroom window?

Not necessarily. Under R310.2.5 in the 2018 IRC, a replacement in an existing opening is exempt from the size and sill rules if it's the largest standard size that fits and doesn't reduce the opening. Lincoln Building and Safety decides how that applies to your address.

### Does the exemption make my old window a legal escape opening?

No. It only means the replacement isn't held to the current numbers. If the opening is too small to meet them, the window still falls short as an escape opening, and that's worth fixing in any room people sleep in.

### Is an insert replacement allowed under the exemption?

It can be, but an insert is smaller than a full-frame replacement in the same opening, which reduces the clear opening. Ask the city whether they measure the frame or the rough opening before you choose one.

### Can I swap a casement for a double-hung?

That can cut the net clear opening roughly in half, which is a reduction of the opening. It may cost you the exemption, so confirm it first.

### Do I need a permit to replace a bedroom window?

It depends on the scope, and Lincoln Building and Safety can tell you for your address. We pull the permit for the work we do.
