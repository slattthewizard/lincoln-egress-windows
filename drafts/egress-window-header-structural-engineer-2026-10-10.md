# Egress Window Header, Lintel and Structural Engineer: What a New Cut-In Needs

A basement wall is not just a wall. It holds back soil on the outside and carries the house on top of it. When you cut a hole in it for an egress window, you remove a piece of that structure, and something has to carry the load that piece used to carry.

That something is a header (also called a lintel, especially in block). This article covers why a new cut-in needs one, how poured and block walls differ, when it makes sense to bring in a structural engineer, and what to ask a contractor before you sign. ## Why a new opening needs a header or lintel

Picture the wall above your future window. The floor framing, the walls on the main level, the roof and the snow on it all push down through that wall to the footing. Before the cut, that load travels straight down through solid concrete or block. After the cut, there is a three-foot gap with nothing in it.

A header or lintel is a beam that spans the gap. It picks up the load above the opening and passes it sideways into the solid wall on each end, which then carries it down. The Concrete Masonry & Hardscapes Association (formerly NCMA) describes lintels as functioning "as beams" that support the wall weight and other loads over an opening and transfer them to the adjacent masonry, in its note on [precast concrete lintels for concrete masonry construction (TEK 17-2A)](https://www.cmha.org/resource/tek-17-02a/).

Two details from that same note are worth knowing as a homeowner:

- A precast lintel is specified at the clear span plus 8 inches, so that it sits at least 4 inches on solid masonry at each end. Bearing at the ends is part of the design, not an afterthought.
- Lintels are sized for the loads they carry. A longer span or a heavier load above means a bigger lintel.

This is the reason a cut-in is not just "cut a hole and drop a window in." The window is the easy part. The load path around it is the part that matters.

## Headers are not the same as the window frame

People sometimes assume the window's frame or the buck around it holds up the wall. It does not. The frame is there to hold glass and seal against water. It is not designed to carry the foundation wall above it.

The structural member sits above the frame, in or on the wall, and it has its own bearing on solid wall at both ends. In a poured wall it can be reinforcement and a concrete section that was designed to span. In a block wall it is usually a steel angle, a precast lintel, or a bond-beam style course with grouted reinforcement. Which of those is right depends on the wall, the opening width and what sits above.

## Poured walls and block walls handle an opening differently

Our guide to [block vs poured foundations for egress](/blog/block-vs-poured-foundation-egress/) goes deeper. The short version:

| | Poured concrete | Concrete block |
|---|---|---|
| How it is built | One continuous mass, usually with rebar | Individual units stacked with mortar |
| Spanning an opening | Can bridge a modest span, but still needs the opening designed | Cannot span on its own once courses are removed |
| What goes over the opening | Header or reinforced section sized to the load | Lintel bearing on sound block at both ends |
| Common trouble | Cutting rebar without a plan; ragged edges | Cracked mortar joints, loose units, hollow cores |

In a poured wall, the concrete around the cut tends to share the load, so the header detail is often simpler. In block, nothing above the opening has anything to sit on until the lintel is in, so the sequence matters. A good crew supports the work as it goes rather than cutting everything out and then thinking about what is above.

None of this makes either wall a bad candidate. It means the wall type changes the method, the sequence and sometimes the cost within the normal range. For the full sequence of a cut-in, see our walkthrough of the [egress window cut-in process](/blog/egress-window-cut-in-process/).

## When a structural engineer's letter is required or wise

Here is the part where we will be careful. Whether the City of Lincoln asks for an engineer on a given job is a decision the city makes for that job. We do not know of a published rule that says every cut-in in Lincoln needs one, and we are not going to tell you there is. A new or enlarged opening is permit work, and whether the city asks for engineered drawings on your job is something to confirm with [Lincoln Building and Safety](https://www.lincoln.ne.gov/City/Departments/Building-and-Safety) for your address. Our post on [the Lincoln and Lancaster County egress permit](/blog/lincoln-lancaster-county-egress-permit/) explains how that process works.

Setting the permit question aside, there are situations where an engineer's letter is simply a good idea, whether or not anyone requires it:

**A wide or unusual opening.** A single standard egress unit is a modest span. If you want a wide slider, two windows side by side, or a larger opening for light, the span goes up and so does the load on the header.

**Heavy load above the opening.** A wall that carries a beam or a post from above, a point load from a girder, a chimney, or a second-story wall stacked over it is not a place to guess. Say the spot you picked for the window sits under the end of a main beam. That is a conversation for an engineer before anyone picks up a saw.

**A wall that is already cracked, bowing or stepped.** A wall with horizontal cracks, stair-step cracks in block, or an inward lean is already telling you something. Cutting into it without understanding why it moved can make it worse. Our post on a [bowing window well wall](/blog/bowing-window-well-wall/) covers the exterior side of the same problem. Foundation repair itself is not work we do; a foundation repair specialist or an engineer should look at that first.

**An opening near a corner, a pilaster or another opening.** Bearing at the ends of the header needs solid wall. Close to a corner or another window, there may not be enough of it.

**A wall with unknown construction.** If you cannot tell whether the cores in a block wall are grouted, whether there is rebar, or what the wall thickness really is, someone qualified should find out.

**Other reasons.** Your lender, insurer or a future buyer's inspector may want written confirmation. A letter from an engineer on file with the permit can answer those questions later.

We install egress windows and window wells and we pull the permit. Structural design is not something we offer, so on a job that calls for it, the engineer is a separate party you or we would bring in, and their direction on the header sets what gets built.

## What the cutting itself involves

The usual method for cutting concrete for egress windows is a wall saw or wire saw that makes clean, straight edges, rather than a hammer that shatters the surrounding wall. Clean edges matter because the header needs full bearing on sound material, and the window needs a square opening to seat and seal.

Before any of that, the exterior is excavated so there is room for the window well and so the opening can be worked from outside. The opening is laid out to land where the code requires the window size and sill height, and where the wall can handle it. For the numbers the finished window and well have to meet, see our posts on [IRC egress window code requirements](/blog/irc-egress-window-code-requirements/) and [window well size requirements](/blog/window-well-size-requirements-nebraska/).

Lincoln's clay soils, freeze-thaw cycles and spring storms are the reason the sealing and drainage details around the cut matter as much as the structure. A well-supported opening that leaks is still a failed job.

## Questions to ask a contractor before you sign

Put these in writing or ask them on the estimate visit:

1. **What is going over the opening, and who sized it?** You want to hear a specific answer: a steel angle, a precast lintel, a reinforced header. "It's fine, we do these all the time" is not an answer.
2. **How much bearing does it get on each side?** The TEK note above gives 4 inches of bearing at each end as a minimum for a precast lintel. Ask what your job gets.
3. **Is the wall poured or block, and how does that change the plan?** A contractor who has looked at the wall should be able to say.
4. **Is there anything above this spot that I should know about?** Beams, posts, chimneys, a deck ledger.
5. **Does the job include a permit, and will the work be inspected?** Walk away from anyone who suggests skipping it. Our post on [the risks of DIY egress window installation](/blog/diy-egress-window-installation-risks/) covers what goes wrong when the structural step is skipped.
6. **If the wall is cracked or bowed, what is the plan?** The honest answer may be "see an engineer or foundation specialist first."
7. **What happens if you find something unexpected, like rebar, a grouted core or a void?** Ask whether it changes the scope and price in writing.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does a new egress window need a header?

Yes. Cutting an opening removes wall that was carrying load from above, so a header or lintel has to span the gap and pass that load to solid wall on both sides. In block walls this is especially important because block cannot span an opening by itself.

### Does Lincoln require a structural engineer for an egress window cut-in?

We have not found a published city rule that requires an engineer for every cut-in, so we do not claim one exists. A new or enlarged opening needs a permit, and Building and Safety staff decide what a given submittal needs. Ask them for your address, and bring in an engineer on your own if the opening is wide, the load above is heavy or the wall is cracked.

### Is a lintel the same thing as a header?

They do the same job, which is to carry load across the top of an opening. "Lintel" is the word usually used in masonry for a steel angle or a precast concrete beam, and "header" is used more often for a reinforced concrete section or a framed beam. Either way, the member needs a design and solid bearing on both sides.

### Is it harder to cut an egress window into a poured wall or a block wall?

Poured concrete is harder to physically cut because it is dense and reinforced. Block is easier to cut but less forgiving, since nothing above the opening has support until the lintel is set. Both are routine for a crew that does them regularly.

### Can cutting an egress window weaken my foundation?

It can if the opening is cut without a properly sized header and enough bearing on each side, or if it is made in a wall that is already cracked or bowing. Done with a designed header in a sound wall, it is a standard modification. If your wall already shows movement, have it assessed before anyone cuts.
