# How to Prepare Your Basement for Egress Window Installation Day

Cutting a new opening through a foundation wall is loud, dusty and a little chaotic for a day or two. The crew handles the hard part. What you do before they show up decides whether the job runs smoothly or starts with an hour of moving a couch, a freezer and three boxes of holiday decorations.

This guide covers what to clear, what to cover, and what each day usually looks like. If you want to talk through your specific wall before the date is set, call [(402) 261-0485](tel:+14022610485) or [request a free written estimate](/#contact).

## Pick the Wall and Walk It Together First

Before installation day, the crew will have looked at the wall, the yard outside it, and the space inside. Walk it with them if you can. You want to settle three things: exactly where the window goes, where the excavation happens outside, and what has to move.

Ask where the dirt will be piled and where the equipment will sit. Ask whether anything in the yard needs to come out of the way. A good plan on paper still needs a clear patch of ground.

## Clear a Work Zone Inside

Sawing or breaking a hole in poured concrete or block throws dust and debris into the room, even with good controls. Give the crew room, and give your belongings distance.

Here's a practical inside list:

- **Clear the wall itself.** Move everything away from the wall where the window goes. A common recommendation is to leave a wide open area in front of it so people can carry tools and debris without squeezing past furniture. More room is better than less.
- **Move furniture out of the room if you can.** If you can't, pull it to the far side and cover it.
- **Take things off the walls nearby.** Vibration from cutting can shake frames, shelves and mirrors loose.
- **Empty shelves and cabinets near the work.** Anything on a shelf on the other side of the wall is in play.
- **Remove or cover floor coverings.** Carpet traps dust and is hard to clean. Roll up rugs and ask the crew about floor protection.
- **Clear a path from the work area to the basement stairs and outside door.** Debris and tools go out that way.

If the wall is already finished, with drywall, paneling or framing, tell the crew ahead of time. Whatever is on that wall may have to come off in the area of the cut, and finish work beyond the window opening is outside what we do. A drywall or paint contractor handles that part afterward.

## Protect Belongings From Concrete Dust

Concrete dust is fine, gets everywhere, and settles on things you don't think of as being near the work. It also isn't something to breathe. Cutting or grinding concrete and masonry can release respirable crystalline silica, which OSHA treats as a serious hazard for workers. You can read the agency's overview at [osha.gov](https://www.osha.gov/).

The better crews control it at the source. That means cutting with water or using dust collection rather than letting dry dust fly. Ask any contractor you hire how they handle dust on a cut-in. It's a fair question, and the answer should be specific.

On your side, a few steps help a lot:

1. **Cover what can't move.** Heavy plastic sheeting taped over furniture, a workbench or a water heater cabinet keeps dust off the surfaces.
2. **Close off the rest of the house.** Hang plastic over the basement stair opening or the doorway, and close the door at the top of the stairs.
3. **Seal the return air.** If a furnace return or a duct runs near the work, ask the crew or an HVAC person about covering the grille temporarily so the system doesn't pull dust through the house. We don't do HVAC work, so this is a question for your furnace company if you aren't sure.
4. **Pack electronics, papers and anything stored in open boxes.** Seal them in plastic bins or move them upstairs.
5. **Plan to clean afterward.** Even with good control you'll be wiping down surfaces. A damp cloth beats a dry one, because dry sweeping puts dust back in the air.

## Kids, Pets and Anyone With Breathing Trouble

Keep children and pets out of the basement on work days. There are tools, open edges, heavy pieces of concrete and a hole in your wall that leads outside. A dog that wanders over to look at the excavation is a problem for everyone.

Plan for the day somewhere else if you can: at a relative's house, or upstairs with a firmly closed door and a gate. Birds and small animals in cages upstairs are also sensitive to dust, so move them as far from the work as you can.

If anyone in the home has asthma or another breathing condition, talk to the crew about timing and about where they can stay during the cutting. A day away from the house may be the easiest answer.

## Outside: Where the Dirt and the Equipment Go

The window needs a hole in the ground outside it before the hole in the wall. That excavation goes down to below the sill and out from the foundation, and the soil has to go somewhere.

Walk the outside and clear it:

- **Move anything within several feet of the wall.** Planters, grills, kids' toys, patio furniture, hoses and decorations.
- **Trim shrubs and low branches** that are in the way of the dig.
- **Mark sprinkler heads and lines.** Tell the crew where they are, and see our note on utilities below.
- **Decide where the soil pile goes.** Clay soil is heavy and sticky. Ask the crew whether it will sit on a tarp, get hauled off, or be reused as backfill, and whether you want some of it moved.
- **Clear a path for equipment.** A narrow side yard with a gate can limit what can get in. Mention fences, tight gates and steep slopes when you get the estimate.
- **Check what is above or beside the spot.** Decks, porches, A/C units and gas meters near the wall all affect the plan.

Lincoln's clay soil holds water, so a wet week can turn the yard to mud and slow the dig. Ask what happens to the schedule if it rains.

## Utilities Near the Wall

Before anyone digs, the underground lines near your foundation have to be located. Gas, electric, water, sewer and communications lines often run close to the house. The state's one-call service, Nebraska811, is the place to start, and the person digging normally makes that call. Ask your contractor to confirm it's done. We cover how that works in [call 811 before digging a window well](/blog/call-811-before-window-well-nebraska/).

Lines on your own property, such as a feed to a detached garage, a sprinkler system, a pet fence or yard lighting, usually aren't marked by the utilities. If you know where any of those run, say so. Inside, note where the water, gas, electrical and sewer lines run along the wall or near the spot, including a panel, a meter or a pipe. We're not electricians or plumbers, and rerouting is that trade's job, but we need to know before cutting.

## What a 1 to 3 Day Cut-In Looks Like

Every house differs, so treat this as a general picture, not a promise. Most cut-ins run one to three days. The timeline depends on foundation type, how deep the well goes, weather and what the crew finds. For the cutting steps, see [the egress window cut-in process](/blog/egress-window-cut-in-process/). For timing, see [the egress window installation timeline](/blog/egress-window-installation-timeline/).

**Day 1: Excavation and layout.** The crew sets up, protects the work areas and lays out the opening. Soil comes out on the outside to expose the foundation wall. They may mark the cut from inside and outside. If the day goes well, cutting begins.

**Day 2: Cutting and framing the opening.** The opening is cut through the foundation. This is the loudest and dustiest part. Concrete pieces come out, the opening is prepared, and a header goes in over the opening where the plan calls for one. The window is set and sealed in.

**Day 3: Well and finishing.** The well is set against the wall, with gravel at the base and a drain tie-in if the plan calls for one. Backfill goes in, grade is set so water runs away from the house, and cleanup happens. Covers and ladders go in if you ordered them.

Some jobs wrap up in a day. A harder wall, a deep well, or a rainy week stretches it. The point is not to book a day with an unmovable deadline right after.

## The Morning Of: A Quick Checklist

- Basement cleared and covered
- Plastic up over the stairs and any open doorways
- Kids and pets arranged for
- Yard cleared and sprinkler lines pointed out
- A gate or driveway path open for equipment
- A way for the crew to reach you (a phone number you'll actually answer)
- Basement bathroom or water supply sorted out if the crew needs access

Be reachable, and be ready to make small decisions. A crew that has to guess about where you want a soil pile or a tree trimmed will guess, and you may not like the answer.

## After the Crew Leaves

Walk the job with whoever is in charge. Open and close the window several times. Check that it opens fully and that the well gives you room to climb out. Look at the grade outside and confirm it slopes away from the house. Then do the dust cleanup, damp wipes first, then vacuum with a good filter.

Smoke alarms and, where there's a fuel-fired appliance or attached garage, CO alarms matter in a basement, especially if it's now a sleeping area. Alarm wiring is an electrician's job, not ours. Check with Lincoln Building and Safety about what your address needs.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much should I move out of the basement before installation day?

Clear the wall where the window goes and a wide open area in front of it, and move anything fragile or dust-sensitive out of the room. Cover what stays. When you're not sure, move it or ask the crew.

### Will the dust get into the rest of my house?

It can if nothing is blocking it. Hang plastic over the stairway, close the door at the top, and ask the crew how they control dust while cutting. Water-fed cutting or dust collection cuts down what gets airborne.

### Do I need to be home on installation day?

Plan to be reachable and available for decisions, especially on the first day. The crew may need to ask about the wall, the yard or where debris goes. Kids and pets should be somewhere else.

### How long does an egress window installation take?

A cut-in usually takes one to three days, depending on the foundation, the depth of the well and the weather. Ask for a schedule with some slack in it.
