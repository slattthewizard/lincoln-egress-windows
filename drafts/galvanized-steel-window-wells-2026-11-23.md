# Galvanized Steel Window Wells: What to Look For Before You Buy

Walk through any older Lincoln neighborhood and you'll see them: ribbed metal half-circles set against the foundation, holding back the dirt around a basement window. Corrugated galvanized steel is the classic window well, and for good reason. It's strong, it's affordable, and it's been doing this job for decades.

It's also easy to buy the wrong one. A well that's too shallow, too narrow, loosely anchored or sitting on bad drainage will cause trouble no matter what it's made of. This guide covers what to look for in a steel well, where steel shines, where composite is the better call, and how a well holds up against Lincoln's clay soil and freeze-thaw winters. ## What You're Actually Buying

A corrugated steel window well is a curved sheet of steel, formed into ribs, that bolts to the foundation wall and holds back the soil. The ribs aren't decoration. They give the thin sheet its stiffness, the same way a corrugated roof panel resists bending along its length. Dirt pushes inward on the wall of a well all year, and the ribs are what stop it from folding.

Galvanizing is a zinc coating on the steel. The zinc sits between the steel and the moisture around it and corrodes first, which slows the rusting of the metal underneath. It's a good system, but it isn't a guarantee, and there are a few places where it falls short. We'll get to those.

## Thickness: Ask About It, Don't Guess at It

Steel sheet comes in different thicknesses, and thickness is the main thing separating a well that holds its shape from one that bows after a few seasons. A thicker sheet resists soil pressure better and has more metal to spare when the surface starts to corrode. A thin sheet can feel flimsy in your hands and dent when a lawn mower or a shovel hits it.

We're not going to give you a number to hold a supplier to, because thickness is measured different ways and the right choice depends on the depth of the well and the soil behind it. What you can do:

- Ask the seller what thickness the steel is, and ask them to put it in writing on the quote.
- Compare two wells of the same depth side by side. If one feels clearly stiffer in the hand, that's information.
- Be wary of a deep well sold at the same price as a shallow one. Depth means more soil pressure and usually more steel.

## Why Cut Edges Rust First

Zinc coating covers the steel when it leaves the mill. Every time someone cuts a hole, trims a length or drills a fastener, they expose raw steel. Zinc nearby offers some protection to a small exposed edge, but a long cut or a scraped patch will start to rust over time, especially where soil stays damp.

That's why the following matter on a steel well:

- **Bolt holes and cut edges.** Look for edges that are clean and coated, and use fasteners made to resist corrosion.
- **The bottom edge.** The bottom of the well sits in wet gravel and soil. It's usually the first place you'll see rust.
- **Scratches from installation.** A rock dragged across the surface can strip the coating. Backfill carefully and keep sharp stone away from the wall of the well.

## How a Steel Well Is Anchored

A well that isn't firmly tied to the foundation will pull away. Soil pressure, frost movement and the weight of snow all work on it. A good installation anchors the well to the foundation wall through the flange with masonry fasteners set into sound concrete or block, spaced along the whole height of the wall rather than just at the top.

Things to ask about:

- What the fasteners are, and what they're anchored into. Poured concrete and block hold fasteners differently.
- Whether the well sits on a firm base. We set wells on a gravel base, which gives water a path down and keeps the bottom edge out of standing water.
- Whether the seam between the well and the foundation is sealed or flashed so water doesn't run behind it and into the basement window.

A well that is bolted at the top only can tilt out from the wall over a few winters. That leaves a gap, and gaps let soil and water in.

## Size It to the Code Before You Choose a Style

If the window above your well is an egress window, the well has to meet the 2018 IRC, which Nebraska and Lincoln use. Confirm with Lincoln Building and Safety for your address. The well requirements in section R310.2.3 include:

- At least 9 square feet of horizontal area.
- At least 36 inches of projection and width.
- Enough room for the window to fully open.
- A permanently attached ladder or steps if the well is deeper than 44 inches.
- A drain connection to the foundation drain or another approved method, with an exception for well-drained soils.

You can read the [Nebraska 2018 IRC text](https://up.codes/viewer/nebraska/irc-2018/chapter/3/building-planning#R310.2.3) for the exact wording. Our post on [window well size requirements in Nebraska](/blog/window-well-size-requirements-nebraska/) goes through each number.

The catch with catalog wells: many standard steel wells are sized for a plain basement window, not an egress opening. A well that works for a small hopper window may be too small, too shallow in projection, or too narrow for a swinging egress window. Measure before you order, and check that the sash can open all the way.

## Where Steel Is a Good Choice

Steel is a reasonable pick when:

- You want a sturdy, plain well at a moderate price.
- The well is for a basement window that isn't an egress opening, or the dimensions of a standard model fit your egress needs.
- You're replacing like with like on an older home and want something similar in look.
- The yard sees equipment, kids' toys and lawn work, and you want something that shrugs off bumps.

## When Composite Is the Better Call

Steel isn't right for every wall. Consider composite when:

- Corrosion is the problem you're trying to escape. A well that rusted out once in a damp location may rust out again.
- The wall is visible from the street or the patio and you want a cleaner look, or a stone or stacked-block appearance.
- The soil stays wet, or the well sits below a downspout or low spot where water collects.
- You want a lighter well that is easier to carry and set.

Composite has its own trade-offs, such as how it handles sun and how it's anchored. Our [window well material comparison](/blog/window-well-material-comparison/) goes through the pros and cons side by side, so we won't repeat all of it here.

## How Steel Holds Up Against Lincoln Clay and Freeze-Thaw

Lincoln sits on a lot of clay. Clay holds water, swells when wet and shrinks when dry, and it passes that pressure along to anything buried in it, including a window well. When clay freezes, the soil pushes against the well wall even harder. Then it thaws, and the soil settles.

Two things tend to decide how a steel well fares:

1. **Drainage.** A well that fills with water and sits there rusts faster, and freezing water in the bottom of the well can push the well wall. A gravel base and a drain tile tie-in at the well give water somewhere to go. Our [window well installation](/window-well-installation-lincoln/) work includes both.
2. **Backfill and grade.** Soil that slopes toward the well pours water in. Grade should slope away from the house, and backfill should be put back in layers instead of dumped.

For more on the freeze-thaw side, see [freeze-thaw damage to window wells in Nebraska](/blog/nebraska-freeze-thaw-window-well-damage/). A steel well is not immune to any of this, but a properly anchored, well-drained one handles it.

## A Short Buying Checklist

Before you sign for a steel well, run through these:

1. Is the window above it an egress window, and does the well meet the 9 square feet and 36 inch minimums?
2. Is the well deep enough to need a ladder, and does it come with one?
3. What's the sheet thickness, and is it written on the quote?
4. How will it be anchored, and into what?
5. What's under it: gravel base, drain connection?
6. If you plan a cover, will it open from the inside without keys or tools?
7. Is the price in writing before the work starts?

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long does a galvanized steel window well last?

It depends on soil moisture, drainage and how much of the coating survives installation. A well with good drainage and clean cut edges holds up far better than one sitting in standing water. We won't promise a number of years, because yards differ.

### Can I use a standard steel well for an egress window?

Only if its dimensions meet the 2018 IRC well requirements: at least 9 square feet of area, at least 36 inches of projection and width, and room for the window to fully open. Many standard sizes are too small, so measure first and confirm with Lincoln Building and Safety.

### Does the well need a ladder?

If the well is deeper than 44 inches, the code requires a permanently attached ladder or steps. Rungs need to be at least 12 inches wide and project at least 3 inches from the wall.

### Will a galvanized well rust?

Eventually the coating wears, especially at cut edges, bolt holes and the bottom of the well. Drainage, a gravel base and careful backfill slow it down.

### Is composite better than steel?

Not always. Composite avoids rust and often looks cleaner, while steel is sturdy, familiar and usually economical. The better choice depends on your wall, your drainage and your budget.
