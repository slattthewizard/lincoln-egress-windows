# Call 811 Before Digging a Window Well in Nebraska

A window well looks like a small hole. It isn't. You're taking out a lot of dirt right next to your foundation, down below the basement window. It's also one of the more common places a shovel, a mini excavator or a trencher meets a buried line, because the utilities that serve your house tend to run right alongside it.

Gas, electric, water, sewer, cable, phone and fiber lines all come into a house somewhere. Some of them cross the exact side of the foundation where a new well wants to go. A free phone call before digging tells you where the public ones are. ## Why a Window Well Dig Is Not Like Planting a Tree

Window well excavation is close to the foundation, deeper than a planting hole, and often wider than people expect. Egress wells have to meet minimum size rules (see [window well size requirements in Nebraska](/blog/window-well-size-requirements-nebraska/); the 2018 IRC calls for at least 9 square feet of horizontal area and at least 36 inches of projection and width, covered in [window well size requirements in Nebraska](/blog/window-well-size-requirements-nebraska/)), so the dig is larger than the small opening it ends in. The excavation also usually reaches the footing zone and may go several feet down.

Buried lines near a house can be shallower than you'd guess. Some are only a short way below the surface. Hitting a gas line can create a leak. Hitting an electric line can injure the person digging. A cut water or sewer line floods the hole and may take the yard apart. A damaged communications line is the lucky outcome, and still a repair bill and an outage.

That's why the one-call system exists.

## What 811 Is and How Nebraska Handles It

811 is the national number for underground utility location. In Nebraska, the one-call center is Nebraska811. You can call 811 or file a request online at [nebraska811.org](https://nebraska811.org/). The service is free to the person digging.

Here is how it works in general terms:

1. You contact Nebraska811 with the address and the area you plan to dig, and what kind of work it is.
2. The center passes your request to the member utilities that have lines near the site.
3. Those utilities send someone to mark the approximate location of their lines, usually with paint, flags or both.
4. You wait until the lines are marked, then dig with care around the marks.

Notice has to be given before digging starts. As a general rule, Nebraska calls for at least two full business days of notice before you dig, not counting weekends and holidays. Rules and details change, so check nebraska811.org for the current requirements, including what counts as a valid ticket and how long it lasts, before you plan the dig.

Build that wait into your schedule. If you call on a Friday afternoon and want to dig Monday morning, that's not enough time.

## Who Is Supposed to Call?

The person doing the digging makes the call. If a homeowner is digging the well, the homeowner calls. If a contractor excavates the well, the contractor calls for that job.

When we dig a well for you, we place the locate request for our excavation. You don't have to file a separate ticket for our work, but do tell us about anything on your property you know about. Ask your contractor, whoever it is, to confirm the ticket is in and the marks are down before the equipment arrives. If a contractor says they don't bother with the call for a small dig near the house, treat that as a warning sign and find someone else.

## What the Marks Mean and What They Don't

The painted marks show the approximate path of the public utilities' lines. A few practical points:

- **Marks are approximate.** Digging within the zone around a mark calls for hand tools or careful methods, not a bucket. Ask the crew how they handle the area near a marked line.
- **Not everything gets marked.** Utilities mark their own lines up to a point, usually the meter or the edge of their ownership. The line from the meter to the house may be a different story. Check with the one-call center on what the participating utilities cover.
- **Marks fade and get disturbed.** Rain, mowing and foot traffic wear them down. If the dig is delayed for a long time, the ticket may need renewal. Look at nebraska811.org for current timing.
- **Take photos** of the marks before the dig, in case there's a question afterward.

## Private Lines: The Ones Nobody Marks

This is where many homeowners get caught. Lines that you own, or that a previous owner put in, generally aren't marked by the utilities. Think of:

- A power feed to a detached garage, shed or workshop
- Water lines to a hose bib, yard hydrant or outbuilding
- Irrigation lines and sprinkler heads
- Buried pet fence wire
- Yard lighting and low-voltage landscape lines
- A gas line to a grill, fire pit or generator
- Drain lines from downspouts or a French drain, and old sump discharge pipes (see [how window well drainage works](/blog/how-window-well-drainage-works/))

A private utility locator can find these. Private locators use electronic equipment to trace buried metal pipes and cables, and some can find non-metallic lines too. If you can't say with confidence what's buried within a few feet of your well location, hire one before the dig. It's a small cost compared with a cut line.

Look for clues as well. Where does the sprinkler valve box sit? Where do the downspouts go? Is there a conduit coming out of the foundation? Old as-built drawings or permit records can help, and so can asking the previous owner.

## What a Contractor Should Do Before Digging

A responsible contractor will:

1. File the Nebraska811 request and wait for the marks.
2. Ask you about private lines and the sprinkler system.
3. Walk the site, compare the marks with what they see (a meter location, a cleanout, a downspout) and confirm the dig plan.
4. Hand dig or use careful methods near marked lines.
5. Stop if something unexpected turns up, such as a line that wasn't marked.

## If You Are Digging It Yourself

Some homeowners dig their own wells. You can, but the same rules apply to you. Call before you dig, wait for the marks, and think hard about private lines. Also think about:

- **Foundation support.** Digging next to a foundation, especially a deep dig, can undermine it. A footing is meant to stay in undisturbed soil. We are not structural engineers, and a significant excavation next to a footing is a good reason to talk to one.
- **Cave-ins.** Clay holds a vertical wall for a while and then lets go. Don't stand in a deep narrow hole alone.
- **Water.** Spring storms can fill an open hole fast.
- **The window itself.** A well has to let the window fully open and meet the size, ladder and drainage rules in the code. See our [window well installation](/window-well-installation-lincoln/) page if you'd rather have it done for you.

## A Short Pre-Dig Checklist

- Decide the wall and the well size.
- File a Nebraska811 request at 811 or nebraska811.org and note the date.
- Wait the required notice period and confirm the marks are down.
- Mark your own private lines, or hire a locator.
- Photograph the marks.
- Dig with care around every mark.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is calling 811 free?

Yes. Contacting Nebraska811 is free for the person digging, whether you call 811 or file online. The member utilities mark their lines at no charge to you.

### How far ahead do I need to call?

Nebraska calls for at least two full business days of notice before digging, not counting weekends and holidays. Check nebraska811.org for current rules before you schedule.

### Does 811 mark the sprinkler system or the line to my garage?

Generally no. Utilities mark their own public lines. Private lines, such as sprinklers, pet fences, yard lights and a feed to a detached garage, are the owner's responsibility. A private locator can trace them.

### Who calls if a contractor digs my window well?

The contractor places the request for their excavation. Ask them to confirm the ticket is in and the lines are marked before they start, and tell them about any private lines you know about.

### Can I skip the call if the hole is small?

No. A small hole near a foundation can still reach buried lines, and the call is free. Make it every time.
