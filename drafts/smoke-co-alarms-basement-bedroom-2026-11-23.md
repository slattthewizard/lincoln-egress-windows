# Smoke and CO Alarm Requirements for a New Basement Bedroom

A basement bedroom project has a few big moving parts: the egress window, the walls, the flooring, the electrical. Alarms tend to get treated as an afterthought, something you pick up at the hardware store the week before the inspection. They deserve more thought than that, because the alarm is what wakes you up in time to use the window.

## Where Smoke Alarms Go

Under IRC section R314, smoke alarms are required in three kinds of places:

- **In each sleeping room.** The alarm goes inside the bedroom itself, not only in the hall.
- **Outside each sleeping area, in the immediate vicinity of the bedrooms.** This is usually the hallway or open space just outside the bedroom door.
- **On each story of the dwelling, including the basement.** The basement counts, finished or not.

So a new basement bedroom needs an alarm in the bedroom, one just outside it, and one on the basement level. If the bedroom opens into a family room, the alarm goes in that room outside the bedroom door. Placement matters too. Manufacturers give guidance about distance from walls, corners and vents, and the instructions that come with the alarm are worth reading before anyone drills a hole.

## Carbon Monoxide Alarms

Carbon monoxide is covered in R315. The rule is not that every bedroom needs one. It's that CO alarms are required outside each sleeping area, in the immediate vicinity of the bedrooms, when the home has either of these:

- **A fuel-fired appliance.** Think gas furnace, gas water heater, gas dryer, a gas fireplace or a wood stove.
- **An attached garage.**

A lot of Lincoln homes have one or both, and basements are exactly where the furnace and water heater tend to live. If your future bedroom shares a floor with a gas furnace, treat the CO alarm as part of the plan from day one. Many alarms are sold as combination smoke and CO units, and an electrician can tell you which kind suits your layout.

A quick note on safety: a CO alarm is not a substitute for a properly vented appliance. If an alarm goes off, get everyone out and call for help.

## When a Permit Triggers Alarm Upgrades

This is the part that surprises people. You may already have alarms. They may be old, battery-only, or in the wrong places. A permit can change what's required.

The model code says that when work requiring a permit is done in an existing home, the alarms have to be installed as they would be in new construction. Adding a bedroom is a good example. Creating a new sleeping room in the basement is work that needs a permit, so the house is expected to have alarms as if it were new.

New construction rules call for alarms that are hardwired to the house power, with battery backup, and interconnected, so that when one sounds, they all sound. That's the reason a fire in the basement can wake someone upstairs.

There is an exception. For alterations that don't remove interior wall or ceiling finishes, the code allows alarms to be battery powered, since running new wire without opening walls can be impractical. But if your project strips drywall, as many basement bedroom projects do, the exception may not apply. How the city reads that for your project is a question for Building and Safety, not for us.

## Interconnection in Plain Language

Interconnected alarms talk to each other. When the one in the basement hallway detects smoke, the one in the upstairs bedroom hears about it and sounds too. This is the single biggest practical benefit of the newer rules for a basement bedroom, because a fire can start on one floor while the people who need to hear it are asleep on another.

Interconnection can be by wire between hardwired units, or by wireless signal in some types of alarm. The code language is "where practical" for existing homes, which is why the details depend on your house. An electrician looking at your walls and panel can say what's practical.

## What This Means for Your Project Timeline

If you're adding a bedroom, here's a sensible order of operations:

1. **Decide the room.** Which room becomes the bedroom, and where does the window go?
2. **Talk to the city.** Ask Building and Safety what your permit will cover and how they treat alarms for your address.
3. **Get the egress window scoped.** We give free written estimates, and we pull the permit for our work. A Lincoln building permit typically runs $75 to $200. See the [cost page](/egress-window-cost/) for typical project figures.
4. **Hire an electrician** for the alarm work, especially if hardwired and interconnected units are needed.
5. **Coordinate the inspections.** The alarms, the window and the room framing often get looked at in the same stretch of the project.

Cut-in egress windows typically take one to three days. The alarm work can happen before or after, but it needs to be done before the final inspection.

## Alarms Do Nothing If You Can't Get Out

Think of the alarm and the window as a pair. The alarm gets you awake, the window gets you out. A bedroom with perfect alarms and a window that's too small or blocked isn't a good bedroom. Your window should have a net clear opening of 5.7 square feet (5.0 for grade-floor openings), at least 24 inches high and 20 inches wide, with a sill no higher than 44 inches above the floor.

Check the other side too. Make sure nothing blocks the well or the cover, and run a drill. The U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission has general information on [smoke and CO alarms](https://www.cpsc.gov/) for homeowners, and the [U.S. Fire Administration](https://www.usfa.fema.gov/) publishes escape-planning guidance.

## Test and Replace What You Install

Alarms aren't forever. They have a life span, usually marked on the unit, and they need regular testing. Press the test button on a schedule, replace batteries when the unit tells you to, and replace the whole alarm when the label says its time is up. Put a date on a sticker if that helps you remember.

When you finish the basement bedroom, write down where each alarm is and when it was installed. It's a small thing that saves a lot of guessing years later.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does a basement bedroom need its own smoke alarm?

Yes. Under R314, smoke alarms go in each sleeping room, outside each sleeping area, and on each story of the home including the basement. A new basement bedroom needs one inside the room and one just outside it.

### Do I need a CO alarm in the basement bedroom?

CO alarms are required outside each sleeping area when the home has a fuel-fired appliance or an attached garage. Many basements have a gas furnace or water heater, so it's likely. Confirm with Lincoln Building and Safety and your electrician.

### Do the alarms have to be hardwired and interconnected?

When a permit is pulled for work like adding a bedroom, alarms are expected to meet new-construction rules, which means hardwired with battery backup and interconnected where practical. There are exceptions for work that doesn't remove interior finishes. Ask the city how it applies to your project.

### Can you install the smoke and CO alarms when you put in the egress window?

No. We install egress windows, wells, covers and ladders. Alarm wiring and interconnection is an electrician's job, and we're not going to claim otherwise. We can work around your electrician's schedule on the window side.

### Does adding an egress window by itself trigger alarm upgrades?

That depends on the permit and on how Building and Safety reads your project. The code ties alarm upgrades to work that requires a permit and to creating a sleeping room. Call the city with your address and a short description of the work, and ask directly.
