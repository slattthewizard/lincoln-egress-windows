Most basement windows in Lincoln-area homes were not sized for escape. They were sized to fit in the strip of concrete wall between the ground outside and the floor joists above. That is why the window you have is probably short and wide, and why it almost certainly does not meet egress rules on its own.

This post is about the windows people actually have: the common sizes, what each style can and cannot do, and how to measure your opening so you can plan a swap or an upgrade. ## The three styles you will find in a basement

Nearly every basement window is one of three types.

**Hopper.** The sash hinges at the bottom and tilts inward from the top. These are the classic small basement window. They are cheap, they seal reasonably well, and they fit in short openings.

**Slider.** The sash slides sideways past a fixed pane. Sliders are common in wells because the sash stays inside the frame and does not swing into the well or into a cover. A retailer guide from RiteScreen puts horizontal sliders at [roughly 32 by 18 inches up to 48 by 24 inches](https://www.ritescreen.com/blog/post/basement-windows-with-screens.html), wider than tall because they have to fit between the foundation wall and the floor structure.

**Casement.** The sash is hinged on one side and cranks outward like a door. Casements are the style most often used when a basement window has to double as an exit.

Awning windows (hinged at the top, open outward) also turn up.

## Common basement window sizes

There is no single standard. Window makers sell a range of sizes, and what fits depends on your rough opening (the framed hole in the wall, not the window sitting in it). The figures below come from published manufacturer and retailer pages, so treat them as typical, not exact.

| Style | Sizes a source lists (inches) | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Small basement windows (older homes, utility basements) | 14 x 18 up to 18 x 24 | [RiteScreen](https://www.ritescreen.com/blog/post/basement-windows-with-screens.html) |
| Medium basement windows (the most common in newer basements, per that source) | 20 x 28 up to 28 x 36 | [RiteScreen](https://www.ritescreen.com/blog/post/basement-windows-with-screens.html) |
| Horizontal slider | 32 x 18 up to 48 x 24 | [RiteScreen](https://www.ritescreen.com/blog/post/basement-windows-with-screens.html) |
| Awning | 24 x 24, 36 x 24, 48 x 24 | [Magic Window](https://www.magicwindow.ca/blog/standard-basement-window-size-a-complete-guide) |
| Sliding | 36 x 24, 36 x 36, 48 x 36 | [Magic Window](https://www.magicwindow.ca/blog/standard-basement-window-size-a-complete-guide) |
| Casement | 24 x 42, 28 x 42, 30 x 48 | [Magic Window](https://www.magicwindow.ca/blog/standard-basement-window-size-a-complete-guide) |

Magic Window is an Ontario window manufacturer, and RiteScreen sells replacement screens. Neither is a Nebraska source, and neither lists a Lincoln-specific "standard." What they agree on is the pattern: the openings people already have run small, and the sizes that suit an exit run larger and taller.

Two cautions before you shop from any chart. First, a nominal size is a label, not a measurement. Frames and sash hardware shrink the glass and the opening you can actually climb through, and Magic Window says the same. Second, a window that is the "right size" to drop into your existing opening is only a good match if the opening is square and the wall is the right thickness. Measure first (more on that below).

## Which sizes can meet egress, and which cannot

Egress is about the net clear opening, meaning the space left when the window is fully open, not the size of the frame. The numbers in the IRC are 5.7 square feet of net clear opening (5.0 at grade-floor openings), at least 24 inches of clear height, at least 20 inches of clear width, and a bottom edge no higher than 44 inches above the finished floor. The retailer Shop Egress Windows [lists the same minimums](https://www.egresswindows.com/its-the-law). For how those numbers play out in a Nebraska bedroom, see [IRC egress window code requirements](/blog/irc-egress-window-code-requirements/).

Convert 5.7 square feet and you get about 820 square inches. That is the target to hold up against every size in the table above.

**Small and medium hoppers. No.** Hoppers are short, and the sash only tilts open part way, so the clear opening ends up well under the frame size. Most never reach 820 square inches of clear opening, and many miss the 24-inch height before area even comes up. The same goes for the 14 x 18 to 28 x 36 range in the table, where the openings are simply too small or too short. Awnings fail for a different reason: Magic Window notes that [awning windows only open to about 45 degrees](https://www.magicwindow.ca/blog/standard-basement-window-size-a-complete-guide), which is not an exit you can crawl through.

**Sliders. Rarely.** Only about half of a slider opens, so you need a big window to get a big clear opening. Say you have a 36 x 36 slider. Half the width is 18 inches, which is already under the 20-inch minimum width, so it fails no matter how you slice it. A 48 x 36 slider gets you close to 24 inches of clear width by 36 inches of height, roughly 860 square inches before the frame takes its share. That is borderline at best, and it is also a lot of wall to find in a basement. Magic Window's own table says a slider [needs a large opening area](https://www.magicwindow.ca/blog/standard-basement-window-size-a-complete-guide) because only half is unobstructed.

**Casements. Often.** A casement swings the whole sash out of the way, so more of the rough opening turns into usable clear opening. Magic Window lists casements as [often egress-compliant, with the unobstructed opening still to be checked](https://www.magicwindow.ca/blog/standard-basement-window-size-a-complete-guide). Check the product's published clear-opening figure for the exact unit, because hinge type and how far the sash swings change the answer.

One detail catches people out: a window can have a legal clear opening and still fail on sill height. If your basement window sits at 50 inches above the floor, it is out regardless of its size. You would need a lower sill, which usually means cutting the opening, a different project from a swap.

## Why the well matters as much as the window

Egress does not stop at the glass. If the window sits below grade, the well in front of it counts too. The IRC calls for a well with at least 9 square feet of area and at least 36 inches of projection and width, and a permanently attached ladder or steps if the well is deeper than 44 inches. A cover or grate has to open from the inside without keys, tools or special knowledge, and with no more force than a normal window takes. The same Shop Egress Windows page [covers the well and ladder language](https://www.egresswindows.com/its-the-law).

So a casement that would work on paper can still be stuck behind a shallow, narrow well. A hopper-sized well will not do. For the details, read [window well size requirements in Nebraska](/blog/window-well-size-requirements-nebraska/) and [window well ladder requirements](/blog/window-well-ladder-requirements/).

## How to measure an existing basement window opening

You only need a tape measure, a pencil and a few minutes. Do this on the inside.

1. **Open the window all the way** and measure the clear gap you could pass through: width, then height. This is a rough read on what you have, not a code measurement.
2. **Measure the sill height** from the finished floor to the bottom of that clear gap. If the floor is not finished yet, use the planned finished floor level.
3. **Measure the frame width and height** from one inside edge of the frame to the other. Do it in three places (top, middle, bottom for width; left, middle, right for height) and write down the smallest number. Basement walls are not always square.
4. **Measure the wall depth**, the thickness of the concrete or block from inside face to outside face. A replacement window has to fit that depth.
5. **Look outside.** Note the well size: width, how far it sticks out from the wall, and depth from the sill to the bottom. Check whether it has a cover and whether the cover would clear a window that swings outward.
6. **Photograph it** with the tape in the shot.

Do not order a window from those numbers alone. Windows are specified to a rough opening, and the rough opening is behind the trim and, in a block wall, the mortar. Confirm the real measurements on site before anything is ordered.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the most common basement window size?

There is no single standard. Retailer and manufacturer pages list a wide spread, from small windows around 14 x 18 inches to medium ones in the 20 x 28 to 28 x 36 range, with horizontal sliders from about 32 x 18 to 48 x 24. Older homes tend to have the smaller sizes, and the only reliable way to know yours is to measure the rough opening.

### Can a basement hopper window be an egress window?

In the sizes people normally have, no. A hopper tilts open only partway, and the common sizes have far less than the 5.7 square feet of net clear opening the IRC requires. If you need an exit, the usual fix is a larger casement or a properly sized slider in a cut-in or enlarged opening.

### What is the minimum size of an egress window?

The IRC requires at least 5.7 square feet of net clear opening (5.0 at grade floor), 24 inches of clear height and 20 inches of clear width, with the bottom of the opening no higher than 44 inches above the floor. The 24 and 20 inch minimums alone do not add up to enough area, so the window has to be larger in at least one direction. See our post on [egress window size requirements in Nebraska](/blog/egress-window-size-requirements-nebraska/) for the detail.

### Is a window well size chart different from a window size chart?

Yes. The window has to meet the clear opening rules, and the well in front of it has to provide at least 9 square feet with 36 inches of projection and width. If the well is deeper than 44 inches, the code requires a permanent ladder or steps. A big enough window in a too-small well still falls short.

### Should I swap my small basement window or cut a new opening?

If you only want better light, air sealing or security, a swap in the existing opening is the lower-cost path. If you want the room to count as a bedroom, the opening has to meet egress rules, and a small, high opening usually means cutting a new one.
