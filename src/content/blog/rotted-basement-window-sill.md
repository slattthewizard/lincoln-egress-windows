You press a screwdriver into the bottom of a basement window and it sinks in like it is cheese. The paint is peeling, the wood is dark, and there may be a musty smell. Now you are searching for rotted window sill replacement and trying to work out whether this is a Saturday repair or a bigger job.

For basement windows, the answer depends on how deep the rot goes and what is behind it. A sill that is only soft on the surface can sometimes be repaired. A sill that sits on a rotted wood frame in the foundation wall usually means the window itself should come out.

## Why Basement Window Sills Rot

Wood rots when it stays wet. Decay fungi need moisture, air, and a food source, and a wood sill gives them two of the three for free. Dry it out and the process stops. Keep it damp and it keeps going.

Basement windows have more ways to stay damp than most:

- **Window well water.** If the well fills with rain, snowmelt, or runoff and the drain is blocked, water sits against the glass and the sill. Our guide to [how window well drainage works](/blog/how-window-well-drainage-works/) covers the cause.
- **Soil and debris against wood.** Leaves, mulch, and dirt piled in the well hold moisture against the frame long after the rain stops. See window well leaf and debris cleanup.
- **Condensation.** A cold basement window in a humid room sweats all winter. The water runs down the glass and pools on the sill. If you see this, read why egress windows get condensation.
- **Wood frames set into concrete or block.** Many older Lincoln homes have wood frames, often called bucks, fastened into the foundation opening. Wood touching masonry wicks moisture from it.
- **Freeze-thaw.** Lincoln winters crack paint and caulk. Water gets in the cracks, freezes, and opens them wider. By spring the wood is taking on water every day.

Clay soils, common around Lincoln, drain slowly. Water that does not leave the well has nowhere else to go but into the window.

## Surface Rot or Frame Failure? How to Tell

This is the question that decides everything. You can check it in about ten minutes with a flashlight and a screwdriver.

**Look first.** Peeling paint, dark staining, and a swollen or cracked sill are all early signs. Black spots and a damp smell point to ongoing moisture, not just old damage.

**Probe the wood.** Push a screwdriver or an awl into the sill, the side jambs, and the frame where it meets the foundation. Sound wood resists and splinters a little. Rotted wood gives way, feels spongy, or crumbles in chunks.

**Check how far it goes.** Probe in three directions: down into the sill, sideways along the jambs, and back toward the wall. Surface rot stays within the top quarter inch or so. Frame failure keeps going.

**Look at the glass and hardware.** If the window sticks, the frame has moved. If the glass is fogged between the panes, the seals have failed. Neither is caused by the sill, but both change the repair-or-replace math.

Here is a rough way to read what you find:

| What you find | What it usually means |
|---|---|
| Peeling paint, wood is firm | Moisture problem, but no structural damage yet |
| Soft spot in the sill only, jambs firm | Localized rot, may be repairable |
| Soft sill and soft lower jambs | Rot has reached the frame |
| Soft wood where the frame meets concrete | Rot is in the buck, window is failing |
| Soft wood plus a gap or movement in the frame | Replace the window |

If the problem is in the first two rows, you have options. If it is in the last three, patching the sill alone treats a symptom.

## When Epoxy Repair Makes Sense

Epoxy wood repair is a legitimate fix, and it is the right tool in a narrow set of cases. You dig out the soft wood, treat what remains with a consolidant that soaks into the fibers, and fill the void with a two-part epoxy filler that cures harder than the surrounding wood. It can be sanded, primed, and painted.

It makes sense when:

- The rot is limited to the sill surface or one small area.
- The surrounding wood is firm when you probe it.
- The frame is still tight in the opening and the window operates normally.
- The cause of the moisture has been fixed or is about to be.

It does not make sense when:

- The rot runs into the jambs or the buck.
- The wood is soft where it touches the foundation.
- Water will keep reaching the window after the repair.
- The window is already failing in other ways, such as fogged glass or a frame that no longer closes.

Epoxy is a hardener, not a waterproofing system. If water still sits against the sill, the wood next to your patch will rot instead. Epoxy repair is also a carpentry task, so we do not do it and have no figure for it. A local carpenter or painter who works with wood repair products can.

## When Replacing the Window Is the Better Move

If the rot has reached the frame, the cheapest repair is rarely the one that holds. A rotted wood buck cannot be patched from the inside because the damage is hidden behind the window and inside the foundation opening. You are sealing over something you cannot see.

Replacing the window means pulling the old unit, cleaning out the opening, and setting a new frame that does not hold water. Most replacement windows for basements are vinyl or fiberglass, neither of which rots. Our guide to [vinyl versus wood egress window frames](/blog/vinyl-vs-wood-egress-window-frames/) goes through the trade-offs.

Window replacement is also a chance to fix a different problem. Many basement windows are too small to count as an emergency exit. If the room is, or could be, a bedroom, check whether the window meets egress requirements before you buy a like-for-like replacement. Our page on egress window versus basement window explains the difference, and the [IRC egress requirements](/blog/irc-egress-window-code-requirements/) list the numbers.

For reference, the IRC asks for a net clear opening of at least 5.7 square feet (5.0 at grade-floor openings), at least 24 inches high and 20 inches wide, with the sill no higher than 44 inches from the floor. A window that is rotting out and also too small is a good candidate for an egress upgrade rather than a straight swap. If the opening is already big enough, that is an [egress window replacement](/egress-window-replacement-lincoln/); if not, the opening has to be enlarged.

## Fix the Water Before You Fix the Wood

A new window in a well that still floods will have the same problem in a few years. The EPA says controlling moisture is the way to control indoor mold, and that wet materials should be dried within 24 to 48 hours to avoid mold growth, as explained in its [mold course chapter on moisture](https://www.epa.gov/mold/mold-course-chapter-2). A sill that stays wet for weeks does the opposite.

Before any repair or replacement, work through this list:

1. **Clear the well.** Remove leaves, soil, and debris down to the bottom.
2. **Check the drain.** Pour a bucket of water in. If it pools and does not leave, the drain is blocked or was never connected. See a leaking window well and a flooded window well.
3. **Look at the grade.** Soil outside should slope away from the well. Our page on window well grading and drainage shows what to check.
4. **Add a cover if the well is open.** A cover keeps rain and snow out. Read window well cover types before you choose one, and make sure any cover on an egress well can be opened from inside without keys or tools.
5. **Fix interior humidity.** If condensation is the cause, a dehumidifier and better air movement help more than any sealant.

If the well is cracked, bowing, or sinking toward the house, that is a replacement job. The wall is probably part of the problem. We cover that in bowing window well walls.

We do not do waterproofing systems, foundation repair, or excavation. If water is coming through the foundation wall itself, or the soil around the house is settling, that needs a waterproofing or foundation contractor. We can replace the window and well once that is dealt with.

## A Note on Mold

Rotted wood in a damp basement often has mold with it. The EPA's [mold cleanup guidance for homes](https://www.epa.gov/mold/mold-cleanup-your-home) says absorbent or porous materials may have to be thrown away if they become moldy, and it suggests hiring an experienced contractor when mold covers more than about 10 square feet. If you see growth well beyond the sill, deal with that separately from the window. We replace the window, not the mold.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I just replace the sill and keep the window?

Sometimes, on an upstairs window with a separate wood sill. Basement windows are different because the sill is often part of the frame or the buck set into the foundation. If the rot is limited to a removable sill piece and the frame is firm, a carpenter may be able to swap it. If the frame is soft or the window is old and undersized, replacing the whole unit usually holds up better.

### Does epoxy actually fix a rotted window sill?

It can, when the rot is shallow and localized. Epoxy consolidant and filler harden damaged wood and rebuild its shape. It does not stop the moisture that caused the rot, so the water source has to be fixed too. We do not do epoxy repair ourselves; a carpenter or painter who works with these products can.

### Is a rotted sill a sign my window well is failing?

Often it is a sign the well is holding water. A blocked drain, debris, or poor grading keeps the window wet. Check the drain and the soil slope first. If the well walls are cracked or leaning, replacing the well is a better fix than patching the window.

### Do I need a permit to replace a rotted basement window?

It depends on the work. A like-for-like swap in an existing opening is different from enlarging the opening or converting to an egress window. We pull the permit when the job needs one, and our guide to the Lincoln and Lancaster County egress permit explains the process.

### Can you repair rotted sills on my upstairs windows?

No. We work on basement windows, egress windows, and window wells. For main-floor or second-floor windows, siding, and trim, a finish carpenter or window repair company is the right call.
