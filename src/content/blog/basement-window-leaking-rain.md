Water on the sill, a damp stripe down the wall, a dark ring on the carpet under the window. A basement window leaking during a storm looks the same no matter what causes it, but the cause changes everything about the fix. A full window well, a dried-out frame seal, a cracked concrete block and a foundation crack that happens to run through the window opening can all produce the same puddle.

You can usually narrow it down yourself in an hour with a flashlight, a hose and a dry rag. Below are five usual causes in the order worth checking, and who handles each. ## Start With When and Where the Water Shows Up

Note how hard it rained before the leak appeared, where the water first shows (bottom of the frame, the wall beside it, or the floor a few inches out), and whether it stops within an hour of the rain stopping. A leak that only appears in a downpour and stops quickly points toward a well that fills faster than it drains. Seepage that continues for a day or two points toward saturated soil.

## Cause 1: The Window Well Fills Up and Water Gets Over the Sill

This is the first thing to rule out when the leak shows at the bottom of the window. A window well is a bucket set against your foundation. If the drain at the bottom is clogged, missing or just too small, rain collects. Once the water level climbs past the bottom of the window frame, it finds the lowest point and pushes in.

University of Minnesota Extension describes the problem plainly: window wells [act like a drain right next to the basement wall](https://extension.umn.edu/moisture-and-mold-indoors/moisture-basements-causes-and-solutions) and are often built so that water flows toward the foundation. Their recommended fix includes filling the well with coarse aggregate from the footing up to the window sill and adding a drain tile extension from the footing to the base of the well.

**How to test it:** On a dry day, look for standing water, mud, leaves or silt at the bottom of the well. Pour in a bucket of water. If it is still sitting there twenty minutes later, the well is not draining. Debris piled up against the glass gives water a direct path to the frame.

If this is your cause, the well itself is the problem, not the window. We cover that side of it in [why a window well leaks after rain](/blog/leaking-window-well/) and in [what to do when the drain is clogged](/blog/window-well-drain-clogged/). A badly rusted, bowed or undersized well sometimes needs replacing, and a window well replacement typically runs $600-$1,500 (see our [egress window cost page](/egress-window-cost/) for what moves that number).

## Cause 2: The Seal Between the Window Frame and the Opening Has Failed

If the well is dry and clean and water still shows up at the sill, look at the perimeter of the window itself. Most basement windows sit in a rough opening in the foundation wall, and the joint between the frame and that opening is sealed with caulk or sealant. That joint takes a beating: it sits at ground level, it gets wet from the outside, and it swells and shrinks with Nebraska's freeze-thaw cycle. After enough winters, the sealant shrinks, cracks or pulls away.

**How to test it:** Look for gaps, cracks or dark voids in the sealant on the outside of the frame. Run a finger along the inside edge of the frame on a dry day. Rust stains on a metal frame, white mineral crust, or soft, dark wood on a wood frame all say water has been getting in there for a while.

A hose test narrows it further. Do this with a helper inside the basement and a dry rag on the sill:

1. Spray the lower edge of the frame and the well wall around it for several minutes. Stay away from the top of the window at first.
2. If nothing shows inside, move the spray up the sides, then the top of the frame.
3. Have your helper call out the moment water appears, and note exactly where it appears.

Skip the pressure washer, which forces in water that real rain would not.

A worn sealant joint is a maintenance fix. A frame that has rotted or rusted through is a replacement job. ## Cause 3: A Cracked or Crumbling Buck or Sill

The "buck" is the frame set into the foundation around the window opening. In poured concrete it is usually a wood or metal form that stays in place; in block foundations the window sits in a notched block or has a concrete or mortar sill under it. Over time, these edges crack and crumble, especially at the bottom corners where water sits.

**How to check it:** Look at the concrete or mortar directly around the window from both sides. Hairline cracks that run from the corners of the window outward are common. Crumbling mortar, loose pieces at the sill, or a gap between the buck and the surrounding concrete is a path for water. If a screwdriver tip sinks into a wood buck, it is rotted.

A cracked buck is not the same as a foundation crack, even though they can look similar. A buck problem stays at the window. A crack that continues up or down the wall past the opening is a different problem, covered below.

Replacing a rotted wood buck can sometimes be part of a window replacement. Repairing the foundation itself is outside what we do.

## Cause 4: A Foundation Crack That Runs Through the Window Opening

Window openings are weak points in a foundation wall. The corners concentrate stress, so a crack that starts at a window corner and runs diagonally up or down is common in settled clay soils.

**How to tell if it is this:** Trace the water back. If it comes from a crack that continues well beyond the window frame (a stair-step crack in block, a vertical or diagonal crack in poured concrete), the window is just where the crack happens to cross. Seal the window perfectly and the water will still come in six inches away.

We do not do foundation repair or waterproofing systems, so this is a call for a foundation or waterproofing contractor or a structural engineer. A crack wider than a hairline, a growing crack, or one that comes with a bowing wall deserves a professional look before you spend money on anything else.

## Cause 5: It Is Not a Leak. It Is Condensation.

A basement stays cool because it is mostly underground. In summer, warm, humid air coming through the window or from upstairs meets cool glass and cool walls, and water forms on the surface. In winter, warm indoor air condenses on cold glass and a single-pane or old aluminum frame can run with water. Neither has anything to do with rain, though both are more likely on muggy, stormy days when the outside air is wet.

University of Minnesota Extension notes that in summer, humid outside air entering through basement windows can condense on cool walls and floors, and homeowners may assume the wall is leaking [when the moisture is actually condensation](https://extension.umn.edu/moisture-and-mold-indoors/moisture-basements-causes-and-solutions).

**Clues it is condensation:**

- The water is on the inside face of the glass and frame, not coming from outside the frame.
- It shows up on warm, humid days or cold mornings regardless of whether it rained.
- The window is wet along the whole glass surface, not just the bottom edge.

**A simple test:** Dry the glass and the sill completely with a rag. Tape a square of aluminum foil, shiny side out, over a patch of the wall or sill beside the window, and tape a second square to the glass. Check both the next morning. Water on the room-facing surface of the foil means indoor air is condensing there. If the foil is dry but the sill under it is wet, water is arriving from behind or below. This test is rough, but it is cheap.

If it is condensation, fixing the window will not fix the water. You need to lower indoor humidity and keep warm, damp air away from cool surfaces. We cover that in [egress window condensation](/blog/egress-window-condensation/).

## What To Do While You Sort It Out

Whatever the cause, a few things are worth doing right away.

**Dry the area.** The EPA's mold guidance says to [clean and dry wet or damp spots within 48 hours](https://www.epa.gov/mold/mold-remediation-schools-and-commercial-buildings-guide-chapter-2), and notes that moisture control is the key to mold control. Pull wet carpet back from the wall, run a fan, and move stored boxes off the floor.

**Check outside.** The same Extension article says ground should slope away from the foundation wall at least 1 inch per foot for at least 6 feet, and that downspouts should discharge at least 4 feet from the wall. If a downspout dumps next to your window well, no amount of window work will keep the well dry. Our post on [grading and drainage outside the window well](/blog/window-well-grading-drainage-outside/) goes through what to look for.

**Do not just caulk over it.** Fresh sealant over a wet or failing joint tends to trap water behind it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Why does my basement window only leak during heavy rain?
Usually because the window well fills faster than it drains. A clogged or missing drain, a well packed with leaves or silt, or ground that slopes toward the house all let water rise past the bottom of the frame in a hard rain. In light rain the same well keeps up, so the leak never shows.

### Can I fix a leaking basement window with caulk?
Sometimes, if the only problem is a worn sealant joint around a sound frame. Remove the old sealant, let the area dry fully, and use a sealant made for exterior masonry. Caulk will not help if the well is filling with water, the frame is rotted, or the water is coming through a crack in the wall.

### How do I know if it is condensation or a real leak?
Condensation forms on the room side of the glass and frame, shows up on humid or cold days whether or not it rained, and usually wets the whole pane. A leak shows up after rain, starts at the sill or frame edge, and often leaves rust, mineral crust or staining. Drying the area and checking it again the next morning, with and without rain, is the quickest way to tell.

### When is a leaking basement window a foundation problem?
When the water follows a crack that runs past the window, when the crack is wider than a hairline or growing, or when the wall around the window is bowing. Those need a foundation or waterproofing contractor or a structural engineer, not a window installer. If the crack stays at the window corners and the wall is otherwise sound, resealing the opening during a window and well replacement may solve it.

### Should I replace the window or the window well first?
Fix whatever lets water reach the window first. If the well fills with water, a new window will leak again. If the well drains properly and the frame is rotted or the seal is gone, replace the window. If both are failing, doing them together is often simpler than doing them in two visits.
