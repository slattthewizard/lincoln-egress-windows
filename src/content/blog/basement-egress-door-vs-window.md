Every sleeping room in a basement needs a way out that does not depend on the stairs. The code does not say that way out has to be a window. An exterior door at the bottom of a set of steps can do the same job, and on some houses it is the better answer. On most Lincoln houses with a standard basement, though, it is the harder one.

This page walks through what a basement egress door involves, how it differs from a window and well, and how to tell which route fits your foundation. ## What the code lets you use for escape

The IRC requires an emergency escape and rescue opening in basements and in every sleeping room, and each basement bedroom needs its own. (An existing basement that is only being remodeled does not need a new one unless a new sleeping room is created.) The opening can be a window or a door that leads directly outside. Either one has to be usable from the inside without keys, tools or special knowledge.

For windows, the numbers are the familiar ones: 5.7 square feet of net clear opening (5.0 at grade floor), at least 24 inches of clear height, at least 20 inches of clear width, and a sill no higher than 44 inches from the floor. For the full list, see our [IRC egress window code breakdown](/blog/irc-egress-window-code-requirements/) and the Nebraska size requirements.

A door has its own rules. Under [IRC R310.3](https://up.codes/viewer/nebraska/irc-2018/chapter/3/building-planning#R310.3), an escape door has to be a side-hinged door or a slider, and it has to meet the same minimum net clear opening as a window. If it sits below the adjacent grade, it needs an area well at least 36 inches wide, sized so the door can open fully, with steps or a ladder once the well gets deep. In plain terms, the path from the door to open air has to be usable: wide enough, drained, and not blocked by a cover you cannot lift.

## Three kinds of "egress door"

People search for "basement egress door" and mean three different things.

**A walkout door.** If your lot slopes and part of the basement wall is above grade, a regular exterior door at ground level is the simplest escape there is. That situation is covered in [walkout and daylight basement egress](/blog/walkout-daylight-basement-egress/). Nothing to dig, no steps.

**A bulkhead or hatchway door.** This is the sloped steel or fiberglass door you see on the outside of older houses, with a short staircase underneath. Bilco is the best-known maker, and its site says its basement products provide "access, light, ventilation and code-compliant emergency egress" ([Bilco](https://bilco.com/)). Compliance depends on how the unit is installed and kept. A hatch that is bolted shut, buried under a deck, or opens onto a stair full of dirt gives you nothing.

**An areaway with steps and a door.** This is a sunken, walled stairwell outside the foundation with a door at the bottom that opens into the basement. It is a walkout built downward into level ground.

## What an egress door project involves

The reason doors are less common than windows is the work around the door, not the door.

**Excavation.** A door needs a full-height opening in the foundation wall, usually down to the basement floor. Then you need a stairwell outside it. That means digging a hole several feet deep and wide enough for steps, possibly right next to a footing. Digging in Nebraska starts with a locate: [Nebraska 811](http://nebraska811.com/) says you must request one at least two full business days before you dig, and it does not locate privately owned lines, such as a buried sprinkler or gas line to a grill. [OSHA](https://www.osha.gov/trenching-excavation) calls cave-ins the greatest risk in trench work, which is one reason deep digging beside a foundation is a job for an excavation contractor.

**Structural work.** Removing a section of foundation wall down to the floor takes out more concrete or block than a window cut-in does. The wall above needs a properly sized header or lintel, and that is a design question for a structural engineer. We do not do structural design, excavation or foundation repair.

**Stairs and walls.** The areaway needs retaining walls (poured concrete, block or precast units) plus steps with a code-compliant rise and run, and a guard if the drop is tall enough to need one. Freeze-thaw cycles and Lincoln's clay soil push on those walls every year, so footings and backfill matter.

**Drainage.** A stairwell is a bucket. Rain and snowmelt run into it, and the door sill sits at basement floor level. The bottom needs a drain tied to the foundation drainage or a sump, and a cover or grading plan that keeps water out. It is the problem described in [how window well drainage works](/blog/how-window-well-drainage-works/), at a larger scale, with a door threshold that can leak.

**A bigger permit.** A cut-in window is a modest permit. A new foundation opening with stairs and retaining walls usually means more drawings and more review. Start with [Lincoln Building and Safety](https://www.lincoln.ne.gov/City/Departments/Building-and-Safety) for what they require, and see our [Lincoln and Lancaster County permit guide](/blog/lincoln-lancaster-county-egress-permit/) for how the simpler window permit works.

## Where a window and well win

A cut-in egress window needs one opening, sized to the window, and a well outside it. The well is a small, shallow excavation. The code asks for a well area of at least 9 square feet with at least 36 inches of projection and width, and a ladder or steps once the well is deeper than 44 inches. Our window well size guide has the details.

On a typical Lincoln house, the window route usually wins when:

- The basement is below grade on every side, with no slope to work with.
- You need one or two rooms to meet the code, such as a bedroom.
- The foundation wall is poured concrete or block that can take a cut-in. See [block vs poured foundation egress](/blog/block-vs-poured-foundation-egress/).
- The yard on that side is tight: a driveway, a deck, a fence, or a utility run close to the house.
- You would rather keep the wall mostly intact.

For scale on the window side, an egress window replacement in an existing opening runs $1,200 to $3,000, a new cut-in $3,500 to $7,000, and a full Lincoln project with well and interior finish $5,500 to $9,500. The Lincoln building permit runs $75 to $200. The breakdown is on our [egress window cost page](/egress-window-cost/). We do not price doors, stairwells or retaining walls, and a door project is a different class of quote.

## Where a door makes sense

A door is worth serious thought when:

- Your lot already slopes toward a back or side yard, so part of the basement could open at grade.
- You want to move furniture or equipment in and out of the basement without going through the house.
- You are finishing a large part of the basement as living space and want a real outside entrance.
- You have an old hatchway you could restore, rather than digging a new one.

If any of that fits, ask a general contractor who does foundation openings, and an excavation contractor, for quotes on the door. Get a structural engineer involved before anyone cuts concrete. Many homeowners collect a bid for the door route and one for the window route, then compare cost, access and how long the yard will be torn up.

## Check an existing hatchway before you count on it

Plenty of older houses have a bulkhead door that someone assumed was the basement's way out. Test it honestly:

- Can you open it from inside, alone, without a key, a tool, or moving something?
- Is the stair clear, dry and complete, with no rotted treads?
- Is there room to climb out into open air, not into a garden bed, deck frame or snow drift?
- Does it drain, or does it hold water?

Even a hatch that passes does not make a basement room a legal bedroom by itself, because other requirements apply too, including ceiling height and smoke alarms. See [what counts as a legal bedroom in Nebraska](/blog/what-counts-as-a-legal-bedroom-nebraska/). We can describe what the code requires, not how a given house will be judged.

## Deep window wells that act like small areaways

There is a middle path. A deeper window well with fixed steps, paired with an egress window, can give you much of what a door gives without moving the foundation. Covers matter here: an emergency escape cover has to open from inside without keys, tools or special knowledge, and with no more force than a normal window. Our guides to well covers and ladders explain what to look for.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does a basement exterior door count as egress?
It can. The IRC lets an emergency escape and rescue opening be a window or a door that opens directly to the outside. The door has to open from the inside without keys, tools or special knowledge, and the stairs or areaway leading out have to be usable. A door that opens into a blocked or flooded stairwell does not do the job in practice.

### Is a Bilco door good enough for egress?
Bilco states that its basement products provide code-compliant emergency egress. Whether yours does depends on the model, how it was installed, and the condition of the stairs and drainage beneath it. For an old unit, check that it opens easily from inside and confirm the details with the city before you rely on it.

### Is an egress door cheaper than an egress window?
Usually not. A door needs a larger foundation opening, an excavated stairwell, retaining walls, steps and drainage, so the scope is much bigger than a window and well. We do not price doors, but our window ranges are on the [egress window cost page](/egress-window-cost/) so you can compare.

### Do you install basement egress doors?
No. We install egress windows and window wells. Doors, stairwells and retaining walls are foundation, concrete and excavation work, and an engineer should design the wall opening. We are glad to quote the window route for comparison.

### Can I use a walkout door as the bedroom egress?
Only if the door is in the bedroom itself. Each basement sleeping room needs its own escape opening, so a walkout door in the family room does not cover a bedroom down the hall. A door that opens from the bedroom straight outside can serve, as long as it opens easily from inside. The room still has to meet the other bedroom requirements, such as ceiling height and smoke alarms.
