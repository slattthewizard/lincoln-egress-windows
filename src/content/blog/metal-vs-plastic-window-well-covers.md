## Metal vs Plastic Window Well Covers: The Real Tradeoff

Every window well cover on the market is some version of two families. Steel or aluminum grates are welded bar mesh, often powder-coated, built to be walked on. Polycarbonate or acrylic domes and flat panels are molded clear or tinted plastic, built to be seen through.

Neither one is universally better. A grate outlasts a dome in a yard with a riding mower and two dogs. A dome lets afternoon light into a basement bedroom that a solid grate never will. The right pick depends on which of the tradeoffs below actually matters for your house.

One rule cuts across every material choice: if the well sits under an egress bedroom window, the cover has to release from the inside without a key, a tool, or any special knowledge. That single requirement rules out padlocks, bolted-down grates, and anything that needs a wrench, no matter how good the material looks on a shelf.

## Light Transmission Into a Basement Bedroom

If the room behind the window is a bedroom, an office, or anywhere someone spends real time, light matters more than most homeowners expect going in.

Clear polycarbonate and acrylic domes let daylight reach the window almost unobstructed, since there's nothing between the glass and the sky but molded plastic. Tinted versions cut some glare but still pass far more light than metal.

Steel and aluminum grates block a meaningful share of that light. The bars themselves shade the window, and even a wide-gap grate casts a pattern across the glass for most of the day. In a basement bedroom that already gets less natural light than the rest of the house, that difference is noticeable within the first week of living with it.

A homeowner finishing a basement bedroom in Crete told us the grate cover on the model home next door made the room feel more like a stairwell than a bedroom. She chose a bubble-style acrylic dome instead, in the $180 to $260 range, and kept the metal option for a utility window well on the other side of the house where light didn't matter.

## Snow and Ice Load: Which Cover Holds Up in a Nebraska Winter

Southeast Nebraska stacks heavy wet snow, sudden thaws, and hard refreezes into the same six months. A cover has to carry that load without collapsing into the well.

Steel and aluminum grates are rated for real weight. A quality metal cover holds up under a foot of wet snow, an ice sheet, or someone briefly stepping on it while shoveling nearby, without flexing enough to crack.

Polycarbonate and acrylic domes flex under load by design, which is part of how they shed snow. But repeated freeze and thaw cycles work at the plastic's edges and fasteners, and a dome that already has UV damage can crack under a load it would have shrugged off when new.

A Beatrice homeowner replaced a five-year-old acrylic dome after a February ice storm caved in one side while the cover was still buried under snow from the week before. The dome had gone slightly cloudy from sun exposure the summer before, and that brittleness is likely what let it fail instead of flexing. A new metal grate cover, sized for the well and rated for snow load, ran $310 installed.

## UV Yellowing, Brittleness, and Rust at the Fasteners

Both materials have a slow failure mode. It's just a different one for each.

Polycarbonate and acrylic break down under ultraviolet light. A clear cover that looked brand new in May can turn cloudy and yellow by its third or fourth Nebraska summer, and yellowing is a warning sign, not just a cosmetic issue. Yellowed plastic has usually lost a real share of its impact strength, which is why an old dome can shatter from a fall that a new one would absorb.

Steel and aluminum don't yellow, but they're on their own clock. Even galvanized or powder-coated steel rusts eventually, especially at the screws, hinges, and clips that hold the cover to the well frame. Rust in a fastener swells it slightly and can seize the hinge shut. That's exactly the failure you can't afford on an egress cover.

A homeowner in Ashland found this out during a home inspection ahead of a sale. The metal cover over her basement bedroom's window looked fine from the yard, but the hinge had rusted enough that the inspector couldn't lift it without pliers. Swapping the hardware and adding a stainless replacement cover cost her $265, cheap compared to the closing delay it prevented.

## The Egress Rule That Decides Several of These Tradeoffs

This is the rule that overrides personal preference: an egress window well cover has to be openable from inside the house, by the person escaping, without a key, a tool, or any special knowledge. That's a code requirement, not a suggestion, and it applies no matter which material you choose.

For plastic covers, this usually isn't the problem. A polycarbonate dome or flat panel is light enough that most adults, teenagers, and older kids can push it free from underneath with one hand, even after some age-related stiffness sets in.

For metal covers, this is where rust turns into a safety issue instead of a cosmetic one. A grate that hinges cleanly is fine. A grate that's been screwed down, padlocked for security, or left to rust at the hinge for years can turn into something a first responder or a person escaping can't move fast enough. Go with metal, and the hinge and release mechanism deserve as much attention as the bar spacing.

Either material can meet the rule when it's installed and maintained correctly. Neither one meets it automatically just by being the "safer-looking" option.

## Security Against Someone Climbing In, Without Locking Anyone In

The same opening that has to let someone out has to keep an unwanted visitor from getting in, and that pulls the two goals in opposite directions.

Metal grates read as more secure because they look harder to force, and a welded steel grate really is tougher to pry open from outside than a plastic dome. That toughness only holds up if the release mechanism inside stays smooth. A grate that's bolted down tight enough to stop an intruder is often too tight for a homeowner to release quickly in an emergency. That's the tradeoff a security-minded metal buyer needs to watch.

Polycarbonate and acrylic domes are easier to release from inside, which is good for escape and less good for keeping someone out. A determined person can crack an aging, sun-brittled dome faster than they can defeat a metal grate. A newer, impact-rated polycarbonate cover closes most of that gap.

The safest approach we see in Lincoln yards is a cover with an interior release that needs no tool, paired with a latch that resists outside tampering but doesn't require force to open from inside. That combination exists in both materials. It just has to be specified, not assumed. The [Consumer Product Safety Commission](https://www.cpsc.gov) publishes general guidance on window well hazards worth a look if young kids or pets use the yard.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can a metal window well cover rust shut and trap someone inside?

Yes. If the hinge or latch corrodes without maintenance, a metal cover can bind tight enough that it won't release quickly in an emergency. This is more likely on unpainted or poorly galvanized steel near a well that holds moisture. A yearly check that the cover still lifts freely catches this before it becomes a safety problem.

### Does a metal grate cover let enough light into a basement bedroom?

Not as much as a clear plastic dome. The bars shade the window for most of the day, which can make an already dim basement bedroom feel darker. If natural light matters for the room behind the well, a polycarbonate or acrylic cover is usually the better fit.

### Will a plastic dome cover crack under snow load in Nebraska?

A newer polycarbonate or acrylic dome flexes under typical snow loads without cracking. Risk goes up once the plastic has yellowed from a few summers of UV exposure, since brittle plastic loses the flexibility that let it handle weight when new. Replacing a visibly cloudy or yellowed dome before winter avoids this.

### Which costs less to replace, a metal or plastic window well cover?

Plastic covers are usually cheaper, typically $150 to $350 installed compared to $250 to $500 for a metal grate on a standard-size well. Custom sizing or a well that needs frame repair pushes either material toward the higher end of that $150 to $600 range.
