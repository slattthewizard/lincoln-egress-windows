A window well bubble cover is the clear, arched plastic lid you see over basement windows all over Lincoln. A flat cover is the same idea with no arch. Sloped covers sit somewhere between, pitched in one direction like a small roof. They all keep leaves out and kids and pets from dropping in. They do not behave the same way under a February snow load or a July sun.

This post compares the three shapes on the things that matter here: snow, weight, light, fit on an egress well, UV yellowing and cold-weather cracking. ## The three shapes, plainly

**Bubble or dome cover.** A curved lid that rises a few inches above the well rim. Most are clear or lightly tinted polycarbonate in a metal or plastic frame.

**Flat cover.** A level panel, usually polycarbonate or corrugated plastic, that sits on or just above the rim. It is the cheapest to make and the simplest to fit.

**Sloped cover.** A panel that rises from the house wall out toward the yard (or the reverse), so water and snow run off one side. You see these in metal and in polycarbonate.

One point first. Shape is only part of the picture. Material and thickness matter as much, and a thin dome can fail faster than a thick flat panel. Keep that in mind through everything below.

## Snow shedding

Snow is the big difference between the shapes.

A flat cover is a shelf. Snow lands on it and stays until it melts. A well that is open to the sky gets the same snow, but the snow has somewhere to go: down into the well, where it melts slowly against the foundation and your window. A flat lid holds that weight up top instead.

A dome sheds some of it. Light, dry snow slides off the curve, and wind helps. Wet, heavy snow and drifted snow do not slide much. Anyone in Lincoln who has shoveled a spring storm knows that wet snow sticks to nearly everything.

Sloped covers shed best in one direction, as long as the low edge is clear. If a drift or a pile from your shoveling sits against that low edge, the cover loses its advantage.

Practical read: a dome or slope will carry less snow than a flat lid of the same size, but none of them replaces brushing off a heavy load. Do not assume a clear plastic cover is built for foot traffic or a snow bank. Check what the maker says. For example, [Rockwell's polycarbonate cover page](https://rockwellinc.com/window-well-products/polycarbonate-window-well-cover) lists a 500 lb figure for that product, and also notes that its Cascade 42 polycarbonate covers are not load bearing. Two products from one company, two different answers. Read the spec for the exact model you are buying.

## Load: what each shape can hold

Load is about span and support, not just shape.

- **Arch.** A curve carries load out to the frame, so a dome of a given thickness usually resists a distributed load (snow) better than a flat sheet of the same thickness over the same width.
- **Flat panel.** Bends in the middle. The wider the well, the more it flexes. Flexing is what cracks plastic over time.
- **Point loads.** A dog that jumps on the lid, a kid who climbs it, a ladder leaned against it. Polycarbonate covers are generally meant to keep people and pets from falling in, not to be stood on. Most are not rated to be walked on. If foot traffic is a real possibility, look at a walk-on grate instead. We cover that tradeoff in [metal vs plastic window well covers](/blog/metal-vs-plastic-window-well-covers/).

Wider wells need more support under any shape. A 5-foot-wide well with a thin flat lid and no center bar is asking for trouble.

## Light

This is where clear covers earn their spot. A clear dome or flat panel lets daylight through to a basement window. If that window serves a bedroom or a family room, the light matters a lot.

A few differences:

- Flat clear panels pass light well when clean. Pollen, dust, leaf stains and mineral spots cut it down. A flat lid collects them more because water sits on it.
- Domes tend to stay a bit cleaner because rain runs off, but the curve can create glare and a slightly distorted view from inside.
- Tinted and fluted (ribbed) polycarbonate diffuses light. You get less brightness and more privacy. Clear gives the most light and the most view in.
- Metal grates and solid sloped covers let in the least.

If light is the whole point of the window, the cover's cleanliness over time matters more than the day-one look. Plan to rinse it twice a year.

## Fit on an egress well

An egress well is not just any well. The code Lincoln enforces follows the IRC, which sets the window opening and the well around it: the well needs at least 9 square feet of floor area with a 36 inch minimum projection and width, and a ladder or steps if the well is deeper than 44 inches. Our post on [window well size requirements in Nebraska](/blog/window-well-size-requirements-nebraska/) walks through the numbers, and [window well ladder requirements](/blog/window-well-ladder-requirements/) covers the ladder side.

The cover has to fit those rules too. The IRC says a cover or grate over an emergency escape well must open without keys, tools or special knowledge, and with no more force than a normal window needs. That affects the shape you choose in a few ways.

**Clearance for the window.** An egress window swings or slides open into the well. A flat cover that sits tight to the rim can block a casement or an inswing sash. A dome gives more headroom. If your window is a casement or inswing, measure the swing before you pick a cover.

**Release.** Covers on egress wells need to open from inside. [Rockwell's polycarbonate covers page](https://rockwellinc.com/window-well-products/polycarbonate-window-well-covers) says its covers are "easy to open for egress routes," though it does not describe the release. Whatever you choose, look for a latch you can work with one hand and no padlocks. A cover that someone can lift only by finding a hidden clip is a problem in a fire.

**Weight.** A heavy cover is harder to push open from below. Think about whether a child or older adult could push that up from inside the well.

**Exact size.** Covers are made to standard well widths and depths. A cover that overhangs a rim gap lets in leaves and water, and one that is too small can slip off. Measure before you buy: [how to measure a window well cover](/blog/how-to-measure-window-well-cover/).

A well cover or ladder add-on typically runs $150-$600.

## UV yellowing

Polycarbonate left in Nebraska sun without UV protection yellows and turns cloudy over time. The plastic also becomes more brittle as it ages. That is true of clear plastic in general, not just this product.

The fix is a UV-protected material. Rockwell states on its polycarbonate cover page that the polycarbonate is UV protected. Look for that statement, or for a UV coating, on any cover you buy, and ask how it is applied. If the coating is on one face only, that face has to point up.

Shape adds to the problem. A dome faces more sky and gets more sun hours than a flat cover in a shaded well. A cover on the south or west side of a house takes the most. North-side wells age slowest.

If a cover has already yellowed, replacing it is usually the answer. Polishing can improve the look but does not restore strength.

## Cracking in Nebraska cold

Lincoln winters go from a mild day to a hard freeze fast. Plastic expands and contracts with temperature, and a lid held rigidly in a frame has no room to move. Cold also makes plastic less flexible.

What typically breaks covers:

1. **Snow and ice load on a thin panel.** A flat or sagging lid cracks along a seam.
2. **Impact on cold plastic.** A hailstone, a dropped shovel or a kick hurts more at 5 degrees than at 65.
3. **Aged, UV-damaged plastic.** An old cover that survived five summers can fail in its first hard freeze.
4. **Over-tightened screws.** A hole drilled too snug or a screw cranked down leaves no room for expansion and starts a crack.

A dome handles flex better than a flat panel because the curve spreads the stress. A thicker panel or a ribbed (fluted) design resists impact better. None of these makes a cover crack-proof. ## Which should you pick?

| Situation | Better fit |
| --- | --- |
| Egress well with a casement or inswing window | Dome (headroom for the sash) |
| Wide well in an area that gets deep snow | Dome or sloped, with support |
| Shallow well, no foot traffic, tight budget | Flat |
| Well near a path, play area or pets that jump | Walk-on grate instead |
| Well that is mostly shaded and used for light | Clear flat or dome, UV protected |

A dome is often the safer all-around choice for an egress well, because of the extra clearance and better snow behavior. A flat cover is fine where the well is small, shaded and not under a drift line.

Do not pick on shape alone. Ask about UV protection, thickness, the rated load for the exact model, and how the cover opens from inside.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is a bubble cover better than a flat cover for snow?

Usually, yes. A curve sheds light snow and spreads weight toward the frame, while a flat lid holds it. Heavy wet snow and drifts stick to either shape, so you still need to brush off a large load.

### Do bubble window well covers yellow?

Polycarbonate without UV protection can yellow and turn cloudy in the sun. Look for a cover that states it is UV protected, as Rockwell does for its polycarbonate cover. Even protected covers age, so check yours each spring.

### Can I walk on a clear polycarbonate cover?

Do not assume so. Many clear covers are meant to keep people and pets from falling in, not to carry foot traffic, and some are labeled not load bearing. Check the rating for your exact model, or use a walk-on grate where people or animals will cross the well.

### Does the cover on an egress window well have to open from inside?

Yes. The IRC says covers and grates over an emergency escape well must open without keys, tools or special knowledge and without more force than a normal window. Avoid padlocks, and pick a cover light enough that you could push it up from the bottom of the well.

### Why do window well covers crack in winter?

Cold plastic is stiffer, so it takes snow weight and impacts worse than warm plastic. Age and UV damage make it worse, and screws set too tight leave no room for the cover to expand and contract. A thicker or ribbed cover and a looser fit usually help.
