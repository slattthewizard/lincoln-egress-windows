The bottom of a window well looks like the least important part. It is just a patch of stone under a window. But that stone does a job. It is the layer that lets water leave the well instead of sitting against your basement window, and in Lincoln, with clay soil that drains slowly and freezes every winter, a well with the wrong bottom fills up fast.

This article covers what window well gravel is for, what size and type to use, how deep it should be, and how it connects to the drain and the footing. ## What the Gravel Is Actually For

The stone in and around a window well has three jobs.

**It gives water somewhere to go.** Rain and snowmelt that land in the well need a path down. Clean, coarse stone has big gaps between the pieces, so water drops through it quickly instead of pooling on top.

**It carries water to the drain.** In a properly built well, the stone sits over or around a drain connection that leads to the foundation drainage system. The stone keeps soil and debris from plugging the opening. For more on that connection, see [how window well drainage works](/blog/how-window-well-drainage-works/).

**It keeps soil off the well walls.** Stone placed around the outside of the well (the backfill) reduces the pressure of wet soil pushing on it. Clay holds water, swells when wet, and freezes. Packing clay tight against a plastic or steel well is how walls end up leaning. We cover that failure in [bowing window well walls](/blog/bowing-window-well-wall/).

## The Code Piece: Drainage Is Not Optional in Most Soils

The IRC addresses window well drainage directly. Its window well section says wells need to be designed for proper drainage by connecting to the building's foundation drainage system, or by an approved alternative method. There is an exception where the foundation sits on well-drained soil or a sand-gravel mixture. You can read the text in the [IRC window well section, R310.2.3.2, as reproduced by UpCodes](https://up.codes/viewer/nebraska/irc-2018/chapter/3/building-planning#R310.2.3).

Clay is not well-drained soil. If your home sits on clay, plan on the drain connection being part of the job, and plan on the stone being part of that drain. For the rest of the well rules (size, projection, ladders), see [window well size requirements in Nebraska](/blog/window-well-size-requirements-nebraska/) and the [IRC egress window code requirements](/blog/irc-egress-window-code-requirements/).

## What Size and Type of Stone to Use

Use clean, washed, angular or rounded stone with no fines. "No fines" means no dust, sand, or silt mixed in. Those fine particles wash down into the gaps and turn the stone into something close to concrete.

Manufacturers give you a range, not one number. The Marflex install sheet for its Stonewell egress well says to use [pea gravel or 3/4 inch or smaller free-draining rock around the well](https://mar-flex.com/wp-content/uploads/Stonewells-Install.pdf). In practice, that means:

- **Washed crushed stone, roughly 1/2 to 3/4 inch.** This is the common pick. The angular edges lock together and stay put, and the gaps stay open.
- **Washed pea gravel.** Rounded stone that is also free-draining. It is fine for backfill and for the bottom of the well, but it shifts underfoot, which matters if you ever need to climb out.
- **Whatever your well maker specifies.** If your well came with an install sheet, that sheet wins. Some manufacturers make the warranty depend on following it.

## What Not to Use

**Dirt or reused excavation soil.** In Lincoln that is clay. It holds water against the glass and frame, which is the opposite of what you want.

**Sand.** Sand drains better than clay, but it packs and can wash into the drain opening. It also does not leave the open gaps you get with clean stone.

**Crusher fines or "road base" mix.** These contain dust and compact hard. They are made to form a firm surface, not to pass water.

**Large rock, like river cobble.** It drains, but it is awkward to stand on, and it leaves gaps big enough for soil to slump into from above.

**Decorative mulch or bark.** It rots, floats, and holds moisture.

If you are not sure what a bag of stone contains, look at the label for words like "washed" or "clean." If it says "mix," "base," or "fines," it is not what you want here.

## How Deep Should the Gravel Be?

There is no single code number for stone depth. The depth depends on the well's size and what the maker says. Two published examples show the range.

EHS Sales, a window well supplier, says that for wells under 50 inches wide you [put 4 inches of drainage stone in the bottom of the well, and 8 to 10 inches below wells over 56 inches wide](https://ehssales.ca/recommended-strategies-window-well-install/). The same page recommends 12 inches of drainage stone behind the well so backfill is not settling against it.

The Marflex sheet shows a similar setup: a stone backfill of about 12 inches in its cross-section drawing, with a drain line from the foundation drainage coming up to the base of the well.

So a sensible way to think about it:

| Where | What to expect |
|---|---|
| Bottom of the well | Several inches of clean stone, more for a wide well, per the maker's sheet |
| Behind and around the outside of the well | A thicker stone backfill, often about a foot, so clay does not sit against the wall |
| Top of the backfill | Soil or topsoil to final grade, sloped away from the well |

Any depth you choose needs to leave the stone below the window. The finished stone should sit lower than the window's bottom edge, not up against the frame, or water and stone can end up at the sill. If the stone you want would reach the window, the hole has to be dug deeper to fit the drain and the stone together. That is one reason this is easier to plan before the well goes in than after.

## How the Gravel Ties Into the Drain and the Footing

The stone is only half of the system. The other half is where the water goes next.

In a typical setup, the foundation has a drain line at the footing, and a pipe extension rises from that line up to the bottom of the well. The Marflex sheet describes exactly that: tie into the perimeter drainage with [a capped pipe extension up from the drain line to the base of the well](https://mar-flex.com/wp-content/uploads/Stonewells-Install.pdf). Where no perimeter drain exists, it says to excavate below the wall to the top of the footing and fill with pea stone or clean free-draining rock, or else direct the water away to well-drained soil.

That is why the stone at the bottom of the well matters. It is the first layer of the path: well floor, stone, drain cover, pipe, footing drain.

Two cautions:

- **A drain in the well is not a drain system.** If the pipe goes nowhere, the stone just fills with water. The connection to the foundation drainage is the part that makes it work.
- **Our scope stops at the well.** We install egress windows and window wells and tie the well into working footing tile. We do not install new footing drains or waterproofing systems. If your house has no footing drain or yours has failed, a waterproofing contractor handles that side, and we will tell you what we find at the well.

If the well is on a house where the drain is already clogged or collapsed, the stone will not save it. See [window well drain clogged](/blog/window-well-drain-clogged/) for what to check.

## Why Lincoln Clay and Winter Make This Harder

Clay soil around Lincoln drains slowly. After a heavy spring storm, water that reaches the well bottom has few ways out except the drain. If the stone is silted in or the drain is blocked, the well fills like a bucket.

Winter adds freeze-thaw. Water that sits in or against the well freezes, expands, and then thaws. Open stone gives that water room to drain before it freezes. Packed soil does not.

The stone only works if water can reach it. Grade the soil so it slopes away from the well, and keep downspouts well clear. Marflex says to keep downspouts a minimum of 8 to 10 feet from the window well, or at least 10 feet from the foundation wall, and to direct them away with pipe. For the yard side of the problem, read [window well grading and drainage outside](/blog/window-well-grading-drainage-outside/).

## Maintenance: What Happens to the Stone Over Time

Window well gravel does not last forever without attention.

- **Leaves, seeds, and dirt wash in.** Over a few seasons a layer of silt can cover the stone. Scoop out debris each spring and fall.
- **Stone sinks or gets muddy.** If the top few inches have turned to mud, the stone beneath may be fine. Remove the dirty top layer and add clean stone.
- **Standing water after rain is the sign to act.** If water stays in the well for a day or more, the stone, the drain, or the grading outside needs a look. See [flooded window well](/blog/flooded-window-well/).

Do not top up the well with whatever is on hand. Match what is already there, and keep the finished level below the window.

## When to Redo the Well Bottom

Refreshing stone is a small job. Redoing the well bottom is worth it when:

- You are replacing the well anyway, since the old stone is exposed and the drain is easy to inspect.
- Water stands in the well after rain, and cleaning the debris does not fix it.
- The stone was dirt or mixed fill from the start.
- The well has been pulling away from the wall, or the walls are leaning.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much gravel do I need in a window well?

It depends on the well's width and the maker's instructions. One window well supplier recommends about 4 inches of drainage stone in the bottom of wells under 50 inches wide and 8 to 10 inches for wells over 56 inches wide. Check your well's install sheet, and keep the finished stone level below the window.

### What kind of rock should I use in a window well?

Use clean, washed stone with no fines, such as crushed stone around 1/2 to 3/4 inch, or washed pea gravel. One manufacturer's install sheet calls for pea gravel or 3/4 inch or smaller free-draining rock. Avoid soil, sand, crusher fines, and base mixes, since they pack down and block drainage.

### Can I use pea gravel in a window well?

Yes, washed pea gravel drains well and is a common choice. It is rounded, so it shifts when you step on it, and crushed stone locks together better. Either one needs to be clean, with no sand or dust mixed in.

### Does the gravel replace a window well drain?

No. The stone helps water reach the drain, but the code expects window wells to drain to the foundation drainage system or an approved alternative unless the soil is well-drained. In Lincoln clay, plan on a real drain connection.

### Why does my window well fill with water even with gravel in it?

Usually the stone is silted in, the drain is clogged or was never connected, or the ground outside slopes toward the well. Clean the top layer first, then check the drain and the grading. If it keeps happening, have the well and drain looked at.
