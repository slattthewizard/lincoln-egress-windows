The question usually arrives in one of two forms. Either "will my insurance deny a claim because my basement bedroom doesn't have an egress window," or "is my insurance going to cover the water that came in through my window well."

Both have answers, and both are more specific than the internet's version. The honest short answer on the first: a standard homeowners policy generally doesn't void your fire coverage because a room isn't code-compliant. The exposures are real, but they're in different places than people expect, and the sharpest one has nothing to do with your dwelling coverage.

## The Fire Claim Question

Standard homeowners policies insure against covered perils. They don't usually contain a clause voiding coverage because part of your house doesn't meet current building code. Houses across Lincoln contain all kinds of legacy conditions that wouldn't pass a permit today, and insurers know that.

So if a fire damages your house and you have a basement bedroom that lacks a compliant escape opening, the dwelling claim generally proceeds on the damage.

Two caveats worth raising with your agent:

**Material misrepresentation.** If your application or policy describes the home differently than it is, that's a different issue than code compliance. The common versions: a bedroom count that doesn't match reality, finished square footage that was never disclosed, or occupancy described as owner-occupied when part of it is rented. Insurers price on what you told them.

**Ordinance or law coverage.** After a significant loss, rebuilding has to comply with current code, not the code that applied when the house was built. That can mean an egress window is now required where none existed, along with other upgrades. Basic policies often limit or exclude the extra cost of code-required upgrades. An ordinance or law endorsement covers that gap, and it's worth asking whether you have one and at what limit. This is the mechanism by which a code issue actually reaches your claim.

## The Sharp Edge: Liability

This is the exposure people don't think about, and it's much more serious than the property question.

If someone is injured or killed in a fire in a basement bedroom that had no legal means of escape, the claim that follows is a liability claim, and the question becomes whether the homeowner knew, or should have known, that the room was unsafe to sleep in.

That question gets more pointed the further you are from "my own family":

- **Your own household.** Still a tragedy, but the negligence framing is less pointed.
- **Guests.** More exposure.
- **A tenant.** Substantially more, and the landlord's duty is a real legal standard rather than an abstraction. [Rental property egress window inspection](/blog/rental-property-egress-window-inspection/) covers the obligations.
- **A tenant in a space you rented while telling your insurer the house was owner-occupied.** Now you have the liability exposure and a coverage argument at the same moment.

This is the argument that most reliably persuades people to do the work. The property loss is insurable. A death is not something a policy makes right.

## Renting the Basement

If any part of the house is rented, your insurer needs to know, and a standard homeowners policy is generally not the right product for a rental arrangement.

Renting a basement room or apartment without disclosing it creates two problems at once. The coverage may not respond as you expect for the rental portion, and the liability exposure to a tenant is exactly the situation the previous section describes.

If you're converting basement space to rent, the sequence is: bring the space into compliance, permit it, then tell your insurer what the house is now. ## Water Through the Window Well

Completely different topic, equally common question, and the answer disappoints people.

Water that enters through a window well after heavy rain or snowmelt is typically treated as surface water or seepage, and standard homeowners policies commonly exclude both. The fact that it came through a window rather than up through the floor doesn't usually change the analysis.

What to ask your agent about:

- **Water backup and sump overflow endorsements.** These address sump pump failure and drain backup, which is a different peril from surface water but frequently relevant in the same basement.
- **What your policy says about seepage and surface water** specifically.
- Whether a **separate flood policy** is appropriate for your property.

## Unpermitted Work

Unpermitted work doesn't automatically void anything, but it complicates every conversation it touches.

An adjuster examining damage in a finished basement may ask about permits. A code-required upgrade after a loss interacts with your ordinance or law coverage. A buyer's inspection flags it and the issue follows the house, as [selling a house with a non-compliant basement bedroom](/blog/selling-house-non-compliant-basement-bedroom/) covers.

Once the work is done, a short call to your agent is worth making:

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Will my insurance deny a claim because my basement bedroom has no egress window?

Generally not on the property damage side, since standard policies don't void coverage for code non-compliance. The real exposures are liability if someone is hurt, coverage problems if the space is rented without disclosure, and the cost of rebuilding to current code after a loss if you don't carry ordinance or law coverage.

### Does homeowners insurance cover window well flooding?

Usually not. Water entering through a window well is typically treated as surface water or seepage, both commonly excluded. Water backup and sump overflow endorsements address a related but different peril. Ask your agent about your specific policy, and treat the drainage as something to fix rather than insure.

### Do I need to tell my insurance company I added an egress window?

Yes, it's worth a call. Finished square footage, bedroom count, and replacement cost may all change, and a permitted code-compliant improvement is generally a positive. It's also a good moment to ask about ordinance or law coverage and water backup endorsements.

### Can I be liable if someone is hurt in a basement bedroom without egress?

It's the most serious exposure in this whole subject. A liability claim turns on whether you knew or should have known the room was unsafe to sleep in, and that question is sharper when the occupant is a guest or a tenant rather than a member of your own household.

### Does unpermitted basement work affect my insurance?

It doesn't automatically void coverage, but it complicates claims, interacts with code-upgrade coverage after a loss, and follows the house at sale. Given that the Lincoln permit runs **$75 to $200** on a project costing thousands, there's very little upside to skipping it.
