The name matters, because the name tells you how the window opens, and how it opens decides whether anyone could ever use it to get out of the house.

## Why the Type Matters More Than the Size

Two windows can have the same frame size and behave nothing alike. A casement swings out on a hinge and opens nearly the whole frame. A slider only opens about half of it, because one sash stays put while the other slides past. The opening that counts for escape is the clear space left when the sash is fully open, not the size of the frame you see from the inside.

Code for an escape opening is the same no matter the type: 5.7 square feet of net clear opening (5.0 square feet for a grade-floor opening), at least 24 inches of clear height, at least 20 inches of clear width, and a sill no higher than 44 inches above the finished floor. Those numbers come from the 2018 IRC, which Nebraska and Lincoln use. Confirm the details with Lincoln Building and Safety for your address.

The type is what makes those numbers easy or hard to hit.

## Hopper Windows

A hopper hinges at the bottom and tilts inward from the top. Picture a small window you pull toward you at the top edge. You'll find them all over older Lincoln basements, usually set high on the wall near the ceiling, often in a wood or steel frame.

**Why people like them:** they're cheap, they vent a basement without letting rain straight in, and they fit short, wide openings between the sill plate and the top of the foundation.

**Why they don't work for escape:** the sash tilts into the room instead of clearing the opening. Even if the frame is big, the swung-in sash blocks the way and the gap is narrow. They're also usually small and high. A hopper is almost never an egress window. We go deeper on that in [basement hopper window vs egress window](/blog/basement-hopper-window-vs-egress/).

## Sliding Windows

A slider has two sashes in one frame. One slides sideways past the other, or both slide. They're common in basements because they fit wide, short openings and have no swing to clear.

**Good for:** ventilation, easy cleaning, and spaces where there's no room for a sash to swing.

**The catch for escape:** only about half of the frame opens. If the frame is 48 inches wide, you might get around 22 or 23 inches of clear width once a sash slides over. That can pass the 20 inch minimum, but the area math punishes it. Sliders need to be large to reach 5.7 square feet. A slider can be an egress window, but the frame needs to be bigger than you'd guess. Our [egress window size chart](/blog/egress-window-size-chart/) shows how the opening works out for each style.

## Casement Windows

A casement hinges on one side and swings outward like a door, usually opened with a crank. The whole sash clears the frame, so most of the frame becomes open space.

**Good for:** egress. Of all the types here, casements give the most clear opening for the frame size. That's why you see them in so many new egress windows. A smaller frame can still reach the required area.

**Watch for:** the crank has to open the sash far enough. Some casements stop short of a full 90 degrees, and the hardware, the sash edge and the sill can all eat into the clear opening. The window well also has to be wide enough that the sash can swing without hitting the well wall. In a tight well, a casement can be blocked by its own hinge side.

For an overview of how casements, sliders and inswing styles compare for escape, read [casement, slider and inswing egress windows](/blog/casement-slider-inswing-egress-windows/).

## Single Hung and Double Hung Windows

A hung window moves up and down. In a single hung, only the lower sash opens. In a double hung, both can move.

**Good for:** a familiar look and easy ventilation, especially in a finished basement where you want the window to match the floors above.

**The catch for escape:** like a slider, a hung window opens only about half the frame height, since the sash that lifts overlaps the one that stays. To reach 24 inches of clear height, the frame has to be roughly twice that tall. That can mean a very large opening cut into the foundation. Hung windows can work as egress, but they're rarely the most practical choice below grade.

## Awning Windows

An awning window hinges at the top and swings outward at the bottom, like a small canopy. It stays dry in light rain.

**Good for:** ventilation in wet weather.

**Escape:** awning sashes are usually short and wide, and the sash angles out over the opening instead of leaving a clear walkway. They're uncommon in basements, and rarely suited to escape.

## Fixed Windows

A fixed window doesn't open at all. It's glass in a frame, set to let in light.

**Good for:** daylight, and sometimes paired next to a window that does open.

**Escape:** a fixed pane has no net clear opening. It can't be an egress window. If your basement room only has a fixed window, it doesn't have a code-compliant escape route, and a bedroom there would need a new opening.

## Glass Block Windows

Glass block windows are built from glass bricks set in mortar and a frame. They're common in older basements, partly for light and privacy and partly because they resist break-ins better than a standard pane.

**Good for:** light, privacy, and a sturdy look. Many have a small vent insert you can open, but not much more.

**Escape:** glass block is fixed. Even with a vent, you can't climb through. ## What Counts as an Egress Window

Egress isn't a window type. It's a result. Any style that opens wide enough, with a clear opening that meets 5.7 square feet, 24 inches of height and 20 inches of width, with a sill no higher than 44 inches, can serve as an escape opening. In practice:

- **Casements:** the usual egress choice because they open the most.
- **Sliders and hung windows:** can qualify, but the frame must be much larger.
- **Hoppers, awnings, fixed and glass block:** generally don't qualify.

Below grade, a window well also comes into it. The well needs at least 9 square feet of horizontal area, with at least 36 inches of projection and width. Nobody can climb out of a well that's too small to stand in, and wells deeper than 44 inches need a permanent ladder or steps.

## What Fits an Older Lincoln Basement

Older Lincoln houses often have small basement windows set high in a poured or block foundation. They were built for light and air, not for people. Many are hoppers or small sliders, a few are glass block, and some have been painted shut for decades.

Here's how that usually breaks down for an owner:

1. **You only want a fresher, tighter, better-looking window.** A basement window swap in the same opening keeps the work small. That's typically $400 to $1,200 per window.
2. **You want the basement to be a legal bedroom.** The opening almost always has to be made bigger, which means a cut-in, usually $3,500 to $7,000 for the cut-in and window, or $5,500 to $9,500 for a full project with a well and finishing.
3. **You already have an egress window that's worn out.** Replacing it in an existing opening typically runs $1,200 to $3,000 and is often a half-day job. See [egress window replacement](/egress-window-replacement-lincoln/).

Altering an existing basement doesn't require a new escape opening unless you're creating a new sleeping room (R310.6). So swapping a worn hopper for a better one is a different project from turning the same room into a bedroom. Lincoln's clay soil and freeze-thaw cycles also take a toll on old frames, so rot or moisture around the frame is worth checking while you're looking.

## How to Tell Which Type You Have

Quick checks you can do with a flashlight:

- **Open it.** Does the top tilt in? Hopper. Does a sash slide sideways? Slider. Does it crank outward on a hinge? Casement. Does it lift up? Hung.
- **Look at the glass.** Bricks in mortar? Glass block.
- **Try to open it.** If it doesn't move, it's either fixed or stuck. Paint and corrosion on old windows are common.
- **Measure the clear opening.** Open the sash all the way and measure open space only, not the frame.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What are the most common types of basement windows?

Hoppers, sliders, casements, glass block and fixed windows. Single and double hung and awning styles show up too, but less often below grade. Older Lincoln homes tend to have small hoppers, sliders or glass block.

### Can a hopper window be an egress window?

Almost never. The sash tilts into the room and the opening is small and high. If you need an escape opening, you'd replace it with a larger window and a well, usually through a cut-in.

### Which basement window type is best for egress?

A casement is usually the easiest, because it opens nearly the full frame. Sliders and hung windows can qualify, but they need a much larger frame to reach the same clear opening.

### Can glass block be turned into an opening that works for escape?

Glass block is fixed, so it can't be used as is. A glass block opening can be swapped for a window that opens, and if the opening needs to be larger for egress, it's cut larger and a well is added.

### Do I need a permit to change a basement window?

It depends on the work. Enlarging an opening or adding a well normally needs one, and a Lincoln building permit is typically $75 to $200. Confirm with Lincoln Building and Safety for your address. We pull the permit for our own work.
